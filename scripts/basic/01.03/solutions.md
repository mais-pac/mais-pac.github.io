## Thermodynamics: worked solutions

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Read the state.</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p1)

Convert: $P=10^5$ Pa and $V=0.025$ m³. $T=PV/(nR)=2500/8.31446=\mathbf{300.7\ K}$. One mole contains **$6.02214076\times10^{23}$ molecules**.

**Check.** Litres and kilopascals must be converted before using SI $R$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Isothermal expansion.</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p2)

For an ideal gas at fixed $T$, $\Delta U=0$. $W=nRT\ln2=8.31446\times300\times0.693147=\mathbf{1729\ J}$. Therefore $Q=W=\mathbf{1729\ J}$.

**Check.** The gas does positive work and must absorb heat to keep its temperature fixed.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Constant-pressure heating.</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p3)

$W=nR\Delta T=\mathbf{831.4\ J}$. $\Delta U=nC_V\Delta T=1.5R(100)=\mathbf{1247.2\ J}$. $Q=\Delta U+W=nC_P\Delta T=\mathbf{2078.6\ J}$.

**Check.** More heat is needed than at fixed volume because the gas expands.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Expansion without heat.</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p4)

$T_2=T_1(V_1/V_2)^{\gamma-1}=300/2^{2/3}=\mathbf{189.0\ K}$. With $Q=0$, $W=-\Delta U=nC_V(T_1-T_2)=1.5R(111.0)=\mathbf{1384\ J}$.

**Check.** Positive work comes from internal energy, so the gas cools. The adiabatic relation used here requires reversibility.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — A rigid vessel.</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p5)

At fixed $V$ and $n$, $P\propto T$, so $T_2=\mathbf{600\ K}$. No boundary work is done. $Q=\Delta U=1.5R(300)=\mathbf{3741\ J}$.

**Check.** Pressure can rise without expansion when heat is added.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — A rectangular engine.</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p6)

**Idea.** Net work is the rectangle’s area: $(300-100)\,\mathrm{kPa}\times(30-10)\,\mathrm L=\mathbf{4000\ J}$. For monatomic gas, constant-pressure legs have $Q=\frac52P\Delta V$ and constant-volume legs $Q=\frac32V\Delta P$.

The four heats are $+15000$, $-9000$, $-5000$, $+3000$ J. Thus $Q_{\rm in}=18000$ J and $\eta=4000/18000=\mathbf{22.2\%}$.

**Check.** The heats sum to 4000 J, matching net work. Do not divide by 4000 J: that would incorrectly give 100% efficiency.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — A bare planet.</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p7)

$T=[(1-A)F/(4\sigma)]^{1/4}$. For $A=0.3$, **254.6 K**; for $A=0.5$, **234.0 K**. Emitting over only half the area raises the uniform dayside temperature by $2^{1/4}$, giving **302.8 K** for $A=0.3$.

**Check.** Higher albedo cools; a smaller emitting area warms. These are effective temperatures, not weather forecasts.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — One ideal greenhouse layer.</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p8)

Let the surface emit $S=\sigma T_s^4$. The layer absorbs $S$ and emits $S/2$ upward and $S/2$ downward. Top-of-atmosphere balance gives $S/2=\sigma T_{\rm eff}^4$, hence $T_s=2^{1/4}T_{\rm eff}$. For 254.6 K, **$T_s=302.8$ K**.

**Check.** The surface receives stellar energy plus downward atmospheric radiation. Real atmospheres have spectral windows, convection, clouds and vertical structure; one opaque layer is not a climate model.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Mixing water.</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p9)

Equal masses and equal specific heat make the heat lost by the hot water equal the heat gained by the cold water. The final temperature is the average, **50°C**.

**Check.** The result lies between the initial temperatures. Unequal masses would require a mass-weighted average.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Heat through a wall.</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p10)

$\dot Q=kA\Delta T/\ell=0.20(0.010)(100)/0.05=\mathbf{4.0\ W}$. This is steady conduction through the stated slab.

**Check.** A thicker slab reduces the power; a larger area or temperature difference increases it.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s11" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 11 — An atmospheric ruler.</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p11)

$H=k_BT/(\mu m_u g)=1.380649\times10^{-23}(250)/(28.97\times1.660539\times10^{-27}\times9.81)=\mathbf{7.31\ km}$. At $z=H$, $P/P_0=e^{-1}=\mathbf{0.368}$.

**Check.** Hotter gas has a larger scale height; heavier molecules or stronger gravity make the atmosphere more compressed.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s12" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 12 — Could contraction power the Sun?</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p12)

$GM_\odot^2/(R_\odot L_\odot)=9.91\times10^{14}$ s $=\mathbf{3.14\times10^7\ yr}$. A structure-dependent coefficient changes this by a factor of order unity. Nuclear fusion supplies a much larger reservoir, allowing a main-sequence lifetime of billions of years.

**Check.** The estimate is a reservoir divided by a rate. It is relevant to contraction phases, not the present solar energy source.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s13" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 13 — A photon random walk.</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p13)

With $\ell=0.01$ m, $N\sim(7\times10^{10})^2=4.9\times10^{21}$. Time $t=N\ell/c=L^2/(\ell c)=\mathbf{1.63\times10^{11}\ s}\approx5.2\times10^3$ yr.

**Check.** This is far longer than straight-line crossing. Real opacity, density and energy exchange vary with depth, and photons are absorbed and re-emitted; the constant-mean-free-path estimate is deliberately crude.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s14" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 14 — Is the atmosphere stable?</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p14)

The dry adiabatic lapse rate is $g/c_p=0.00977$ K/m $=9.77$ K/km. The parcel becomes **290.23 K**; the environment becomes **293.5 K**. At the same pressure, the colder parcel is denser and tends to sink: **stable against dry convection**.

**Check.** Compare parcel and environment at the same height. Moisture and condensation can change the parcel’s lapse rate.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s15" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 15 — A blackbody dust grain.</span> [Return →](01.03.html#p15)

**Idea.** Equate absorbed stellar power to emitted thermal power. The stellar flux at $r$ is $\sigma T_*^4(R_*/r)^2$. Thus $\pi s^2\sigma T_*^4(R_*/r)^2=4\pi s^2\sigma T_d^4$, giving $T_d=T_*\sqrt{R_*/(2r)}$.

At 1 AU, **$T_d=278.3$ K**. Since $T_d\propto r^{-1/2}$, the 150 K distance is $(278.3/150)^2=\mathbf{3.44\ AU}$.

**Check.** Grain size cancels only for ideal blackbody absorption and emission. Small real grains can be hotter because their emissivity depends on wavelength.
</div>
