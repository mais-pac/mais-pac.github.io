## Nuclear Physics: worked solutions

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Read and transform a nuclide.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p1)

$Z=\mathbf{92}$ and $N=238-92=\mathbf{146}$. Alpha decay gives **$^{234}_{90}\mathrm{Th}$**. Beta-minus decay of the original gives **$^{238}_{91}\mathrm{Pa}$** plus an electron and antineutrino. Gamma emission changes the nucleus’s energy state, not $A$ or $Z$.

**Check.** Charge and nucleon number balance in each transformation.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Weigh the binding.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p2)

Using two hydrogen atoms cancels the two electrons in neutral helium: $\Delta m=2m_H+2m_n-m_{He}=\mathbf{0.03037664u}$. Multiplying by 931.494 MeV/u gives **$B=28.296$ MeV**, or **7.074 MeV per nucleon**.

**Check.** Binding energy is positive because the bound atom is lighter than its separated constituents.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Four hydrogens to helium.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p3)

The atomic-mass difference is $4m_H-m_{He}=0.02869687u$, giving **$Q=26.731$ MeV**. Efficiency is $\Delta m/(4m_H)=\mathbf{0.00712}$, about 0.71%. The full weak process emits two positrons (which annihilate with electrons) and two neutrinos; atomic masses account for the electron bookkeeping. Neutrinos carry away part of the energy, so the photon/thermal share is smaller.

**Check.** Do not add positron masses again when the atomic-mass convention already balances the electrons.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — A fission fuel bill.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p4)

One kilogram contains $1000/235$ mol, or $2.563\times10^{24}$ nuclei. Each fission gives $200\,\mathrm{MeV}=3.204\times10^{-11}$ J. Thus energy per kilogram is **$8.21\times10^{13}$ J/kg**. A 1 GW thermal reactor uses $8.64\times10^{13}$ J/day, corresponding to **1.05 kg fissioned per day**.

**Check.** This is thermal fuel consumption, not electrical output; real fuel loading and burnup differ.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Three half-lives.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p5)

24 days is three half-lives. Surviving fraction $=2^{-3}=\mathbf{1/8=12.5\%}$. Activity follows the same factor: **10 MBq**.

**Check.** Activity is proportional to the number remaining.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — A radiocarbon age.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p6)

$t=-T_{1/2}\ln(0.72)/\ln2=5730(0.4741)=\mathbf{2716\ yr}$ under the stated closed-system assumption.

**Check.** Less than one half-life has elapsed because more than half remains. Real radiocarbon calibration accounts for atmospheric variation and contamination.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — From atoms to becquerels.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p7)

$\lambda=\ln2/(5.27\times3.15576\times10^7)=4.168\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Initial activity $A_0=\lambda N=\mathbf{4.17\times10^6\ Bq}=4.17$ MBq. After two half-lives, activity is one quarter: **1.04 MBq**.

**Check.** Convert the half-life to seconds before reporting becquerels.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — A daughter grows and decays.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p8)

**Idea.** Daughter number peaks when production equals daughter decay: $\lambda_pN_p=\lambda_dN_d$. With half-lives 10 and 20 h, $\lambda_p=2\lambda_d$. The two-member solution peaks at $t=\ln(\lambda_p/\lambda_d)/(\lambda_p-\lambda_d)=\mathbf{20\ h}$. Then $N_p=N_0/4$ and $N_d=\mathbf{N_0/2}$. The activities are equal at that instant.

**Check.** The daughter is more numerous but decays more slowly per nucleus. This is not secular equilibrium because the parent is shorter-lived.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — A reaction threshold.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p9)

Initial atomic mass exceeds final by $-0.00127953u$, so **$Q=-1.192$ MeV**. The reaction is endothermic. The stationary-target threshold is approximately $1.192(1+4/14)=\mathbf{1.53\ MeV}$.

**Check.** The threshold exceeds $|Q|$ because momentum conservation leaves kinetic energy in the final centre of mass. Atomic electrons balance: 7+2 on the left and 8+1 on the right.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Count solar neutrinos.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p10)

$26.2\,\mathrm{MeV}=4.198\times10^{-12}$ J. Helium production rate $L_\odot/E=\mathbf{9.12\times10^{37}\ s^{-1}}$. Two neutrinos per reaction give $1.82\times10^{38}$ s⁻¹. At 1 AU, flux $=2\dot N/(4\pi d^2)=\mathbf{6.49\times10^{14}\ m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$, or $6.49\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$.

**Check.** This is an all-flavour production estimate; oscillations change the electron-neutrino fraction detected at Earth.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s11" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 11 — Fusion energy sharing.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p11)

The mass difference is 0.01888289u, giving **$Q=17.589$ MeV**. With negligible initial momentum, the two products have equal and opposite momenta. Nonrelativistically their kinetic energies divide inversely with mass: neutron gets about $4/5$ and alpha $1/5$. Thus **$K_n\simeq14.1$ MeV** and **$K_\alpha\simeq3.5$ MeV**.

**Check.** The lighter neutron carries most of the kinetic energy. The energies sum to $Q$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s12" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 12 — Why cooling continues.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p12)

Fission products are radioactive and continue decaying after the neutron chain reaction stops. Their decay energy appears as **decay heat**, initially significant and then decreasing. Cooling must continue even when the reactor is shut down.

**Check.** Stopping fission does not stop all nuclear energy release.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s13" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 13 — Parent and daughter.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p13)

A 1:3 ratio means one quarter of the original parent remains. That is two half-lives, so the age is **24 years**. Initial daughter would make the sample appear older if not corrected; loss or gain of either isotope would also break the closed-system clock.

**Check.** Parent plus daughter is conserved only under the stated assumptions.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s14" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 14 — Secular equilibrium.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p14)

In secular equilibrium, **$A_d\simeq A_p$**. Since $A=\lambda N$, $N_d/N_p=\lambda_p/\lambda_d=T_d/T_p=10/10^9=\mathbf{10^{-8}}$. The daughter has far fewer nuclei but each decays much faster, so the total rates match.

**Check.** Equilibrium takes several daughter half-lives to establish and assumes the parent changes negligibly on that timescale.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s15" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 15 — The Sun’s nuclear clock.</span> [Return →](01.05.html#p15)

Available energy is $0.1\times0.007M_\odot c^2$. Dividing by $L_\odot$ gives $3.27\times10^{17}$ s $=\mathbf{1.04\times10^{10}\ yr}$. The gravitational Kelvin–Helmholtz scale is only about $3\times10^7$ yr.

**Check.** Nuclear burning provides a reservoir hundreds of times larger. The estimate assumes constant present luminosity and a fixed burnable fraction; a stellar evolution model refines it.
</div>
