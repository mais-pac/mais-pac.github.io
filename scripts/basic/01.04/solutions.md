## Spectroscopy and Atoms: worked solutions

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — A small redshift.</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p1)

$\beta=100/299792.458=3.33564\times10^{-4}$. The relativistic factor gives **656.49895 nm**, a shift of 0.21895 nm. The low-speed formula gives 656.49891 nm, differing by only $3.7\times10^{-5}$ nm.

**Check.** Recession shifts the line redward. At this speed the approximation is excellent.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Two stars in one spectrum.</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p2)

$v\simeq c\Delta\lambda/\lambda_0$: **−119.9 km/s** and **+179.9 km/s**. Their mean, the systemic velocity, is **+30.0 km/s**. Relative to the system both semiamplitudes are 150 km/s, so $m_1/m_2=K_2/K_1=1$.

**Check.** The momenta about the barycentre are opposite. At quadrature in the stated circular edge-on orbit, the observed speeds are the orbital semiamplitudes.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Weighing a rotating galaxy.</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p3)

**Idea.** Remove systemic motion, then deproject. $v_{\rm rot}=200/\sin30^\circ=\mathbf{400\ km\,s^{-1}}$. For circular motion, $M=v^2R/G$. With $R=5$ kpc, **$M=1.86\times10^{11}M_\odot$**.

**Check.** The mass is enclosed mass under the spherical-equivalent estimate; a disk geometry changes the coefficient. The two observed sides would lie at about 4800 and 5200 km/s.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Thermal width.</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p4)

$\sigma_v=\sqrt{k_BT/m_H}=\mathbf{9.08\ km\,s^{-1}}$. FWHM $=2.35482\sigma_v=\mathbf{21.39\ km\,s^{-1}}$. In wavelength, $\Delta\lambda=656.28(21.39/299792.458)=\mathbf{0.04682\ nm}$.

**Check.** This is a width, not a bulk shift. Heavier atoms at the same temperature have smaller thermal speeds.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Can the spectrograph separate them?</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p5)

$\Delta\lambda=\lambda/R=\mathbf{0.025\ nm}$; equivalent velocity width $c/R=\mathbf{15.0\ km\,s^{-1}}$. A 0.040 nm separation is larger than the instrumental FWHM, so the doublet is **resolvable in principle**. Real contrast and line widths still matter.

**Check.** Higher resolving power means a smaller instrumental width.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — A finite lifetime.</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p6)

With $\Delta E\sim\hbar/(2\tau)$ and $\Delta E=h\Delta\nu$, the half-width at half-maximum is $\Delta\nu_{\rm HWHM}\sim1/(4\pi\tau)=\mathbf{7.96\ MHz}$; the corresponding full width is $\mathbf{15.9\ MHz}$. Using the half-width, $\Delta\lambda\simeq\lambda^2\Delta\nu/c=\mathbf{6.64\times10^{-6}\ nm}$.

**Check.** Always state whether a linewidth is HWHM or FWHM. A precise Lorentzian width depends on whether the quoted lifetime refers to amplitude or population decay; the key physics is that shorter lifetime means broader line.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — A magnetic fingerprint.</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p7)

$\Delta\nu=eB/(4\pi m_e)=\mathbf{1.400\ GHz}$ for each shifted component. Near 500 nm, $\Delta\lambda=\lambda^2\Delta\nu/c=\mathbf{0.001167\ nm}$ (0.01167 Å). The two outer components are separated by **2.800 GHz**, or **0.002334 nm**.

**Check.** The unshifted component remains at the centre in the normal triplet. Real atomic structure can produce anomalous splitting.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Two polarizers.</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p8)

For already polarized light, $\cos^260^\circ=\mathbf{0.25}$. For unpolarized light, the first polarizer leaves 0.5 and the second leaves another 0.25: **0.125** of the original intensity.

**Check.** The first ideal polarizer both selects a direction and removes half the unpolarized power.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Brewster’s angle.</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p9)

$\theta_B=\tan^{-1}(1.5/1)=\mathbf{56.3^\circ}$ from the normal. In the ideal dielectric case the reflected beam is fully linearly polarized perpendicular to the plane of incidence.

**Check.** The reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular at Brewster’s angle. Absorption, roughness and multiple surfaces reduce the ideal purity.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — More atoms, not always twice the depth.</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p10)

Optically thin: equivalent width $W\propto N$. Saturated: the centre is already dark, so $W$ grows slowly as the line widens. Damping wings: strong Lorentzian wings eventually give approximately $W\propto\sqrt N$ for fixed damping. Depth alone misses width and saturation, and also depends on instrumental resolution.

**Check.** Equivalent width is an area; two lines with the same depth can have very different strengths.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s11" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 11 — How transparent?</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p11)

$e^{-0.2}=\mathbf{0.819}$ and $e^{-2}=\mathbf{0.135}$. Half transmission requires $\tau=\ln2=\mathbf{0.693}$.

**Check.** Optical depth adds through successive screens; transmitted fractions multiply.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s12" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 12 — Populating an excited level.</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p12)

With equal weights, $n_2/n_1=e^{-2/(k_BT)}$. At 6000 K, $k_BT=0.5170$ eV and the ratio is **0.0209**. At 12,000 K, $k_BT=1.0341$ eV and the ratio is **0.1446**.

**Check.** Higher temperature populates the excited level more strongly, but it still need not contain most atoms.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s13" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 13 — Why blue scatters more.</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p13)

The ratio is $(800/400)^4=\mathbf{16}$. In ideal single Rayleigh scattering, polarization is strongest near **90°** scattering angle.

**Check.** Multiple scattering, large particles and reflected light reduce the simple polarization and colour rules.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s14" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 14 — Expansion parallax.</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p14)

**Idea.** Proper motion links transverse speed and distance: $v_t=4.74047\,\mu(''/\mathrm{yr})\,d(\mathrm{pc})$. The observed angular expansion rate is $\mu=0.10''$/yr. Then $d=100/(4.74047\times0.1)=\mathbf{211\ pc}$. The radius grows by **1 arcsecond**, doubling to 2 arcseconds.

**Check.** The calculation assumes spherical expansion so that the rim’s transverse speed equals the spectroscopic speed. A non-spherical shell breaks that equality. The distance follows from comparing angular and physical expansion rates, not from the angular size alone.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s15" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 15 — An unresolved line through a spectrograph.</span> [Return →](01.04.html#p15)

**Idea.** A Gaussian’s area is depth times $\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}$. Here $\sigma=0.10/2.35482=0.04247$ nm, so $W=0.4(0.04247)\sqrt{2\pi}=\mathbf{0.04258\ nm}$. Convolution with an equal Gaussian gives FWHM $\sqrt{0.10^2+0.10^2}=\mathbf{0.1414\ nm}$. Conservation of area makes the new depth $0.4/\sqrt2=\mathbf{0.2828}$.

**Check.** The instrument makes the line wider and shallower while preserving its equivalent width under the stated ideal conditions. This is why depth alone is resolution-dependent.
</div>
