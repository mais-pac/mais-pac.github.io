## 1. Charge, field and potential

Charge is quantised, conserved, and comes in two signs. The force between two point charges is Coulomb's law:

$$F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}, \qquad \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}} .$$

It is mathematically the same law as gravitation, except that charge can be negative and the force can be screened (in a conductor, or through a dielectric).

Rather than tracking forces, physicists use **fields**. The electric field is

$$\mathbf E = \frac{\mathbf F}{q},$$

and the potential is the potential energy per unit charge,

$$V = \frac{U}{q}, \qquad \mathbf E = -\nabla V .$$

The field is a vector and the potential is a scalar, so the potential is usually easier to add: $V = \sum kq_i/r_i$, while $\mathbf E$ must be added as vectors.

## 2. Gauss's law and the method of symmetry

The flux of the field through a closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by $\varepsilon_0$:

$$\oint \mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0} .$$

{{FIG:gauss}}

Gauss's law is always true and almost never useful *directly*; it becomes a calculation only when the symmetry makes $\mathbf E$ constant over the surface. The three classic results:

- Spherical shell of charge $Q$: $E = 0$ inside, $E = kQ/r^2$ outside — exactly as if all charge sat at the centre.
- Uniformly charged solid sphere: $E = kQr/R^3$ inside, growing linearly from the centre.
- Infinite plane with surface charge density $\sigma$: $E = \sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)$, uniform.

Conductors in equilibrium have $E=0$ inside, so all excess charge sits on the surface, and the surface is an equipotential. Sharp points have larger surface charge density and therefore stronger fields — the reason lightning rods work.

## 3. Capacitance and stored energy

A capacitor stores charge proportional to the applied voltage, $Q = CV$, and holds energy

$$U = \tfrac12 CV^2 = \frac{Q^2}{2C} .$$

For a parallel-plate capacitor of area $A$ and separation $d$,

$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}\quad(\text{multiplied by } \varepsilon_r \text{ with a dielectric}).$$

Capacitors in parallel add, $C = C_1 + C_2$; in series they combine reciprocally, $\frac1C = \frac1{C_1}+\frac1{C_2}$ — the opposite of resistors, because what adds is charge, not voltage drop.

{{FIG:energy-density}}

## 4. Current, resistance and Ohm's law

Current is charge per unit time, $I = dQ/dt$. For an ohmic conductor,

$$V = IR, \qquad R = \frac{\rho L}{A},$$

where $\rho$ is the resistivity. Power dissipated is

$$P = IV = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R} .$$

A non-ohmic element has a curved $I$–$V$ characteristic (a filament lamp, a diode); then $R = V/I$ still defines a resistance at each point, but it is not a constant.

## 5. Kirchhoff's rules and circuit analysis

Two laws, both conservation statements:

- **Junction rule:** $\sum I_{\text{in}} = \sum I_{\text{out}}$ — charge conservation.
- **Loop rule:** $\sum \Delta V = 0$ around any closed loop — energy conservation.

{{FIG:circuit}}

Working method: label a current direction in every branch (the sign is irrelevant as long as you are consistent), write one junction equation per independent node, then one loop equation per independent loop. A circuit with $n$ nodes and $b$ branches needs $b$ independent equations.

Two rewriting tools solve most problems without algebra:

- **Series/parallel reduction** when the network allows it.
- **The potential divider**: with no load, $V_{\text{out}} = V\dfrac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}$. With a load, replace $R_2$ by $R_2\parallel R_L$ — the ideal formula fails badly once the load draws comparable current.

{{LAB:divider}}

Real sources have internal resistance $r$, so $V_{\text{terminal}} = \varepsilon - Ir$; maximum power transfer to a load happens at $R_L = r$, where the efficiency is only 50%.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">The electrostatics checklist.</span>
Symmetry first (then Gauss) → potential rather than field when adding contributions → energy $U = \frac{Q^2}{2C}$ or $\frac12CV^2$ for capacitors → junction and loop rules for circuits → check limits (distance to zero, load to infinity, dielectric to vacuum).</div>

<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Three charges in a line.</span> [Return →](02.06.html#p1)

(a) $F = kq_1q_2/r^2 = (8.99\times10^{9})(2.0\times10^{-6})(3.0\times10^{-6})/(1.2)^2 = 0.037\ \mathrm{N}$, repulsive.

(b) Let the third charge sit at distance $x$ from $+2.0\ \mu\mathrm{C}$ toward $+3.0\ \mu\mathrm{C}$. For zero net field the field magnitudes must cancel:
$$\frac{2}{x^2} = \frac{3}{(1.2-x)^2} \Rightarrow \frac{1.2-x}{x} = \sqrt{1.5} = 1.225 \Rightarrow x = 0.539\ \mathrm{m}.$$
The other root lies outside the segment and corresponds to fields pointing the same way.

(c) $V = k\left(\dfrac{2}{0.539} + \dfrac{3}{0.661}\right)\times10^{-6} = 8.99\times10^{9}\times(3.71+4.54)\times10^{-6} = 7.4\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{V}$. **Check:** zero *field* does not mean zero *potential* — a distinction that carries marks.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Field of a charged sphere.</span> [Return →](02.06.html#p2)

(a) By Gauss, outside $E = kQ/r^2$. Inside, the enclosed charge is $Qr^3/R^3$, so $E = kQr/R^3$.

(b) Outside, $V = kQ/r$. Inside, integrate inward from the surface with the field $kQr/R^3$: $V(r) = \dfrac{kQ}{2R}\left(3 - \dfrac{r^2}{R^2}\right)$.

(c) $E$ drops to zero at the surface only if the charge is a shell; for a solid sphere $E$ is continuous but its derivative jumps, because the charge density is discontinuous there. $V$ is continuous always — otherwise the field (its gradient) would be infinite. **Check:** at $r=R$ both formulas give $kQ/R$ for $V$ and $kQ/R^2$ for $E$, as continuity requires.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Capacitors in series and parallel.</span> [Return →](02.06.html#p3)

(a) Parallel: $C = 11\ \mu\mathrm{F}$, $U = \tfrac12(11\times10^{-6})(144) = 7.9\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{J}$.

(b) Series: $\frac1C = \frac12+\frac13+\frac16 = 1$, so $C = 1.0\ \mu\mathrm{F}$ and $U = \tfrac12(10^{-6})(144) = 7.2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{J}$.

(c) At a fixed supply voltage, energy is $\tfrac12CV^2$, so it scales directly with the capacitance. In series the total voltage is shared and the equivalent capacitance is smaller than the smallest individual one. **Check:** $11$ times less energy for exactly $11$ times less capacitance — the ratio is consistent.

<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Charging a capacitor.</span> [Return →](02.06.html#p4)

(a) $\varepsilon = IR + q/C$ with $I = dq/dt$ gives $R\dot q + q/C = \varepsilon$, whose solution with $q(0)=0$ is $q = C\varepsilon(1-e^{-t/RC})$.

(b) The battery supplies $\int\varepsilon I\,dt = \varepsilon Q = C\varepsilon^2$. The capacitor stores $\tfrac12C\varepsilon^2$. The remaining half is dissipated in the resistor as heat, whatever the value of $R$ — a result that surprises everyone once.

(c) At $t = RC$, $q/Q = 1 - e^{-1} = 0.632$, so $63\%$. **Check:** as $R\to0$ the *power* diverges as $1/R$ while the *duration* shrinks as $R$, so the energy stays $\tfrac12C\varepsilon^2$; the limit is still consistent.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — A Wheatstone bridge.</span> [Return →](02.06.html#p5)

(a) Both arms divide $6.0\ \mathrm{V}$ in the ratio $1:2$, so the midpoints sit at $4.0\ \mathrm{V}$ and $4.0\ \mathrm{V}$: zero potential difference, so no current.

(b) Balanced when $\dfrac{R_1}{R_2} = \dfrac{R_3}{R_4}$.

(c) With $R_4 = 33\ \Omega$ the right midpoint becomes $\dfrac{33}{48}\times6 = 4.125\ \mathrm{V}$, above the left midpoint at $4.0\ \mathrm{V}$, so current flows from right to left through the galvanometer. **Check:** the reasoning uses only potential division, not the full three-loop solution — the physically correct shortcut.

<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Drift, mean free time and the electron gas.</span> [Return →](02.06.html#p6)

(a) $I = nAev_d \Rightarrow v_d = \dfrac{2.0}{(8.5\times10^{28})(10^{-6})(1.6\times10^{-19})} = 1.5\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(b) From $\rho = m_e/(ne^2\tau)$: $\tau = \dfrac{9.11\times10^{-31}}{(8.5\times10^{28})(1.6\times10^{-19})^2(1.7\times10^{-8})} = 2.5\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm{s}$.

(c) Thermal speeds are $\sqrt{2kT/m}\sim10^{5}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, nine orders of magnitude larger than the drift speed. The electrons do not travel slowly; they scatter constantly and drift *collectively* slowly, so a signal travels at nearly the speed of light while individual electrons creep. **Check:** the mean free path $v\tau\sim2.5\ \mathrm{nm}$ is a few tens of atomic spacings, as expected for a metal.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Two spheres connected by a wire.</span> [Return →](02.06.html#p7)

(a) Equal potentials: $kQ_1'/r_1 = kQ_2'/r_2$ with $Q_1'+Q_2' = Q_1+Q_2$, so
$$Q_1' = (Q_1+Q_2)\frac{r_1}{r_1+r_2},\qquad Q_2' = (Q_1+Q_2)\frac{r_2}{r_1+r_2}.$$

(b) $\sigma_1' = Q_1'/(4\pi r_1^2) \propto \dfrac{1}{r_1(r_1+r_2)}$, and similarly for 2, so $\sigma_1'/\sigma_2' = r_2/r_1$. The fields at the surfaces, $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$, follow the same ratio squared relative to the spheres' own charges.

(c) A sharp point is a small-radius conductor: it carries a high surface charge density and therefore a strong field, which ionises the air and starts a discharge. **Check:** as $r_1\to r_2$ the charges equalise, as they should for identical spheres.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Internal resistance and maximum power.</span> [Return →](02.06.html#p8)

(a) $I = 12/5.5 = 2.18\ \mathrm{A}$, $V_{\text{terminal}} = 12 - 2.18(0.50) = 10.9\ \mathrm{V}$.

(b) $P = \dfrac{\varepsilon^2R}{(R+r)^2}$ is maximal at $R = r = 0.50\ \Omega$, giving $P = \dfrac{144}{4(0.5)} = 72\ \mathrm{W}$.

(c) At $R=r$ the load and internal resistance each dissipate 72 W, so the efficiency is 50%. Real supplies are designed for low internal resistance and operation below the maximum-power point, because efficiency matters more than power. **Check:** at $R\to\infty$ and $R\to0$ the power delivered tends to zero, so the maximum in between is genuine.

<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Potentiometer.</span> [Return →](02.06.html#p9)

(a) The wire carries $2.0/12 = 0.167\ \mathrm{A}$, so the potential gradient is $2.0\ \mathrm{V/m}$. A balance length of $0.620\ \mathrm{m}$ corresponds to $\varepsilon_x = 1.24\ \mathrm{V}$.

(b) At balance, no current flows through the unknown cell, so its terminal voltage equals its emf; the balance length is proportional to $\varepsilon_x/\varepsilon_{\text{driver}}$ times the length. Only the *uniformity* of the wire enters, so a precise total resistance is unnecessary.

(c) A lower driver emf halves the potential gradient proportionally, so the balance length becomes $0.620\times(2.0/1.9) = 0.653\ \mathrm{m}$. **Check:** longer wire for the same unknown cell at a lower driver emf — exactly what a smaller gradient implies.

<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — A sliding contact and a nonlinear element.</span> [Return →](02.06.html#p10)

(a) While the diode conducts, the loop rule is $9.0 - 0.70 - IR(x) = 0$, so
$$I(x) = \frac{8.3}{6.0\left(1 + 3x/L\right)}\ \mathrm{A}.$$

(b) The diode is forward-biased as long as the open-circuit voltage across its position would exceed $0.70\ \mathrm{V}$. Here the source is $9.0\ \mathrm{V}$ and the only series element is $R(x)\ge 6.0\ \Omega$, so the diode never turns off for any finite $x$: the current merely falls from $1.38\ \mathrm{A}$ at $x=0$ to $0.35\ \mathrm{A}$ at $x=L$. The physically important statement is that a pure series resistance cannot make a diode stop conducting; you need a second voltage source or a change of bias direction.

(c) Kirchhoff's loop rule is energy conservation per unit charge and holds for any element; the nonlinearity only means the $V$–$I$ relation is a curve, so the loop equation must be solved *together with* that curve (graphically, or by a load-line construction). **Check:** the current is always positive and decreases monotonically with $x$, consistent with the increasing resistance.
