## 1. Three ways for heat to move

Thermodynamics says *how much* heat flows; heat transfer says *how fast*. There are exactly three mechanisms:

- **Conduction:** energy passes from molecule to molecule without bulk motion. Dominant in solids.
- **Convection:** a moving fluid carries energy with it. Dominant in liquids and gases, and the reason radiators are at floor level.
- **Radiation:** energy is carried by electromagnetic waves and needs no medium at all. The only mechanism that works through a vacuum.

{{FIG:conduction}}

## 2. Conduction and thermal resistance

For a slab of area $A$, thickness $d$ and thermal conductivity $k$, with faces held at $T_1$ and $T_2$, the steady heat current is

$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{kA(T_1-T_2)}{d} = \frac{\Delta T}{R_{\text{th}}}, \qquad R_{\text{th}} = \frac{d}{kA} .$$

This is Ohm's law with temperature in place of voltage: layers in series add their resistances, and the same heat current passes through every layer once the steady state is reached.

{{FIG:layers}}

Typical values: copper $400\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, glass $1$, water $0.6$, still air $0.026$. The tiny conductivity of air is why foam, wool and double glazing work — they trap air so that convection cannot short-circuit the conduction path.

## 3. Convection

A fluid heated from below becomes less dense, rises, cools, and sinks: a **convection current**. The rate depends on geometry and on whether the flow is laminar or turbulent, and it is not captured by a simple formula in the way conduction is. The physics is worth remembering qualitatively:

- Convection is much more efficient than conduction in a fluid, so a forced draught (a fan) can carry away heat far faster than still air.
- Heated rooms stratify, with warm air at the ceiling — the opposite of what a well-designed radiator position should give.
- In the Earth's mantle, convection drives plate tectonics; in the Sun's interior, it carries the energy that eventually leaves as sunlight.

## 4. Thermal radiation

Every body above absolute zero radiates. The power emitted from area $A$ at temperature $T$ is

$$P = \varepsilon\sigma AT^4, \qquad \sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}},$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the **emissivity** ($0\le\varepsilon\le1$; a perfect black body has $\varepsilon=1$).

{{FIG:radiation}}

A body both emits and absorbs, so its net loss to surroundings at $T_0$ is

$$P_{\text{net}} = \varepsilon\sigma A\left(T^4-T_0^4\right).$$

The $T^4$ makes radiation negligible at room temperature and dominant at high temperature — which is why a hot poker glows and a warm one does not. The wavelength at which the emission peaks obeys **Wien's law**:

$$\lambda_{\max}T = 2.90\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,K}.$$

{{FIG:blackbody}}

A body at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ peaks at $\lambda\approx9.7\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ — the infrared, which is why thermal cameras work. The Sun at $5800\ \mathrm{K}$ peaks at $500\ \mathrm{nm}$, in the visible, and that is not a coincidence: eyes evolved to use the most abundant light available.

## 5. Specific heat and latent heat

Heating a body of mass $m$ with specific heat capacity $c$ takes

$$Q = mc\Delta T .$$

But changing the *phase* of a substance takes energy with no change in temperature:

$$Q = mL, \qquad L_f = 3.34\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\ (\text{ice}), \quad L_v = 2.26\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\ (\text{water}).$$

{{FIG:phase}}

{{FIG:latent}}

The latent heat of vaporisation of water is enormous: boiling away a kilogram takes more than five times the energy needed to heat it from $0$ to $100^\circ\mathrm{C}$. That single number explains sweating (evaporation cools the skin), the severity of steam burns, and why a pot of water takes so long to boil dry.

For a mixture or a calorimetry problem, the method is always the same: energy lost by the hot bodies equals energy gained by the cold bodies, **plus** any latent heat, and the final state may be partly melted — in which case the temperature is pinned at the melting point and you must check the energy budget before assuming it.

## 6. Newton's law of cooling

For small temperature differences, the rate of heat loss is proportional to the excess temperature:

$$\frac{dT}{dt} = -k(T-T_0) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = T_0 + (T_{\text{start}}-T_0)e^{-kt}.$$

{{LAB:cooling}}

That is the same exponential as radioactive decay, and for the same reason: the loss rate is proportional to the quantity that remains. The constant $k$ lumps together conduction, convection and (weakly) radiation; when the object is much hotter than its surroundings the $T^4$ law dominates and the cooling is no longer exponential.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">The heat-transfer checklist.</span>
Which mechanism? (solid → conduction, fluid → convection, vacuum or high T → radiation) → for steady conduction, treat it as a resistance network → for radiation, use $P = \varepsilon\sigma AT^4$ and remember the net exchange needs a *difference* → for heating problems, separate the temperature changes ($mc\Delta T$) from the phase changes ($mL$) and check whether the energy budget actually reaches the next phase → for cooling, use the exponential only for a small excess temperature.</div>

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Heating water.</span> [Return →](02.16.html#p1)

(a) $Q = mc\Delta T = (1.5)(4200)(80) = 5.0\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J}$.

(b) $t = Q/P = 5.04\times10^{5}/2000 = 2.5\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{s}$ — about four minutes.

(c) The kettle itself heats up, some heat is lost to the room, the element is not perfectly efficient, and the water evaporates a little. In addition, the rated power is delivered to the element, not to the water, so the transfer efficiency is below $100\%$. **Check:** $5.0\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J}$ is about $0.14\ \mathrm{kWh}$, and a $2\ \mathrm{kW}$ kettle delivers that in $4$ minutes.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Melting ice.</span> [Return →](02.16.html#p2)

(a) Energy to melt the ice: $Q_1 = (0.50)(3.34\times10^{5}) = 1.67\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J}$. Energy released by the water cooling to $0^\circ\mathrm{C}$: $Q_2 = (2.0)(4200)(25) = 2.1\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J}$. Since $Q_2 > Q_1$, all the ice melts, and the surplus $(2.1 - 1.67)\times10^{5} = 4.3\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{J}$ warms $2.5\ \mathrm{kg}$ of water:
$$\Delta T = \frac{4.3\times10^{4}}{(2.5)(4200)} = 4.1\ \mathrm{K},$$
so the final temperature is about $4.1^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

(b) Confirmed above: $2.1\times10^{5} > 1.67\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J}$.

(c) With $0.30\ \mathrm{kg}$ the melting energy is $1.0\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J}$, still less than $2.1\times10^{5}$, so the final temperature is $\Delta T = (2.1-1.0)\times10^{5}/(2.3\times4200) = 11\ \mathrm{K}$ above zero, i.e. about $11^\circ\mathrm{C}$. **Check:** less ice gives a warmer mixture, as expected; the check on the assumption is always whether the available energy exceeds the latent heat.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Conduction through a window.</span> [Return →](02.16.html#p3)

(a) $A = 1.2\times1.5 = 1.8\ \mathrm{m^2}$, so
$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{kA\Delta T}{d} = \frac{(0.80)(1.8)(20)}{0.0040} = 7.2\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{W} = 7.2\ \mathrm{kW}.$$

(b) In a day, $E = 7200\times86\,400 = 6.2\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{J} = 173\ \mathrm{kWh}$.

(c) Setting $d/k$ equal for air and glass: $d_{\text{air}} = 0.026\times(0.0040/0.80) = 1.3\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m}$ — a tenth of a millimetre of still air is as good as $4\ \mathrm{mm}$ of glass. That is why double glazing uses a thick gap; in practice the air gap must be wide enough that convection does not short-circuit it. **Check:** the window loses about $7\ \mathrm{kW}$, which would be a large fraction of a domestic heating system — the reason glazing matters.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Radiation from a person.</span> [Return →](02.16.html#p4)

(a) $T = 306\ \mathrm{K}$:
$$P = \varepsilon\sigma AT^4 = (0.97)(5.67\times10^{-8})(1.8)(306)^4 = 8.8\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{W}.$$

(b) The room at $293\ \mathrm{K}$ returns
$$P_{\text{in}} = (0.97)(5.67\times10^{-8})(1.8)(293)^4 = 7.4\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{W},$$
so the net loss is about $1.4\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{W}$.

(c) The body generates roughly $100\ \mathrm{W}$ at rest, and evaporative loss and convection make up the rest. Cold walls lower $T_0$ in the fourth-power term, so the net radiative loss grows even if the air is warm — which is why a room with single glazing feels cold at the same air temperature. **Check:** the ratio $P/P_{\text{in}} = (306/293)^4 = 1.19$, and $0.19\times880 = 170\ \mathrm{W}$, consistent with the difference calculated directly.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — The Sun and the Earth.</span> [Return →](02.16.html#p5)

(a) $A = 4\pi R^2 = 4\pi(6.96\times10^{8})^2 = 6.09\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, so
$$P = \sigma AT^4 = (5.67\times10^{-8})(6.09\times10^{18})(5800)^4 = 3.9\times10^{26}\ \mathrm{W}.$$

(b) At $r = 1.5\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{m}$, the intensity is $S = P/(4\pi r^2) = 3.9\times10^{26}/(2.83\times10^{23}) = 1.4\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$.

(c) Energy balance for a black sphere absorbing over its cross-section $\pi R^2$ and radiating over its whole surface $4\pi R^2$:
$$\pi R^2S = 4\pi R^2\sigma T^4 \Rightarrow T = \left(\frac{S}{4\sigma}\right)^{1/4} = \left(\frac{1360}{4(5.67\times10^{-8})}\right)^{1/4} = 279\ \mathrm{K} = 6^\circ\mathrm{C}.$$
Adding the albedo $0.30$ lowers it to about $255\ \mathrm{K}$; the real mean is $288\ \mathrm{K}$ because the atmosphere absorbs and re-radiates infrared, the greenhouse effect. **Check:** the calculation reproduces the measured solar constant to about $3\%$, and the $33\ \mathrm{K}$ gap is the standard greenhouse figure.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Newton's law of cooling.</span> [Return →](02.16.html#p6)

(a) Using excess temperatures: $90-20 = 70$ and $70-20 = 50$, so $50 = 70e^{-300k}$ and $k = \dfrac{\ln(70/50)}{300} = 1.12\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

(b) After $15\ \mathrm{min}$ total: excess $= 70e^{-1.12\times10^{-3}\times900} = 70(0.365) = 26\ \mathrm{K}$, so $T = 46^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

(c) $30^\circ\mathrm{C}$ means an excess of $10\ \mathrm{K}$: $t = \dfrac{\ln7}{1.12\times10^{-3}} = 1.7\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{s} \approx 29\ \mathrm{min}$. The model fails at large excess because radiation ($T^4$) takes over and because evaporation is significant for hot water. **Check:** the model predicts an asymptote at $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$, never below, which is right but approached more slowly than reality allows.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Two layers of insulation.</span> [Return →](02.16.html#p7)

(a) Resistances per unit area: brick $0.10/0.84 = 0.119$, insulation $0.10/0.040 = 2.50\ \mathrm{m^2\,K\,W^{-1}}$; total $2.62$. Then
$$\text{flux} = \frac{20}{2.62} = 7.6\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}.$$

(b) The drop across the brick is $7.6\times0.119 = 0.91\ \mathrm{K}$, so the interface is at $19.1^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

(c) The insulation has twenty-one times the resistance of the brick, so it takes twenty-one times the temperature drop: $19.1\ \mathrm{K}$ across the insulation and $0.9\ \mathrm{K}$ across the brick. **Check:** summing the drops, $0.91 + 19.1 = 20\ \mathrm{K}$, as required.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — A calorimetry puzzle.</span> [Return →](02.16.html#p8)

(a) Warming the ice: $(0.20)(2100)(10) = 4.2\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{J}$. Melting it: $(0.20)(3.34\times10^{5}) = 6.7\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{J}$. Total $7.1\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{J}$.

(b) The water cooling to $0^\circ\mathrm{C}$ releases $(1.0)(4200)(30) = 1.26\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J}$.

(c) Since $1.26\times10^{5} > 7.1\times10^{4}$, all the ice melts with $5.5\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{J}$ to spare, which warms $1.2\ \mathrm{kg}$ of water by $5.5\times10^{4}/(1.2\times4200) = 11\ \mathrm{K}$. The final state is $1.2\ \mathrm{kg}$ of liquid water at $11^\circ\mathrm{C}$. **Check:** the surplus is about half of the cooling energy, and $11^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is about a third of $30^\circ\mathrm{C}$ — consistent with the water being diluted by the meltwater.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — The greenhouse effect.</span> [Return →](02.16.html#p9)

(a) Absorbed power $= S(1-\alpha)\pi R^2$, radiated power $= 4\pi R^2\sigma T^4$; equating them gives $T^4 = S(1-\alpha)/(4\sigma)$.

(b) $T = \left(\dfrac{1360(0.70)}{4(5.67\times10^{-8})}\right)^{1/4} = (4.20\times10^{9})^{1/4} = 255\ \mathrm{K} = -18^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

(c) The observed mean surface temperature is about $288\ \mathrm{K}$. A single-layer atmosphere that is transparent to visible light but absorbs all infrared re-radiates half downwards, so the surface must radiate twice as much: $T^4$ doubles and $T$ rises by a factor $2^{1/4} = 1.19$, giving $303\ \mathrm{K} = 30^\circ\mathrm{C}$ — too warm, which shows the single-layer model is crude but captures the mechanism. **Check:** the $33\ \mathrm{K}$ discrepancy is exactly the greenhouse effect, and doubling $T^4$ predicts $45\ \mathrm{K}$, of the right order.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Design of a hot-water tank.</span> [Return →](02.16.html#p10)

(a) Side $= 2\pi rH = 2\pi(0.35)(1.5) = 3.30\ \mathrm{m^2}$; ends $= 2\pi r^2 = 0.77\ \mathrm{m^2}$; total $A = 4.07\ \mathrm{m^2}$.

(b) Using the mean radius of the insulation, about $0.39\ \mathrm{m}$, $A\approx4.5\ \mathrm{m^2}$, and
$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{kA\Delta T}{d} = \frac{(0.035)(4.5)(40)}{0.080} = 79\ \mathrm{W}.$$

(c) In a day the loss is $79\times86\,400 = 6.8\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{J}$, and cooling $200\ \mathrm{kg}$ of water by that much gives $\Delta T = 6.8\times10^{6}/((200)(4200)) = 8.1\ \mathrm{K}$ — about $8^\circ\mathrm{C}$ per day. Real tanks lose more because of the metal walls (which conduct), pipe connections (thermal bridges) and convection in the air gap. **Check:** $79\ \mathrm{W}$ is a plausible standing loss for a domestic tank, and $8^\circ\mathrm{C}$ per day matches the "loses a few degrees a day" rule of thumb.
</div>
