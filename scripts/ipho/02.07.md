## 1. Magnetic fields and the force on a moving charge

A magnetic field is the part of the electromagnetic field that acts on *moving* charge. Its strength and direction at a point are written $\mathbf B$ and measured in tesla, $1\ \mathrm{T} = 1\ \mathrm{N\,s\,C^{-1}\,m^{-1}}$.

The force on a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\mathbf v$ is

$$\mathbf F = q\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf B .$$

Read that formula slowly, because all of magnetism's odd behaviour comes out of it.

- The force is **perpendicular to both** $\mathbf v$ and $\mathbf B$. With your right hand: fingers along $\mathbf v$, curl towards $\mathbf B$, thumb points along $\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ for a positive charge (and opposite for a negative one).
- The force is **proportional to the sine** of the angle between them: $F = qvB\sin\theta$. It vanishes when the charge moves *along* the field and is maximal when it moves *across* it.
- Because the force is perpendicular to the velocity, it can never change the speed. A magnetic field does **no work** on a free charge, and the kinetic energy is conserved. This single sentence solves a large fraction of olympiad magnetism questions.
- The force is proportional to $v$, so a charge at rest feels nothing at all. There is no "magnetic charge" to feel it.

{{FIG:force}}

A common mistake is to think the field energy comes from the particle. It does not: the field changes the momentum direction, and the energy bookkeeping stays flat.

## 2. Fields produced by currents: Biot–Savart and Ampère

A current is moving charge, so it produces a magnetic field. The key results, all worth memorising:

$$\text{long straight wire: } B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}, \qquad \text{centre of a loop: } B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2R}, \qquad \text{long solenoid: } B = \mu_0 n I,$$

with $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\,m\,A^{-1}}$ and $n$ the turns per metre. The direction is given by the right-hand rule: grasp the wire with your right hand, thumb along the current, and the fingers curl the way the field lines circle.

{{FIG:biot}}

The general law behind these is the **Biot–Savart law**,

$$d\mathbf B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\boldsymbol\ell\times\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2},$$

and the integral form is **Ampère's law**,

$$\oint \mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = \mu_0 I_{\text{enclosed}} .$$

Ampère's law plays the same role for magnetism that Gauss's law plays for electrostatics: always true, only useful when symmetry makes $\mathbf B$ constant along a chosen loop. It is also incomplete — Maxwell added the displacement current so that a charging capacitor's gap still carries a field — but for steady currents the form above is enough.

The field lines of $\mathbf B$ are always **closed loops**: there are no magnetic monopoles. That is why the flux of $\mathbf B$ through any closed surface is zero, $\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf A = 0$, whereas the flux of $\mathbf E$ gives the enclosed charge.

## 3. Charged particles in magnetic fields: circular motion

If a charge moves perpendicular to a uniform field, the constant perpendicular force makes it travel in a circle:

$$qvB = \frac{mv^2}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{mv}{qB} = \frac{p}{qB}.$$

The radius grows with momentum and shrinks with field. The period, however,

$$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi m}{qB}, \qquad \omega = \frac{qB}{m},$$

does **not** depend on the speed. That is the cyclotron principle: particles of the same species always come round in step, so an alternating voltage of the right frequency can accelerate them again and again. The kinetic energy grows without limit while the period stays fixed — until relativity spoils the trick and the mass effectively rises.

{{LAB:cyclotron}}

If the velocity has a component along the field, that component is untouched while the perpendicular part circles: the path is a **helix** of pitch $v_\parallel T$. This is how a magnetic field traps particles in the van Allen belts and confines plasma in a tokamak, and it is the principle of the mass spectrometer: for a given speed or given accelerating voltage, the radius depends on $m/q$, so ions of different isotopes land at different places.

{{FIG:lorentz}}

## 4. Force on a current-carrying conductor

A wire carrying current $I$ in a field feels a force because the drifting charges inside it do. For a straight length $\ell$,

$$\mathbf F = I\,\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf B, \qquad F = BI\ell\sin\theta .$$

Two parallel wires each sit in the other's field, so they attract when the currents are in the same direction and repel when they are opposite, with force per unit length

$$\frac{F}{\ell} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1I_2}{2\pi d}.$$

Historically this relation *defined* the ampere. A current loop in a field feels a torque $\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf m\times\mathbf B$, where the magnetic moment is $\mathbf m = NI\mathbf A$; this is the electric motor, and it is why a compass needle turns.

## 5. Electromagnetic induction: Faraday and Lenz

Move a conductor through a field, or change the field through a loop, and a voltage appears. Faraday's law states it exactly:

$$\varepsilon = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt}, \qquad \Phi = \int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf A = BA\cos\theta .$$

{{FIG:induction}}

The minus sign is **Lenz's law**: the induced current flows in the direction that *opposes* the change of flux that caused it. If the flux into the page is growing, the induced current circulates so that its own field points out of the page. Lenz's law is energy conservation in disguise — a current that reinforced its own cause would produce energy from nothing.

Three everyday versions of the same law:

- **Motional emf.** A rod of length $\ell$ moving at speed $v$ perpendicular to $B$ acts as a battery with $\varepsilon = B\ell v$. The charges are pushed by the $q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ force until the electric field balances it.
- **Rotating coil.** A coil of $N$ turns and area $A$ spinning at angular frequency $\omega$ in a field gives $\varepsilon = NBA\omega\sin\omega t$ — the alternator, and the reason mains supply is sinusoidal.
- **Eddy currents.** A changing flux in a solid conductor drives loops of current that dissipate energy. That is magnetic braking, the induction hob, and the reason transformers are laminated.

The flux can change because $B$ changes, because the area changes, or because the orientation changes; the exam skill is to identify which of the three is happening before differentiating.

## 6. Self-inductance and magnetic energy

A circuit's own current creates flux through itself: $\Phi = LI$, with $L$ measured in henries. Any change of current is then opposed by a back-emf

$$\varepsilon = -L\frac{dI}{dt},$$

so an inductor is *electrical inertia*: the current cannot jump, exactly as a mass's velocity cannot jump. The energy stored in the field is

$$U = \tfrac12 LI^2 .$$

{{FIG:magnetic-energy}}

For a long solenoid of $N$ turns, length $\ell$ and area $A$ (so $n = N/\ell$), $L = \mu_0 n^2 A\ell = \mu_0 N^2A/\ell$. Dividing $U$ by the volume gives the magnetic energy density,

$$u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0},$$

the exact analogue of $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ for the electric field — a pairing worth remembering, because it lets you compute the inductance of a field configuration without ever drawing a circuit.

In an $RL$ circuit the current approaches its final value with time constant $\tau = L/R$, just as a capacitor charges with $\tau = RC$. The roles are exchanged: a capacitor resists a change of *voltage*, an inductor resists a change of *current*.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">The magnetism checklist.</span>
Direction first (right-hand rules) → is the force doing work? (no) → circular motion $r = p/(qB)$ and $T = 2\pi m/(qB)$ → for induction, identify *what* is changing the flux → apply Lenz to fix the sign → energy: $\tfrac12LI^2$ for a circuit, $B^2/(2\mu_0)$ per unit volume for the field.</div>

<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Two parallel currents.</span> [Return →](02.07.html#p5)

(a) Each wire sits in the field of the other, $B = \mu_0 I/(2\pi d)$:
$$\frac{F}{\ell} = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(15)(20)}{2\pi(0.040)} = 1.5\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}},$$

(c) Antiparallel currents repel with the same magnitude $1.5\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. <span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — A rod on rails (motional emf).</span> [Return →](02.07.html#p6)

(a) $\varepsilon = B\ell v = (0.50)(0.40)(3.0) = 0.60\ \mathrm{V}$, so $I = 0.60/0.60 = 1.0\ \mathrm{A}$. The magnetic force on the rod is $F = BI\ell = (0.50)(1.0)(0.40) = 0.20\ \mathrm{N}$, so an external force of $0.20\ \mathrm{N}$ must be applied to keep the speed constant.

(b) Mechanical power $= Fv = (0.20)(3.0) = 0.60\ \mathrm{W}$; electrical power $= \varepsilon I = I^2R = 0.60\ \mathrm{W}$. They balance exactly — the rod is a generator converting mechanical work into heat.

(c) Any current induced by the rod's motion produces a force $I\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf B$ that opposes the motion: this is Lenz's law in mechanical form, and it guarantees that energy is never created. If the rod is released, that force decelerates it, and all of its kinetic energy ends up as heat in the resistor; it stops once it is at rest, because then $\varepsilon = 0$. **Check:** if the force were assisting the motion, the rod would accelerate and the resistor would heat up with no energy input.

<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — A solenoid as an inductor.</span> [Return →](02.07.html#p8)

(a) $n = 1200/0.20 = 6000\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $A = \pi(0.012)^2 = 4.52\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, so

(b) $U = \tfrac12LI^2 = \tfrac12(3.6\times10^{-3})(2.5)^2 = 1.1\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{J}$. The field is $B = \mu_0nI = (4\pi\times10^{-7})(6000)(2.5) = 1.9\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{T}$, so

(c) $\tau = L/R = 3.6\times10^{-3}/0.50 = 7.2\ \mathrm{ms}$. At the instant of closing, the current is still zero and the whole supply voltage appears across the inductor ($L\,dI/dt = \varepsilon$), so the current starts from zero and rises with time constant $\tau$; the resistor's voltage grows only as the current does. **Check:** at $t = \tau$ the current has reached $63\%$ of its final value $\varepsilon/R$, the same fraction as a charging capacitor's charge.

<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — A changing field through a loop.</span> [Return →](02.07.html#p9)

(a) $A = \pi(0.050)^2 = 7.85\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, so

(c) $P = I^2R = (0.157)^2(0.020) = 4.9\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{W} = \varepsilon^2/R$. The field energy stored in the loop's volume *increases* as $B^2$, so the resistor's heat cannot come from it: the energy comes from whatever external agent is driving the field up, which must do work against the induced current's field. **Check:** $\varepsilon^2/R = (3.14\times10^{-3})^2/0.02 = 4.9\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{W}$, matching $I^2R$ as energy conservation requires.

<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Helical motion and magnetic confinement.</span> [Return →](02.07.html#p10)

(a) Only the perpendicular component bends the path: $v_\perp = v\sin30^\circ = 2.0\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, so
$$r = \frac{mv_\perp}{qB} = \frac{(1.67\times10^{-27})(2.0\times10^{6})}{(1.60\times10^{-19})(0.25)} = 8.4\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi m}{qB} = 2.6\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{s}.$$

(b) $v_\parallel = v\cos30^\circ = 3.46\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, so the pitch is
$$p = v_\parallel T = (3.46\times10^{6})(2.62\times10^{-7}) = 0.91\ \mathrm{m}.$$

(c) The circling charge is itself a tiny current loop, so it carries a magnetic moment $\mu = \tfrac12mv_\perp^{2}/B$ pointing along $-\mathbf B$, and it feels a force $-\mu\,\nabla B$ that pushes it towards *weaker* field. The Lorentz force still does no work on a point charge, so the speed $|v|$ never changes; what the converging field does is turn part of the axial motion into perpendicular motion, so $v_\perp$ grows and $v_\parallel$ falls while $v_\perp^2+v_\parallel^2$ stays fixed. When $v_\parallel$ reaches zero the particle sits at the mirror point, with all its motion perpendicular to the field, and it then unwinds back the way it came. The axial momentum is not destroyed: it is exchanged with the field configuration that produced the converging field, and the particle leaves with the same speed it entered with.

**Check:** $v_\perp^2 + v_\parallel^2 = (2.0^2 + 3.46^2)\times10^{12} = 16\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}} = v^2$, so only the split between the two components changes, never the total speed.
</div>
