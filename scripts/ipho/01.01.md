## 1. What the first page of every problem is really asking

Physics olympiads do not test whether you can substitute numbers faster than a calculator. They test whether you can turn a messy description into a model. A model is a short list of decisions: which objects have mass, which forces matter, which effects you throw away and why. Once the model is written down, the mathematics is usually ordinary.

There is a reliable working order:

1. **Sketch** the geometry and label the quantities with symbols before any numbers appear.
2. **Name the regime.** Is it fast or slow, dense or dilute, quantum or classical, relativistic or Newtonian?
3. **Look for symmetry** before writing equations. A symmetry can delete an unknown outright.
4. **Check dimensions** as soon as you have an expression.
5. **Take a limit.** If your formula fails in an obvious limit, it is wrong.
6. **Estimate the answer** before computing it, so a factor-of-ten mistake announces itself.

## 2. Units and dimensions: the free check

Every mechanical quantity is a product of powers of mass $[M]$, length $[L]$ and time $[T]$ (add current $[I]$ and temperature $[\Theta]$ for electricity and heat). A force has dimensions $[M\,L\,T^{-2}]$; energy has $[M\,L^2\,T^{-2}]$.

Two rules do all the work:

- **Both sides of an equation carry the same dimensions.** Adding a length to a time is meaningless, no matter how nice the algebra looks.
- **Only dimensionless groups can be arguments of a function** such as $\sin$, $\exp$ or $\ln$. If you find yourself writing $\exp(L)$, the expression is wrong.

Dimensional analysis cannot give you the pure number in front. From $T \propto \sqrt{L/g}$ you learn the exponent $\tfrac12$ but not $2\pi$. That is still a great deal: it tells you how the period changes when you change the length, and it rules out whole families of wrong formulas.

## 3. Scaling laws: the whole physics in one exponent

Most olympiad relationships are power laws, $y = kx^n$. A power law becomes a straight line on log–log axes, and the slope is the exponent:

$$\log y = \log k + n\log x .$$

This is why a log–log graph is a measuring instrument, not decoration. If a student collects planet data and plots $\log T$ against $\log a$, the slope comes out as $1.5$ and they have measured Kepler's third law.

Scaling also answers questions that look impossible. A sphere of radius $R$ has area $\propto R^2$ and volume $\propto R^3$. Stretch everything by a factor $s$ and:

- area grows by $s^2$,
- volume and mass grow by $s^3$,
- the ratio $\text{area}/\text{mass} \propto s^{-1}$ falls.

That single ratio explains why insects cannot be the size of cars: the surface that supplies oxygen grows more slowly than the body that consumes it.

## 4. Approximation you can defend

An approximation is only legitimate when you can say how large the error is. Three tools are enough for most of the syllabus.

**Small parameter.** If a quantity $x$ is small compared with 1, then

$$(1+x)^\alpha \approx 1 + \alpha x,\qquad \sin x \approx x,\qquad e^{x} \approx 1 + x,\qquad \ln(1+x)\approx x .$$

To first order in $x$ these are usually all you need, and they are enough to keep the leading correction — which is exactly what earns marks in estimation problems.

**Taylor series.** Around a point $a$,

$$f(a+\delta) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\delta + \tfrac12 f''(a)\delta^2 .$$

The first term is the value, the second tells you the slope, the third tells you whether a stationary point is a minimum. This is also how you show that small oscillations are harmonic: near a minimum, $U(q) \approx U_0 + \tfrac12 U''(q_0)(q-q_0)^2$, and a quadratic potential means a spring.

**Dimensional and physical limits.** Push a parameter to $0$ or $\infty$ and check the answer behaves as the physical situation does. If a formula for the speed of a rolling ball does not reproduce $v = \sqrt{2gh}$ as the radius goes to zero, something has been lost.

## 5. Order-of-magnitude estimates

Olympiad problems often ask for an estimate rather than a measurement. The method is a chain of ratios, each of which you know to within a factor of two or three:

$$\text{answer} = (\text{rate or density}) \times (\text{geometry}) \times (\text{correction})\ .$$

Two habits make estimates reliable. First, keep units visible so that the wrong numerators cancel audibly. Second, quote the uncertainty of the result — with four factors each uncertain by a factor of two, you cannot quote three significant figures honestly.

## 6. Symmetry and the method of choosing the right frame

If a configuration looks identical after a transformation, the physical result cannot distinguish the two arrangements, so the answer must be invariant under that transformation. This is worth more than an equation.

Three applications recur:

- **Mirror or rotational symmetry** removes a force component or a field component without solving anything.
- **Invariance in a free-fall frame** says a freely falling lift is equivalent to gravity-free space: spring, pendulum and scale questions often collapse.
- **Gauge-like shifts in potential** remind you that only potential differences do work.

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Dimensional fingerprint.</span> [Return →](01.01.html#p1)

Write $T = C\,m^a k^b$ with $C$ dimensionless. Dimensions give $\mathrm{T} = \mathrm{M}^{a}\left(\mathrm{M}\,\mathrm{T}^{-2}\right)^{b} = \mathrm{M}^{a+b}\,\mathrm{T}^{-2b}$. Matching powers: $a+b=0$ and $-2b=1$, so $b=-\tfrac12$, $a=\tfrac12$. Hence

$$T = C\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} .$$

Dimensional analysis cannot fix $C$; the true value is $2\pi$, which requires the equation of motion. **Check:** stiffer spring (larger $k$) gives a shorter period; heavier mass gives a longer one, both physically sensible.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — How big is a star?</span> [Return →](01.01.html#p2)

Check the dimensions of $\sigma T^4$: it is power per unit area, $[\mathrm{M}\,\mathrm{T}^{-3}]$. Multiplying by $R^2$ (an area) gives power $[\mathrm{M}\,\mathrm{L}^2\,\mathrm{T}^{-3}]$, the dimensions of luminosity. The relation is consistent.

Doubling $R$ multiplies $L$ by $2^2 = 4$. Doubling $T$ multiplies $L$ by $2^4=16$. Temperature is the stronger lever because a hotter surface radiates more per unit area *and* the spectrum shifts to shorter, more energetic wavelengths. **Check:** a star twice as hot and the same size is $16\times$ brighter in this model, which is why hot stars dominate the light and cool stars dominate the numbers.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — The mass of a raindrop's argument.</span> [Return →](01.01.html#p3)

(a) Let $m = C\gamma^a r^b g^c$. Since $[\gamma]=\mathrm{M}\,\mathrm{T}^{-2}$, $[r]=\mathrm{L}$, $[g]=\mathrm{L}\,\mathrm{T}^{-2}$:

$$\mathrm{M} = \mathrm{M}^{a}\,\mathrm{L}^{b+c}\,\mathrm{T}^{-2a-2c}.$$

Matching: $a=1$; $b+c=0$; $-2-2c=0 \Rightarrow c=-1$, $b=1$. So $m \approx \gamma r/g$ times a number of order one. (The exact combination is $2\pi\gamma r/g$ when the drop's weight matches the rim force.)

(b) Take $m \approx 2\pi \gamma r / g$ with $g=9.8$: at $r=1\ \mathrm{mm}$, $m \approx 2\pi(0.07)(10^{-3})/9.8 \approx 4.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{kg}$. This is about ten times larger than a real 1 mm drop, so real drops detach at roughly $r \sim 0.3$–$0.5\ \mathrm{mm}$ before the weight exceeds the rim force. The estimate gives the correct scalings $m \propto \gamma r$, so doubling $\gamma$ doubles the hanging mass and doubling $r$ doubles the mass a rim of that size can hold — meaning the *shape* of the drop sets the limit, not the simple balance alone. **Check:** the result is a mass, is proportional to surface tension, and becomes zero when gravity is infinite — all physically required.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Small-angle error budget.</span> [Return →](01.01.html#p4)

(a) $\sin\theta = \theta - \theta^3/6 + \cdots$, so the fractional error of using $\theta$ is $\theta^2/6$. At $\theta = 0.30$: $\theta^2/6 = 0.09/6 = 0.015$, i.e. about $1.5\%$ (it *underestimates* by that fraction).

(b) Set $\theta^2/6 = 0.01 \Rightarrow \theta = \sqrt{0.06} = 0.245\ \mathrm{rad} \approx 14^\circ$. Numerically $\sin(0.245)/0.245 - 1 = -0.0099$, so the estimate is accurate to a few per cent of itself. **Check:** the approximation is generous enough for olympiad work below about $15^\circ$, and you can now say so with a number instead of a feeling.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Reading a log–log slope.</span> [Return →](01.01.html#p5)

(a) $n=\Delta\log y/\Delta\log x = (2.90-1.30)/2 = 0.80$.

(b) At $\log x = 0$, $\log y = 1.30$ so $k=10^{1.30} = 20$ with units $\mathrm{m\,s^{-0.8}}$. Fractional powers look strange but are legitimate for empirical laws.

(c) The slope uses logarithms, so a factor-of-two error in one reading shifts a log by only $0.30$; the average line barely moves. A naive two-point calculation of $y/x$ would be ruined by that same point because errors enter additively. **Check:** a log–log plot converts multiplicative errors into additive ones, which is precisely why it is robust.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Symmetry in a lift.</span> [Return →](01.01.html#p6)

In a freely falling frame every object shares the acceleration $g$ downward, so *relative* to the car nothing accelerates. The pendulum hangs limp: the effective gravity is zero, so its period $T=2\pi\sqrt{L/g_{\text{eff}}}$ becomes infinite. The block does not compress its spring beyond its natural length, and the scale reads zero. All three behave as though gravity had been switched off — this is the symmetry between a falling frame and a gravity-free frame.

With a downward acceleration of $0.5g$, the effective gravity in the car is $g_{\text{eff}} = g - 0.5g = 0.5g$: the pendulum swings with a period $\sqrt{2}$ times longer, the block compresses the spring by half its usual amount, and the scale reads half the weight. **Check:** the limiting case $a\to g$ reproduces the free-fall answer, and $a\to0$ reproduces a lift at rest.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Fermi: number of stars in the observable sky.</span> [Return →](01.01.html#p7)

(a) Photos needed: $4\times10^{4}/10 = 4\times10^{3}$. Stars: $4\times10^{3}\times10^{4} = 4\times10^{7}$, i.e. about $10^{8}$ stars to order of magnitude.

(b) The survey's $10^{9}$ is about $25\times$ larger. Real wide-field images overlap and repeated coverage increases depth; more importantly, a survey's plate limit changes how many stars a square degree contains *non-linearly*, and "stars" usually includes faint distant stars in crowded fields that a simple count under-represents. A factor of order ten is exactly the honesty range of a four-factor chain. **Check:** the chain has three factors each known to about a factor of two, so a factor $\sqrt{2^3}\approx3$ random scatter plus systematic choices fully explains the gap.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Power-law scaling of a body.</span> [Return →](01.01.html#p8)

(a) Surface area $A \propto s^2$, mass $m\propto s^3$, so $A/m \propto s^{-1}$: larger animals lose less heat per unit mass.

(b) With heat content $\propto m$ and loss $\propto A$, the cooling time is $\tau \propto m/A \propto s$. A body twice as large in every dimension holds its temperature roughly twice as long.

(c) Advantage: larger bodies retain heat in cold environments. Disadvantage: the same surface must supply oxygen and food for a growing volume, so internal transport must become specialised (lungs, circulation). The model is crude because it ignores metabolism, insulation, shape change and internal convection — a real organism's metabolic rate is not simply proportional to volume. **Check:** the exponent comes from the ratio of two geometric powers, which is the robust part; the coefficient is biology.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — The limits test that catches wrong formulas.</span> [Return →](01.01.html#p9)

(a) (i) $v^2R/(2GM-v^2R)$ has $\mathrm{L}^2\,\mathrm{T}^{-2}\cdot\mathrm{L}/(\mathrm{L}^3\,\mathrm{T}^{-2}) = \mathrm{L}$: correct. (ii) $vR/\sqrt{GM}$ has $\mathrm{L}\,\mathrm{T}^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{L}/(\mathrm{L}^{3/2}\,\mathrm{T}^{-1}) = \mathrm{L}^{1/2}$: wrong — a square root of a length is not a height. (iii) $v^2/(2g)$ has $\mathrm{L}$, correct as a small-$v$ limit.

(b) As $v\to0$, (i) and (iii) give $h\to0$; (ii) gives zero too. So the small-$v$ limit alone does not discriminate (i) from (iii).

(c) As $v^2R \to 2GM$ — the escape condition — (i) gives $h\to\infty$, which is exactly right: at escape speed the projectile never returns. Formula (iii) gives a finite height and is therefore wrong for speeds approaching escape. Formula (i) is the correct energy result and reduces to $v^2/2g$ when $v^2R\ll GM$. **Check:** only (i) is monotonic in $v$ over the whole allowed range and diverges at escape.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Building a model from scratch.</span> [Return →](01.01.html#p10)

(a) Let $R = C E^a \rho^b g^c$. Dimensions: $\mathrm{L} = \left(\mathrm{M}\,\mathrm{L}^2\,\mathrm{T}^{-2}\right)^{a}\left(\mathrm{M}\,\mathrm{L}^{-3}\right)^{b}\left(\mathrm{L}\,\mathrm{T}^{-2}\right)^{c}$. Mass: $a+b=0$. Time: $-2a-2c=0 \Rightarrow c=-a$. Length: $2a-3b+c=1 \Rightarrow 2a+3a-a = 4a = 1$, so $a=\tfrac14$, $b=-\tfrac14$, $c=-\tfrac14$:

$$R = C\left(\frac{E}{\rho g}\right)^{1/4}.$$

(b) A strength term $Y$ (pressure, dimensions $[\mathrm{M}\,\mathrm{L}^{-1}\,\mathrm{T}^{-2}]$) enters when the target is strong rather than gravity-dominated. Then a dimensionless ratio such as $E/(Y R^3)$ can appear and the single power law becomes a function of a dimensionless group — the crater transitions from strength- to gravity-dominated.

(c) At hypervelocity, the impactor is vaporised and the energy is delivered as a blast, so its shape is irrelevant — only $E$ survives. At low speed the impactor simply presses a hole of its own size, so its radius $a$ must enter. Every such regime change is a missing quantity, and dimensional analysis tells you *which* missing quantity is needed by where the fit fails.
</div>
