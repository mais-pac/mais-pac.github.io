## 1. The algebra that physics actually uses

Mathematics papers test technique; a physics problem tests whether you can *choose* the technique. The algebra you need is narrow and repetitive: solve a pair of linear equations, complete a square, take a ratio from similar triangles, use one trigonometric identity, and handle a power or root without slipping a factor.

For an IPhO problem the most valuable habit is to do the algebra **symbolically first**. Numbers introduced early hide structure and make a mistake unrecoverable; a symbolic result shows which combination governs the physics.

## 2. Functions: shapes you should be able to sketch

{{FIG:functions}}

| function | shape | grows like | typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x^n$, $n>0$ | through the origin, flat then steep | $x^n$ | area, volume, energy |
| $x^{1/2}$ | steep then flat | slow | period of a pendulum, orbital speed |
| $1/x$ | hyperbola | falls fast | Coulomb, gravity, pressure |
| $1/x^2$ | falls faster | — | inverse-square fields |
| $\sin x,\cos x$ | oscillation | bounded | waves, circular motion |
| $e^{-x}$ | decay | bounded | cooling, RC circuits |

Two algebraic manipulations recur so often that they are worth memorising:

- **Completing the square:** $ax^2+bx+c = a\left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 + c - \frac{b^2}{4a}$. This reads off the vertex of a parabola immediately, which is usually the physically interesting point (maximum range, minimum energy, critical damping).
- **Rationalising:** $\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b}} = \frac{\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}}{a-b}$. This turns a small difference of large quantities — the usual source of catastrophic cancellation in numerical work — into a stable expression.

## 3. Geometry: similarity before trigonometry

When two angles of a triangle agree, all three sides are in a fixed ratio. Almost every "find the length in this diagram" problem is a similarity problem in disguise, and writing the proportion is faster and less error-prone than setting up a coordinate system.

{{FIG:similar}}

The altitude rule in a right triangle is the standard example: the altitude to the hypotenuse creates two smaller triangles, each similar to the original, so

$$h^2 = c_1c_2, \qquad a^2 = c_1c, \qquad b^2 = c_2c .$$

These three are Pythagoras plus similarity, and they solve most ladder-and-shadow problems in one line.

For circles, three facts cover a great deal:

- The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference on the same arc.
- A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.
- The power of a point: for a point $P$ outside a circle and any secant through it, $PT^2 = PA\cdot PB$ where $T$ is the tangent contact point.

## 4. Trigonometry

{{FIG:trig}}

Beyond the definitions, three tools do nearly all the olympiad work:

- **The sine rule** $\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = \dfrac{b}{\sin B} = 2R$ and the **cosine rule** $c^2 = a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C$. The cosine rule is the generalisation of Pythagoras; whenever a problem has two known sides at a known angle, this is the tool.
- **Small angles:** for $\theta$ in radians, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, $\tan\theta \approx \theta$, $\cos\theta \approx 1 - \theta^2/2$. These are the approximations that make a pendulum simple-harmonic.
- **Products to sums:** $2\sin A\sin B = \cos(A-B) - \cos(A+B)$ and its relatives, which convert a beat pattern or a standing wave into two travelling waves.

## 5. Simultaneous equations and constraints

A physics problem with $N$ unknowns needs $N$ independent statements. The standard procedure:

1. Write the constraint equations (geometry, conservation, equilibrium).
2. Count them; if there are fewer than unknowns, you have missed a condition — usually a boundary condition or a symmetry.
3. Eliminate symbolically, not numerically, so that the final expression shows the physics.
4. Check the dimensions of every intermediate line, not only the last one.

{{LAB:quad}}

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">Algebra in a physics answer.</span>
A grader can only award method marks for algebra they can follow. So: one relation per line, no arithmetic in your head, symbols until the last line, and a dimensional check at every stage. A symbolic answer that is dimensionally right and reduces correctly in a limiting case is usually worth most of the marks even if a numerical slip spoils the final number.</div>

<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Completing the square.</span> [Return →](04.01.html#p1)

(a) $R$ is largest when $\sin2\theta = 1$, that is $2\theta = 90^\circ$, so $\theta = 45^\circ$.

(b) $\sin2\theta = \dfrac{Rg}{v^2} = \dfrac{40\times9.81}{400} = 0.981$, so $2\theta = 78.8^\circ$ or $101.2^\circ$, giving $\theta = 39.4^\circ$ or $50.6^\circ$. The two angles are complementary, as they must be, because $\sin2\theta$ is symmetric about $45^\circ$. **Check:** the maximum range at this speed is $v^2/g = 40.8\ \mathrm{m}$, so $40\ \mathrm{m}$ is just inside the reachable range — which is why the two angles are close to $45^\circ$.

<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — A ratio from similarity.</span> [Return →](04.01.html#p2)

(a) $\dfrac{H}{12.0} = \dfrac{1.80}{2.40}$, so $H = 9.0\ \mathrm{m}$.

(b) Both are right triangles, and the Sun's rays are parallel, so the angle of elevation is the same at both sites. Two equal angles are enough for similarity.

(c) The ground may not be level, the pole may not be vertical, and the shadows may not be measured at the same instant, so the Sun angle may differ slightly between the two sites. **Check:** a difference of $1^\circ$ in the Sun's altitude changes the ratio by about $2\%$, which is $0.2\ \mathrm{m}$ on a $9\ \mathrm{m}$ tree.

<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Two equations, two unknowns.</span> [Return →](04.01.html#p3)

(a) With $a$ the acceleration of $m_2$ downwards: $m_2g - T = m_2a$ and $T - m_1g = m_1a$.

(b) Adding eliminates $T$: $(m_2-m_1)g = (m_1+m_2)a$, so $a = \dfrac{2.0}{8.0}\times9.81 = 2.45\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. Then $T = m_1(g+a) = 3.0\times12.26 = 36.8\ \mathrm{N}$.

(c) With $m_1 = m_2 = m$, $a = 0$ and $T = mg$: the string simply supports each mass. **Check:** when $m_1\to0$, $a\to g$ and $T\to0$, as a free-falling mass requires.

<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — The cosine rule in a field problem.</span> [Return →](04.01.html#p4)

(a) Each force points along a side away from a vertex of the equilateral triangle, so the angle between the two force vectors is $60^\circ$.

(b) Each force has magnitude $F = kq^2/d^2$. Adding two equal vectors at $60^\circ$: $R = 2F\cos30^\circ = \sqrt3\,kq^2/d^2$.

(c) Bringing the fixed charges closer shrinks $d$, so each force grows as $1/d^2$ *and* the geometry stays equilateral, giving $R \propto 1/d^2$ for fixed $q$ — but if the experiment keeps the total charge fixed while $d$ changes, the potential energy $\propto 1/d$ grows faster in relative terms. **Check:** the factor $\sqrt3$ is the classic triangular-lattice result and reappears in the geometry of close-packed crystals.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Small-angle approximations.</span> [Return →](04.01.html#p5)

(a) The restoring force is $mg\sin\theta$ along the arc, and the displacement is $s = L\theta$. For small $\theta$, $\sin\theta\approx\theta$, so $F \approx -(mg/L)s$: a linear restoring force, hence simple harmonic motion with $\omega = \sqrt{g/L}$.

(b) $\theta = 5.0^\circ = 0.0873\ \mathrm{rad}$, and $\sin\theta = 0.08716$. The fractional difference is $(0.08727-0.08716)/0.08727 = 1.3\times10^{-3}$, about $0.13\%$.

(c) With $\sin\theta \approx \theta - \theta^3/6$, the error is $\theta^2/6 = 0.01$, so $\theta = 0.245\ \mathrm{rad} = 14^\circ$. **Check:** the period error goes as $\theta^2/16$, a quarter of this, so a $5^\circ$ pendulum keeps time to about $0.03\%$ — far better than a human reaction time.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — A discriminant condition.</span> [Return →](04.01.html#p6)

(a) With $y = h + v\sin\theta\,t - \frac12gt^2$ and $y = 0$: $\frac12gt^2 - v\sin\theta\,t - h = 0$.

(b) The discriminant is $v^2\sin^2\theta + 2gh$, which is a sum of two non-negative terms and is therefore positive for any $h>0$ or any non-zero launch speed. So there are two real roots.

(c) Physically, a projectile launched from a height always reaches the ground, whatever the angle — the two roots are the "now" root and the "later" fall. A negative discriminant would mean the projectile never arrives, which happens only if the ground is replaced by a curved Earth and the projectile enters orbit, or if you try to solve for a time before launch. **Check:** one root is negative, corresponding to the fictional past trajectory that would have passed through the launch point from below.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Inverse-square geometry.</span> [Return →](04.01.html#p7)

(a) $I \propto 1/r^2$, so at $2r$ the intensity is $1/2^2 = 1/4$.

(b) By symmetry the point is midway: $2.0\ \mathrm{m}$ from each lamp. Because the lamps are identical, the condition $P/(4\pi x^2) = P/(4\pi(4-x)^2)$ reduces to $x = 4-x$.

(c) The power cancels from the equation: the equality of illuminance depends only on the two distances. If the lamps differ, the point moves towards the weaker one, and the balance condition becomes $P_1/x^2 = P_2/(d-x)^2$. **Check:** with $P_1 = 4P_2$ the balance point is at $x = 2d/3$ from the stronger lamp, which is the working principle of a photometric bench.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Minimising a function.</span> [Return →](04.01.html#p8)

(a) Resolving vertically: $N = mg - F\sin\theta$. Constant speed needs $F\cos\theta = \mu N = \mu(mg - F\sin\theta)$, so
$$F = \frac{\mu mg}{\cos\theta + \mu\sin\theta}.$$

(b) $F$ is smallest when the denominator is largest. Setting the derivative of $\cos\theta+\mu\sin\theta$ to zero gives $-\sin\theta+\mu\cos\theta = 0$, that is $\tan\theta = \mu$. (Alternatively write the denominator as $\sqrt{1+\mu^2}\cos(\theta-\phi)$ with $\tan\phi = \mu$, maximised at $\theta = \phi$.)

(c) With $\mu = 0.40$, $\theta = 21.8^\circ$ and $\cos\theta+\mu\sin\theta = 0.928+0.149 = 1.077$, so $F = 0.40\times10\times9.81/1.077 = 36\ \mathrm{N}$. Pulling horizontally would need $0.40\times98.1 = 39\ \mathrm{N}$. **Check:** pulling upwards at the friction angle lightens the normal force exactly as much as it loses in horizontal component — the classic optimum.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Three equations, one result.</span> [Return →](04.01.html#p9)

(a) Let $N_w$ be the wall force (horizontal), $N_f$ the floor normal force and $f$ the friction. Then $N_w = f$, $N_f = Mg$, and taking moments about the floor end: $N_wL\sin\theta = Mg\frac{L}{2}\cos\theta$.

(b) From the moment equation $N_w = \frac{Mg}{2}\cot\theta$, so $f = \frac{Mg}{2}\cot\theta$. The friction law requires $f \le \mu Mg$, hence $\frac{1}{2}\cot\theta \le \mu$.

(c) As $\theta\to90^\circ$ (rod nearly vertical) $\cot\theta\to0$ and almost no friction is needed. As $\theta\to0$ the required friction diverges, so a rod cannot rest at a very shallow angle on any floor with finite $\mu$. **Check:** at $\mu = 0.5$ the critical angle is $\theta = 45^\circ$, a sensible number for a wooden rod on a wooden floor.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — An identity in disguise.</span> [Return →](04.01.html#p10)

(a) $A\sin(\omega t) + A\sin(\omega t+\phi) = 2A\cos\frac{\phi}{2}\sin\left(\omega t + \frac{\phi}{2}\right)$.

(b) The amplitude is $2A\cos\frac{\phi}{2}$.

(c) The resultant vanishes when $\cos\frac{\phi}{2} = 0$, that is $\phi = \pi$ (or $180^\circ$): the waves are perfectly out of phase and cancel everywhere. **Check:** the result is the simplest case of interference, and it shows that the combined amplitude can be anywhere from $0$ to $2A$ — energy is redistributed, not lost, since the intensity $4A^2\cos^2(\phi/2)$ peaks at $\phi = 0$.
</div>
