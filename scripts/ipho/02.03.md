## 1. Newton's second law is about change, not motion

$$\mathbf F_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\mathbf p}{dt} = m\mathbf a \quad (\text{constant } m).$$

A force does not *cause* motion; it causes *change of* motion. That single sentence disposes of most beginner errors. In components, the law is a pair of independent scalar equations, and problems become bookkeeping — provided you drew the free-body diagram first.

Three equivalent bookkeeping systems are available for any mechanics problem:

- **Force and acceleration** — best when you need the motion at every instant.
- **Energy** — best when the question only cares about speeds and heights.
- **Momentum** — best when forces are large, brief and unknown (collisions, explosions, kicks).

Strong solutions choose the cheapest system, not the most familiar one.

## 2. Work, energy and the area under a graph

Work done by a force along a displacement is

$$W = \int \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r,$$

which for a constant force is $Fd\cos\theta$, and graphically is the **area under an $F$–$x$ graph**. Power is the rate of doing work, $P = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v$.

For a spring, $F = kx$, so the stored energy is the triangular area

$$U = \tfrac12 kx^2 .$$

For a uniform gravitational field near the surface, $U = mgh$. For the inverse-square field, $U = -GMm/r$ — the minus sign is what makes bound orbits possible.

{{FIG:work-area}}

The work–energy theorem follows from Newton's law and is the workhorse of olympiad mechanics:

$$W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K .$$

With only conservative forces, $K + U = \text{constant}$ and the problem collapses to one equation.

## 3. Momentum, impulse and collisions

Momentum is $\mathbf p = m\mathbf v$, and the impulse–momentum theorem says

$$\int \mathbf F\,dt = \Delta \mathbf p .$$

The integral is the area under a **force–time** graph. This is why airbags work: the same $\Delta p$ spread over a longer time means a smaller peak force.

{{FIG:impulse}}

In a collision between two bodies with no external impulse, total momentum is conserved:

$$m_1\mathbf u_1 + m_2\mathbf u_2 = m_1\mathbf v_1 + m_2\mathbf v_2 .$$

In one dimension this is one equation with two unknowns, so you need a second piece of physics. The usual choice is the coefficient of restitution,

$$e = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{u_1 - u_2},$$

with $e=1$ elastic and $e=0$ perfectly inelastic. Solving the pair gives

$$v_1 = \frac{(m_1 - e m_2)u_1 + (1+e)m_2u_2}{m_1+m_2},\qquad
v_2 = \frac{(m_2 - e m_1)u_2 + (1+e)m_1u_1}{m_1+m_2}.$$

The kinetic energy lost is

$$\Delta K = \tfrac12\,\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}(1-e^2)(u_1-u_2)^2 .$$

Everything about collisions is visible in that formula: the loss is largest for $e = 0$, vanishes for $e = 1$, and is proportional to the square of the *relative* speed.

## 4. Rotation: the same laws, one level up

For a rigid body about a fixed axis,

$$\tau = I\alpha,\qquad L = I\omega,\qquad K_{\text{rot}} = \tfrac12 I\omega^2,$$

with

$$I = \sum m_i r_i^2 \quad\text{or}\quad I = \int r^2 dm .$$

The parallel-axis theorem, $I = I_{cm} + Md^2$, lets you shift the axis once you know the centre-of-mass value.

{{FIG:rotation}}

Rolling without slipping ties the two motions together, $v = \omega R$, so

$$K = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 I\omega^2 = \tfrac12 mv^2\left(1 + \frac{I}{mR^2}\right).$$

That bracket is why a hoop, a disc and a sphere released together down a slope arrive in that order — and why the answer does not depend on the mass or the radius, only on the *shape factor* $I/(mR^2)$.

## 5. Frames, fictitious forces, and when to leave the inertial frame

In a frame accelerating with $\mathbf a_0$ relative to an inertial one, Newton's law needs an extra term:

$$m\mathbf a' = \mathbf F - m\mathbf a_0 .$$

The extra $-m\mathbf a_0$ is a **fictitious force**. It is not an interaction; it is the price of doing bookkeeping in a non-inertial frame. The same logic produces the centrifugal force $-m\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)$ and the Coriolis force $-2m\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v'$ in a rotating frame.

Use fictitious forces when the frame buys you simplicity — a bead in a groove on a turntable, a pendulum in an accelerating lift — and be explicit that they are not interactions.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">Choosing the tool.</span>
Ask three questions in order. (1) Are the forces known at every instant? If yes, use Newton and acceleration. (2) Does the question only involve speeds and positions? Then use energy. (3) Are the forces brief, internal or unknown? Then use momentum. Write the choice down; examiners mark reasoning, not just answers.</div>

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Work by a varying force.</span> [Return →](02.03.html#p1)

(a) $W = \int_0^3 6x^2\,dx = \left[2x^3\right]_0^3 = 54\ \mathrm{J}$.

(b) $K = \tfrac12 mv^2 = 54 \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{108/2} = \sqrt{54} = 7.35\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(c) $P = Fv = (6\times9)(7.35) = 397\ \mathrm{W}$. **Check:** the force grows as $x^2$ and the speed grows as $x^{3/2}$, so the power grows as $x^{7/2}$ — steeply, as expected at large $x$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — A block on a rough slope with a spring.</span> [Return →](02.03.html#p2)

(a) With total distance travelled $s + d$ along the slope, the energy balance is
$$mg(s+d)\sin\theta - \mu mg(s+d)\cos\theta = \tfrac12kd^2 .$$

(b) This is a quadratic in $d$:
$$\tfrac12kd^2 - mg(\sin\theta - \mu\cos\theta)d - mgs(\sin\theta - \mu\cos\theta) = 0,$$
whose positive root is the compression.

(c) For $\mu = 0$ it becomes $\tfrac12kd^2 = mg(s+d)\sin\theta$, the standard spring–slope result. Dimensions: each term is an energy, so the equation is homogeneous. **Check:** if $\tan\theta < \mu$ the block never slides, and the quadratic's roots become unphysical — a sign that the model is being applied outside its range.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Elastic and inelastic collisions from one formula.</span> [Return →](02.03.html#p3)

(a) For $e=1$ with $u_2=0$: $v_1 = \dfrac{m_1-m_2}{m_1+m_2}u_1 = \dfrac{1}{3}(3.0) = 1.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $v_2 = \dfrac{2m_1}{m_1+m_2}u_1 = \dfrac{4}{3}(3.0) = 4.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. **Check:** momentum $2(1)+1(4) = 6 = 2(3)$ and kinetic energy $1 + 8 = 9\ \mathrm{J}$, both conserved.

(b) $e=0$: common velocity $= 6/3 = 2.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(c) $\mu = (2)(1)/3 = 0.667\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $(u_1-u_2)^2 = 9$. For $e=0$: $\Delta K = \tfrac12(0.667)(9) = 3.0\ \mathrm{J}$. Directly: $K_i = 9\ \mathrm{J}$, $K_f = \tfrac12(3)(4) = 6\ \mathrm{J}$, difference $3\ \mathrm{J}$ — matched. For $e = 0.5$: $\Delta K = 3.0(1-0.25) = 2.25\ \mathrm{J}$. **Check:** the energy lost grows as $1-e^2$, reaching zero only at $e=1$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — The ballistic pendulum.</span> [Return →](02.03.html#p4)

(a) The impact lasts milliseconds; the external forces (gravity, string tension) are negligible over that interval, so momentum is conserved. During the impact the bullet deforms the block, converting kinetic energy into heat and deformation, so mechanical energy is *not* conserved. Afterwards, the block swings with no further non-conservative work, so energy is conserved again.

(b) Momentum: $mv = (m+M)V$. Energy: $\tfrac12(m+M)V^2 = (m+M)gh$, so $V = \sqrt{2gh}$ and
$$v = \frac{m+M}{m}\sqrt{2gh}.$$

(c) $\dfrac{K_f}{K_i} = \dfrac{(m+M)V^2}{mv^2} = \dfrac{m}{m+M}$, so the fraction lost is $\dfrac{M}{m+M}$. With $m=0.010\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $M=2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ this is $0.995$ — over 99% of the energy is lost, which is why the device was abandoned in favour of electronic timing. **Check:** as $m\to\infty$ the fraction lost $\to1$; as $m\to0$ the bullet carries almost all the energy away in the block.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Rolling race.</span> [Return →](02.03.html#p5)

(a) Energy with rolling constraint: $Mg\sin\theta - f = Ma$ and $fR = I\alpha$ with $\alpha = a/R$. Eliminating $f$,
$$a = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + I/(MR^2)} .$$
Hoop: $1+1 = 2$, so $a = 0.5g\sin\theta$. Disc: $1+0.5 = 1.5$, so $a = 0.667g\sin\theta$. Sphere: $1+0.4 = 1.4$, so $a = 0.714g\sin\theta$.

(b) Times for the same distance scale as $1/\sqrt{a}$: $t_{\text{sphere}}:t_{\text{disc}}:t_{\text{hoop}} = 1 : 1.035 : 1.195$. The sphere wins, the hoop loses.

(c) $M$ and $R$ cancel because both the driving force and the inertia scale with them; only the shape factor $I/(MR^2)$ survives. If the bodies slid without friction, $a = g\sin\theta$ for all three and they would tie — friction is what breaks the symmetry. **Check:** the sphere's shape factor $2/5$ is the smallest, giving the largest acceleration.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Two blocks and a pulley.</span> [Return →](02.03.html#p6)

(a) $a = \dfrac{m_2-m_1}{m_1+m_2}g = \dfrac{2}{8}(9.81) = 2.45\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ toward $m_2$'s side. Tension: $T = m_1(g+a) = 3.0(12.26) = 36.8\ \mathrm{N}$.

(b) As $m_2\to m_1$, $a\to0$ and $T\to m_1g$ — static hanging. As $m_1\to0$, $a\to g$ and $T\to0$: the heavy mass falls freely and the light mass provides no resistance.

(c) With a pulley of inertia $I$, the constraint becomes $a = \alpha R$ and the torque equation gives $(T_2 - T_1)R = I\alpha$. The result is
$$a = \frac{(m_2-m_1)g}{m_1+m_2+I/R^2},$$
so the pulley acts as an extra mass $I/R^2$. Setting $I=0$ recovers part (a). **Check:** the extra term is dimensionally a mass and positive, so it always slows the system.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Explosion in mid-air.</span> [Return →](02.03.html#p7)

(a) $3mv = m(0) + 2mv' \Rightarrow v' = 1.5v$.

(b) $K_i = \tfrac12(3m)v^2 = 1.5mv^2$; $K_f = \tfrac12(2m)(2.25v^2) = 2.25mv^2$. Released energy $= 0.75mv^2$.

(c) The centre of mass continues on the original ballistic parabola: four seconds later it has advanced by $4v$ horizontally and dropped $\tfrac12 g(4)^2 = 78.5\ \mathrm{m}$. The fragments may fly in different directions, but the mass-weighted mean of their positions is exactly that point — which is what momentum conservation means when gravity is the only external force. **Check:** the released energy is positive, as it must be; the explosion adds kinetic energy but cannot change the total momentum.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — The falling chain.</span> [Return →](02.03.html#p8)

(a) When a length $x$ has landed, the weight supported is $\dfrac{Mgx}{L}$. The landing links stop from speed $v = \sqrt{2gx}$, delivering momentum at rate $\dfrac{dp}{dt} = \dfrac{M}{L}v\cdot v = \dfrac{M}{L}(2gx) = \dfrac{2Mgx}{L}$. The scale reads the sum: $\dfrac{3Mgx}{L}$. The factor 3 is one part weight plus two parts momentum flux.

(b) Once the last link has landed the reading drops to $Mg$ — the dynamic term vanishes abruptly.

(c) A scale measures force, not mass: it reads the weight plus the rate of momentum transfer. **Check:** at $x=L$ the instantaneous reading is $3Mg$, so the needle briefly shows three times the weight before settling at $Mg$ — a result students often disbelieve until they see it measured.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Angular momentum and the skater.</span> [Return →](02.03.html#p9)

(a) No external torque about the vertical axis, so $I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2 \Rightarrow \omega_2 = 2.5\omega_1$.

(b) $K_1 = \tfrac12 I_1\omega_1^2$, $K_2 = \tfrac12(0.4I_1)(6.25\omega_1^2) = 1.25K_1$. The work done is $+0.25K_1$.

(c) Her muscles do that work by pulling mass inward against the centrifugal tendency. The reverse process — arms flying out while spinning faster — would require an energy source and a decrease in entropy; it cannot happen spontaneously, which is why skating spins are easy to speed up and hard to slow. **Check:** angular momentum rises by $0\%$ and kinetic energy by $25\%$, so the energy must come from muscle work — it is not created from nothing.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — A variable-mass rocket.</span> [Return →](02.03.html#p10)

(a) In time $dt$ the rocket of mass $m$ ejects mass $dm$ (with $dm<0$) at speed $u$ relative to the rocket. Momentum conservation in the instantaneous rest frame of the rocket gives $m\,dv = -u\,dm$, so
$$\int_{v_0}^{v_f} dv = -u\int_{m_0}^{m_f}\frac{dm}{m} \Rightarrow \Delta v = u\ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}.$$

(b) With gravity, $m\,dv = -u\,dm - mg\,dt$, so $\Delta v = u\ln(m_0/m_f) - gt$.

(c) $\ln(m_0/m_f) = 7.8/3.0 = 2.6$, so $m_0/m_f = e^{2.6} = 13.5$. Over $90\%$ of the launch mass must be fuel — which is exactly why rockets are staged, and why a single-stage-to-orbit vehicle is so hard to build. **Check:** the ratio grows exponentially with the required $\Delta v/u$, the central engineering fact of spaceflight.
</div>
