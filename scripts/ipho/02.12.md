## 1. Why relativity is needed

In 1865 Maxwell's equations gave the speed of light as $c = 1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_0\mu_0}$, without saying what it is measured *relative to*. Physicists invented the ether as the medium. Then the Michelson–Morley experiment of 1887 looked for the Earth's motion through it and found nothing: the speed of light was the same in every direction, at every season, to within a tiny fraction.

Einstein's 1905 answer was to take that result seriously and give up something else instead — absolute time.

{{FIG:postulates}}

**The two postulates.** (1) The laws of physics are identical in all inertial frames; no experiment can tell you that you are "really moving". (2) The speed of light in vacuum is the same for all inertial observers, independent of the motion of the source or of the observer.

The second postulate contradicts everyday intuition. If you run towards a light beam at $0.5c$, Galileo says the light should approach you at $1.5c$; relativity says it still approaches at $c$. Nothing in the first postulate is surprising; *everything* strange in this topic follows from the second.

## 2. Time dilation

Consider a light clock: a flash bouncing between two mirrors a distance $L$ apart. In the clock's own frame it ticks every $\Delta t_0 = 2L/c$. In a frame where the clock moves at $v$, the flash must travel a longer diagonal path, so the tick takes longer:

$$c^2\left(\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)^2 = L^2 + \left(\frac{v\Delta t}{2}\right)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta t = \frac{\Delta t_0}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}} = \gamma\Delta t_0 .$$

{{FIG:time-dilation}}

The **Lorentz factor** is

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}, \qquad \beta = \frac{v}{c} .$$

{{FIG:lorentz-factor}}

$\gamma = 1$ at rest, $1.005$ at $0.1c$, $1.15$ at $0.5c$, $2$ at $0.866c$, $7.1$ at $0.99c$, and it diverges as $v\to c$. Below about $0.1c$ the effect is under $1\%$ — which is why Newtonian mechanics works so well.

The time $\Delta t_0$ measured by a clock present at both events (the clock's own frame) is called the **proper time**, and it is always the shortest time any observer measures between those two events. Muons created in the upper atmosphere live for $2.2\ \mu\mathrm{s}$, enough to travel only about $650\ \mathrm{m}$, yet they reach the ground from $10\ \mathrm{km}$ up because at $0.998c$ their lifetime is stretched by $\gamma\approx16$.

## 3. Length contraction

The same argument, viewed from the moving frame, gives

$$L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma},$$

where $L_0$ is the **proper length**, measured in the object's rest frame. Only lengths along the direction of motion contract; transverse dimensions are unaffected.

{{FIG:length-contraction}}

Time dilation and length contraction are two descriptions of one phenomenon, and neither is "the real one". The muon's journey is $10\ \mathrm{km}$ long to us and $0.6\ \mathrm{km}$ long to the muon — both descriptions agree that it reaches the ground.

## 4. Simultaneity, the twin paradox and the pole in the barn

Two events that are simultaneous in one frame are generally **not** simultaneous in another. In the train thought-experiment, the light from a flash in the middle reaches the front and back of the carriage at different times for a ground observer while it arrives at both ends together for a passenger. That is not a trick of light travel time: it is what "simultaneous" means once $c$ is absolute.

This resolves the classic paradoxes:

- **Twin paradox.** The travelling twin turns round, so she is not in a single inertial frame; the stay-at-home twin is. The asymmetry is physical, and the travelling twin really does come back younger. The effect is measurable with atomic clocks on aircraft and satellites (GPS would drift by kilometres a day without correction).
- **Pole in the barn.** A long pole moving fast fits inside a short barn as seen from the barn frame, but in the pole frame the barn is contracted and the pole does not fit. The resolution is that the two doors are never closed simultaneously in the pole frame.

## 5. Relativistic momentum and energy

To keep momentum conserved in all frames, momentum must be redefined as

$$p = \gamma m v ,$$

and Newton's second law becomes $F = dp/dt$. The work done in accelerating a mass therefore gives a kinetic energy

$$K = (\gamma - 1)mc^2 ,$$

{{FIG:mass-energy}}

which as $v\ll c$ reduces to $\frac12mv^2$. The total energy is

$$E = \gamma mc^2, \qquad E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2 .$$

The second form is worth memorising: it holds for massless particles too. For a photon, $m=0$ gives $E = pc$, which is why light carries momentum despite having no mass — the basis of solar sails and of Compton scattering.

**Rest energy.** Setting $v=0$ gives $E_0 = mc^2$: mass *is* energy. One gram is $9\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{J}$, about the yield of a small nuclear weapon, and the Sun converts $4\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{kg}$ of mass per second into light. In nuclear reactions the mass difference between reactants and products appears as kinetic energy, and the fractional mass change is tiny but measurable: for the deuterium–tritium fusion, about $0.4\%$.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">The relativity checklist.</span>
Which frame is the proper frame? (the one in which the two events happen at the same place for time, or in which the object is at rest for length) → compute $\gamma$ first, then everything else is multiplication or division → proper time is the shortest, proper length the longest → never add velocities naively (the relativistic addition is $u' = (u-v)/(1-uv/c^2)$) → for energy problems, use $E^2 = (pc)^2+(mc^2)^2$ and remember $K = (\gamma-1)mc^2$ → if the answer involves $v>c$, something has gone wrong in the algebra, not merely in the physics.</div>

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — The Lorentz factor.</span> [Return →](02.12.html#p1)

(a) $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.36} = 1/\sqrt{0.64} = 1.25$.

(b) Clocks run slow by $1.25$ and lengths contract by the same factor: a $1\ \mathrm{m}$ rod measures $0.80\ \mathrm{m}$ along the direction of motion.

(c) $\gamma = 2$ requires $1-v^2/c^2 = 1/4$, so $v^2/c^2 = 3/4$ and $v = 0.866c$. **Check:** this is the value quoted in the text, and it is independent of the object — $\gamma$ depends only on the speed.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Muons from the upper atmosphere.</span> [Return →](02.12.html#p2)

(a) $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.998^2} = 1/\sqrt{0.003996} = 15.8$.

(b) The laboratory lifetime is $15.8\times2.2 = 34.8\ \mu\mathrm{s}$, so the muon travels $0.998(3.0\times10^{8})(34.8\times10^{-6}) = 1.04\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{m}$ — just enough to reach the ground from $10\ \mathrm{km}$.

(c) In the muon frame the atmosphere is moving at $0.998c$ and its thickness is contracted to $10\,000/15.8 = 633\ \mathrm{m}$. The muon lives $2.2\ \mu\mathrm{s}$ and travels $0.998(3.0\times10^{8})(2.2\times10^{-6}) = 659\ \mathrm{m}$ in that frame — again just enough. **Check:** both frames agree that the arrival is marginal, which is why muon flux at sea level is so sensitive to the speed spectrum.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — A trip to a nearby star.</span> [Return →](02.12.html#p3)

(a) $t = d/v = 4.3/0.80 = 5.4$ years.

(b) $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.64} = 1.667$, so the proper time is $t_0 = t/\gamma = 5.4/1.667 = 3.2$ years.

(c) In the spacecraft frame the distance is $L = L_0/\gamma = 4.3/1.667 = 2.6$ light years, and $t_0 = L/v = 2.6/0.80 = 3.2$ years. **Check:** the two descriptions agree exactly, as they must: travelling $2.6$ ly at $0.8c$ takes $3.2$ years.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Relativistic kinetic energy.</span> [Return →](02.12.html#p4)

(a) $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.81} = 1/\sqrt{0.19} = 2.29$, and $mc^2 = (1.67\times10^{-27})(9.0\times10^{16}) = 1.50\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{J}$. So $E = \gamma mc^2 = 3.44\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{J}$.

(b) $K = (\gamma-1)mc^2 = 1.29(1.50\times10^{-10}) = 1.94\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{J} = 1.21\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{MeV}$, i.e. about $1.2\ \mathrm{GeV}$. The classical value $\frac12mv^2 = \frac12(1.67\times10^{-27})(0.81)(9.0\times10^{16}) = 6.1\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{J} = 380\ \mathrm{MeV}$ is less than a third of the truth.

(c) $p = \gamma mv = 2.29(1.67\times10^{-27})(0.90)(3.0\times10^{8}) = 1.03\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$. Then $(pc)^2 = (1.03\times10^{-18})^2(9.0\times10^{16}) = 9.6\times10^{-20}$, $(mc^2)^2 = 2.3\times10^{-20}$, and the sum is $1.19\times10^{-19}$, whose square root is $3.4\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{J}$ — the total energy. **Check:** for a proton, $1.2\ \mathrm{GeV}$ is roughly the rest energy $938\ \mathrm{MeV}$ times $1.3$, so relativistic effects are comparable to the rest energy, as the numbers show.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Mass into energy.</span> [Return →](02.12.html#p5)

(a) The reactor must produce $1.0/0.35 = 2.86\ \mathrm{GW}$ of thermal power, so the mass converted per second is
$$\frac{dm}{dt} = \frac{P}{c^2} = \frac{2.86\times10^{9}}{9.0\times10^{16}} = 3.2\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}.$$

(b) In a year ($3.16\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{s}$) that is about $1.0\ \mathrm{kg}$.

(c) The mass change is a kilogram out of thousands of tonnes of fuel, which is why it was missed for so long; but it releases the enormous energy that makes nuclear power practical, and the same relation explains why the Sun can shine for ten billion years. **Check:** $1\ \mathrm{kg}$ of mass gives $9\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{J}$, and a $1\ \mathrm{GW}$ station produces $3\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{J}$ of electricity per year, so the two agree once efficiency is allowed for.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Velocity addition.</span> [Return →](02.12.html#p6)

(a) $u' = \dfrac{u - v}{1 - uv/c^2} = \dfrac{0.80 - (-0.60)}{1 + 0.48}c = \dfrac{1.40}{1.48}c = 0.946c$. (Taking the probe as moving at $+0.60c$ relative to the spacecraft, which moves at $-0.80c$ relative to Earth in the spacecraft frame.)

(b) The Galilean answer is $1.40c$, which is impossible. The denominator, which comes from the relativity of simultaneity, pulls the sum back below $c$.

(c) For $u,v<c$, write $u = c(1-\epsilon)$, $v = c(1-\delta)$ with $\epsilon,\delta>0$; then $1-uv/c^2 = \epsilon+\delta-\epsilon\delta > 0$ and $u+v < 2c$, so $u' < c$. **Check:** if either speed is exactly $c$, the result is $c$ — the velocity of light is the fixed point of the addition law.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — The twin paradox.</span> [Return →](02.12.html#p7)

(a) The return trip is $18$ light years at $0.90c$: $t = 18/0.90 = 20$ years.

(b) $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.81} = 2.294$, so the travelling twin ages $20/2.294 = 8.7$ years.

(c) The traveller accelerates at the turn-around: she is not in one inertial frame, while the stay-at-home twin is. The elapsed proper time along a world line is $\int\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}\,dt$, so the path with acceleration has the *larger* velocity averaged over the journey and accumulates *less* proper time — the travelling twin is genuinely younger. **Check:** an asymmetric difference of $11$ years cannot be an artefact of a symmetric theory; the asymmetry is in the motion, not in the description.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — A relativistic particle in a magnetic field.</span> [Return →](02.12.html#p8)

(a) $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.9025} = 3.20$, and $p = \gamma m_ev = 3.20(9.11\times10^{-31})(0.95)(3.0\times10^{8}) = 8.3\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$.

(b) $r = \dfrac{p}{qB} = \dfrac{8.3\times10^{-22}}{(1.60\times10^{-19})(1.0)} = 5.2\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m} = 5.2\ \mathrm{mm}$.

(c) The classical radius $m_ev/(qB)$ is $1.6\ \mathrm{mm}$ — a factor of $\gamma = 3.2$ too small. Accelerator designers must use the relativistic momentum, otherwise the magnets would be wrong by a factor of $\gamma$, which at LHC energies is thousands. **Check:** the period is still $2\pi m/(qB)$ times $\gamma$, so the cyclotron frequency falls: that is why synchrotrons ramp the frequency as the particles speed up.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — The lifetime of a particle beam.</span> [Return →](02.12.html#p9)

(a) $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.9801} = 7.09$.

(b) The laboratory lifetime is $7.09\times1.0\times10^{-8} = 7.1\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s}$, so the decay length is $0.99(3.0\times10^{8})(7.1\times10^{-8}) = 21\ \mathrm{m}$.

(c) In the particle frame the laboratory is moving at $0.99c$, so the $21\ \mathrm{m}$ of laboratory is contracted to $21/7.09 = 3.0\ \mathrm{m}$; the particle travels $0.99(3.0\times10^{8})(1.0\times10^{-8}) = 3.0\ \mathrm{m}$ in its own frame — the same length, as required. **Check:** both descriptions agree that the particle just survives $3\ \mathrm{m}$ of its own space, which is why beam pipes are designed around these numbers.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Energy needed to reach a speed.</span> [Return →](02.12.html#p10)

(a) $\gamma = 2.294$, so $K = (\gamma-1)mc^2 = 1.294(1000)(9.0\times10^{16}) = 1.16\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{J}$ — about $1.3$ times the rest energy.

(b) $E = \gamma mc^2 = 2.294(9.0\times10^{19}) = 2.06\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{J}$.

(c) $\gamma$ diverges as $v\to c$, so the energy needed diverges too: no finite amount of work can reach $c$. For scale, $1.16\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{J}$ is the entire world energy consumption for several thousand years, for one tonne of payload and no deceleration — which is the real obstacle to interstellar travel, not the speed limit itself. **Check:** at $0.99c$ the same spacecraft would need $\gamma = 7.09$, i.e. $5.5\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{J}$, six times more for an extra $0.09c$.
</div>
