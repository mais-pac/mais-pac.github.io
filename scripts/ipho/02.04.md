## 1. Newton's law of gravitation, and why the inverse square is special

Two point masses attract with

$$F = \frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}, \qquad G = 6.674\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2\,kg^{-2}} .$$

For a spherically symmetric body, the field outside is exactly that of a point mass at its centre (Newton's shell theorem), which is why planets can be treated as points. Inside a uniform sphere the field falls linearly, $g(r) = g_0\,r/R$ — the reason a tunnel through the Earth gives simple harmonic motion.

The potential energy of a pair is

$$U = -\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r},$$

with the zero at infinite separation. The minus sign is not decoration: it is what allows bound states, and it makes the total energy negative for every closed orbit.

## 2. Kepler's three laws

1. **Orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus.** (For a two-body system, both bodies orbit their common centre of mass.)
2. **Equal areas in equal times.** Equivalently, angular momentum about the central body is conserved.
3. **$T^2 \propto a^3$.** For the Solar System in SI units,
$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}a^3 .$$

The third law is the fastest way to weigh a central body: measure $a$ and $T$ for anything orbiting it and $M$ follows immediately.

## 3. The vis-viva equation: one line for every orbit

Energy conservation for a body of mass $m$ in the field of mass $M\gg m$ gives

$$\tfrac12 mv^2 - \frac{GMm}{r} = -\frac{GMm}{2a},$$

hence the **vis-viva equation**

$$v^2 = GM\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right).$$

From it everything follows:

- Circular orbit ($r = a$): $v_c = \sqrt{GM/r}$.
- Escape ($a\to\infty$, $E = 0$): $v_e = \sqrt{2GM/r} = \sqrt2\,v_c$.
- Ellipse: the speed is largest at periapsis and smallest at apoapsis.
- The period follows from the mean motion: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^3/GM}$.

## 4. The effective potential, and why orbits are stable

Angular momentum conservation reduces the two-body problem to one dimension. Writing $L = m r^2\dot\theta$,

$$E = \tfrac12 m\dot r^2 + \underbrace{\frac{L^2}{2mr^2} - \frac{GMm}{r}}_{U_{\text{eff}}(r)} .$$

The effective potential has a single minimum, at $r_0 = L^2/(GMm^2)$. That minimum *is* the circular orbit. Small radial disturbances about it oscillate — which is why orbits are stable. The same formula explains why the angular-diameter distance and orbit periods behave the way they do in astronomy problems.

{{FIG:effective-potential}}

## 5. Two bodies, the barycentre, and Lagrange points

For two masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ separated by $r$, both orbit the barycentre, which lies at distance

$$r_1 = \frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}r$$

from $m_1$. The relative motion obeys the same Kepler problem with $M = m_1+m_2$ — this is why a binary star's period gives the *total* mass, while the two velocity amplitudes give the ratio.

In a rotating two-body system there are five points where a small body can remain at rest relative to the pair:

- **L1, L2, L3** lie on the line joining the masses; they are unstable saddles.
- **L4, L5** form equilateral triangles with the two masses; they are stable when $m_1/m_2 \gtrsim 25$, which is why Jupiter's Trojan asteroids collect there.

For any orbit question: (1) decide whether the speed, the energy or the period is asked; (2) write vis-viva or energy conservation; (3) use angular momentum for the “equal areas” statements; (4) use $T^2 = 4\pi^2a^3/GM$ whenever a mass is wanted; (5) check the limit $e\to0$ (circle) and $E\to0$ (escape).</div>

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Weighing a planet from its moon.</span> [Return →](02.04.html#p1)

(a) $T^2 = 4\pi^2r^3/GM \Rightarrow M = 4\pi^2r^3/(GT^2) = 4\pi^2(1.728\times10^{24})/(6.674\times10^{-11}\cdot1.6\times10^{11}) = 6.4\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{kg}$.

(b) $v = 2\pi r/T = 2\pi(1.2\times10^{8})/(4.0\times10^{5}) = 1.9\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(c) $\rho = M/\left(\tfrac43\pi R^3\right) = 6.4\times10^{23}/(5.24\times10^{23}) \approx 1.2\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, comparable to a rocky planet rather than a gas giant. **Check:** a circular-orbit speed of $1.9\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ is consistent with a low-mass planet.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Escape speed and the atmosphere.</span> [Return →](02.04.html#p2)

(a) $\tfrac12mv_e^2 = GMm/R \Rightarrow v_e = \sqrt{2GM/R}$.

(b) Earth: $\sqrt{2(6.674\times10^{-11})(5.972\times10^{24})/6.371\times10^{6}} = 1.12\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Moon: $\sqrt{2(6.674\times10^{-11})(7.35\times10^{22})/1.74\times10^{6}} = 2.37\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(c) $v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3kT/m}$: for N₂ ($m=4.65\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}$) at 300 K this is $518\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, about $1/20$ of Earth's escape speed but about $1/5$ of the Moon's. With a Maxwellian tail (and the Moon's day-side temperature higher still), a significant fraction exceeds the escape speed over geological time, so the Moon loses gas. **Check:** the ratio $v_e/v_{\text{rms}}$ is the relevant number, not $v_e$ alone.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Ellipse to circle to escape.</span> [Return →](02.04.html#p3)

(a) Specific energy before: $\varepsilon_0 = -GM/(2r_0)$. After the burn the speed is $\alpha v_c$, so $\varepsilon = \tfrac12\alpha^2(GM/r_0) - GM/r_0 = -\dfrac{GM}{2r_0}(2-\alpha^2)$. Comparing with $\varepsilon = -GM/(2a)$ gives $a = r_0/(2-\alpha^2)$.

(b) Escape needs $\varepsilon = 0$, i.e. $2-\alpha^2 = 0$, so $\alpha = \sqrt2$.

(c) For $\alpha = 1.2$: $a = r_0/(2-1.44) = 1.786r_0$. Since the burn is tangential and the speed increased, the burn point is periapsis: $r_p = r_0$, so $r_a = 2a - r_p = 2.57r_0$. For $\alpha>\sqrt2$ the orbit is hyperbolic; for $\alpha$ slightly less than 1 the burn lowers the periapsis and the orbit dips inside $r_0$ — a de-orbit burn. **Check:** the $\alpha\to1$ limit returns $a = r_0$, the original circle.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Kepler's second law, quantitatively.</span> [Return →](02.04.html#p4)

(a) At periapsis $r_p = a(1-e)$ and $v_p = \sqrt{GM(1+e)/(a(1-e))}$, so $h = r_pv_p = \sqrt{GMa(1-e^2)}$.

(b) Areal velocity is $\tfrac12r^2\dot\theta = \tfrac12 h$, constant — Kepler's second law in one line.

(c) From $h = r_pv_p = r_av_a$ with $r_p = a(1-e)$ and $r_a = a(1+e)$: $\dfrac{v_p}{v_a} = \dfrac{r_a}{r_p} = \dfrac{1+e}{1-e}$. **Check:** for $e=0$ the ratio is 1, a circular orbit; for $e\to1$ it diverges, as a near-parabolic orbit should.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Geostationary orbit and launch cost.</span> [Return →](02.04.html#p5)

(a) $r = \left(GMT^2/4\pi^2\right)^{1/3} = \left(3.986\times10^{14}\cdot(86164)^2/39.48\right)^{1/3} = 4.216\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m}$.

(b) Height $= 4.216\times10^{7} - 6.371\times10^{6} = 3.58\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m} \approx 35\,800\ \mathrm{km}$; speed $2\pi r/T = 3.07\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(c) Specific energy in GEO: $-GM/(2r) = -4.73\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$; at the surface, $-GM/R = -6.26\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$. The difference, $5.79\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$, is the ideal launch energy; in LEO the kinetic energy alone is $\tfrac12 v_c^2 = 3.1\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$. **Check:** the GEO launch needs about twice the LEO kinetic energy, ignoring losses — orbital velocity dominates every launch budget.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Tunnelling through the Earth.</span> [Return →](02.04.html#p6)

(a) Gauss's law for gravity: the mass inside radius $r$ is $M(r) = M r^3/R^3$, so $g = GM(r)/r^2 = g_0r/R$.

(b) Let $x$ be the distance from the tunnel midpoint. The component of gravity along the tunnel is $g_0r/R \cdot (x/r) = g_0x/R$ toward the midpoint, so $\ddot x = -(g_0/R)x$. Hence $\omega = \sqrt{g_0/R}$ and $T = 2\pi\sqrt{R/g_0}$.

(c) $T = 2\pi\sqrt{6.371\times10^{6}/9.81} = 5060\ \mathrm{s} \approx 84\ \mathrm{min}$. The restoring force per unit mass is $-g_0x/R$ regardless of the tunnel's direction, so the period is the same for every chord. **Check:** a satellite skimming the surface has the same period, which is the deep reason both are 84 minutes.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Binaries and the barycentre.</span> [Return →](02.04.html#p7)

(a) $r_M = \dfrac{2M}{3M}(9.0\times10^{10}) = 6.0\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m}$ and $r_{2M} = 3.0\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m}$.

(b) $T = 2\pi\sqrt{d^3/G(3M)}$; with $M$ unspecified the period stays in symbolic form, but the ratio $r_1/r_2 = 2$ is fixed.

(c) $v_1/v_2 = r_1/r_2 = 2$: the lighter star moves twice as fast. A single-lined binary gives only one velocity amplitude, hence $M_2^3\sin^3i/(M_1+M_2)^2$ — one equation with two unknowns, so the total mass needs the mass ratio from a second line or from the light curve. **Check:** for equal masses the barycentre is at the midpoint and both speeds are equal.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — The Roche limit.</span> [Return →](02.04.html#p8)

(a) The tidal acceleration across a body of radius $r$ at distance $d$ is $\sim 2GMR_p r/d^3$, while its self-gravity is $\sim G(4\pi\rho_s r^3/3)/r^2 = \tfrac43\pi G\rho_s r$. Equating them gives $d^3 \sim \dfrac{3}{2\pi}\dfrac{M}{\rho_s}$, and writing $M = \tfrac43\pi\rho_pR_p^3$ gives $d \sim R_p\left(2\rho_p/\rho_s\right)^{1/3}$.

(b) $d \sim 7.15\times10^{7}(2\times1330/600)^{1/3} = 7.15\times10^{7}\times1.64 = 1.17\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m}$, about 1.6 Jupiter radii.

(c) Ring particles inside that distance cannot accrete into a moon; tidal forces keep shattering any body that tries. **Check:** the limit scales as $\rho_s^{-1/3}$, so a denser body survives closer in — icy comets are torn apart further out than rocky asteroids.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Gravitational slingshot.</span> [Return →](02.04.html#p9)

(a) In the planet's frame the encounter is elastic (gravity does no net work over a closed path in that frame, and energy is conserved), so the spacecraft's speed relative to the planet is unchanged; only its direction rotates.

(b) In the planet frame the velocity is $\mathbf u$ with $|\mathbf u| = u$ before and after. Transforming back with $+U$: the final speed along the orbit ranges from $|u - U|$ to $u + U$ depending on the rotation angle, giving the stated bounds with the initial $v = |u + U|$ configuration.

(c) The gain comes from the planet's orbital energy: the planet is slowed by an amount proportional to the spacecraft's mass divided by the planet's mass, which is unmeasurably small but not zero. **Check:** the spacecraft cannot gain energy in the planet's frame, only in the Sun's frame — a distinction that separates a correct answer from a hand-wave.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Hohmann transfer.</span> [Return →](02.04.html#p10)

(a) The transfer orbit touches both circular orbits at its apsides, so $2a = r_1 + r_2$ and $a = (r_1+r_2)/2$. At $r_1$ the required speed is that of the ellipse at periapsis, $v_{p} = \sqrt{GM(2/r_1 - 1/a)}$, compared with the circular speed $\sqrt{GM/r_1}$.

(b) Burn 1: $\Delta v_1 = \sqrt{GM(2/r_1 - 1/a)} - \sqrt{GM/r_1}$. Burn 2 at apoapsis: $\Delta v_2 = \sqrt{GM/r_2} - \sqrt{GM(2/r_2 - 1/a)}$. Substituting $a = (r_1+r_2)/2$ gives the stated expression.

(c) With $r_1 = 6.7\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m}$ and $r_2 = 4.216\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m}$: $a = 2.443\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m}$. Burn 1: circular speed $7.71\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, transfer periapsis speed $10.16\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so $\Delta v_1 = 2.45\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Burn 2: transfer apoapsis speed $1.61\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, circular speed at GEO $3.07\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so $\Delta v_2 = 1.46\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Total $3.91\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Transfer time is half the ellipse period: $t = \pi\sqrt{a^3/GM} = 1.90\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{s} \approx 5.3\ \mathrm{h}$.

The first burn is larger because the spacecraft must *raise* its energy near the planet, where the potential well is deepest; far away, the same energy change is achieved with a smaller speed change. **Check:** both $\Delta v$ are positive for $r_2>r_1$, and both vanish as $r_2\to r_1$.
</div>
