## 1. Where kinematics starts

Kinematics is the vocabulary of motion before any force appears. Everything follows from one idea: position is a vector, and velocity is its rate of change. In one dimension,

$$x(t)\ \longrightarrow\ v=\frac{dx}{dt}\ \longrightarrow\ a=\frac{dv}{dt}=\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}.$$

Working backwards you integrate, and each integration brings one constant that a measurement must fix:

$$v = v_0 + at,\qquad x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac12 at^2.$$

Those two lines are *only* true when $a$ is constant. Olympiad problems constantly test whether you know that. If $a$ varies, you must either integrate properly, or read the motion from a graph, or use the energy relation $v\,dv = a\,dx$ obtained by eliminating time.

## 2. Reading a graph like a physicist

A velocity–time graph is a complete description of one-dimensional motion.

- **Slope** of $v(t)$ is the acceleration.
- **Signed area** between the curve and the time axis is the displacement.
- **Total path length** uses absolute values of the areas — turning points matter.
- An area *below* the axis is negative displacement, but it is still distance travelled.

An acceleration–time graph gives velocity through the *change* in area: you must know $v(0)$ to locate the curve. A position–time graph read backwards gives velocity by tangents; its curvature tells you the sign of the acceleration.

Averaged quantities deserve care. Average velocity is displacement over time, $\bar v = \Delta x/\Delta t$, not the average of the speeds. Only for constant acceleration is it also the midpoint value $\tfrac12(v_0+v)$, which is why that formula appears in every textbook and fails in every other case.

## 3. Projectile motion: two independent one-dimensional problems

With constant gravity and no air resistance, the axes decouple:

$$x = v_0\cos\theta\; t,\qquad y = v_0\sin\theta\; t - \tfrac12 gt^2 .$$

Eliminating $t$ gives a parabola,

$$y = x\tan\theta - \frac{gx^2}{2v_0^2\cos^2\theta}.$$

Three results are worth having ready:

- **Time of flight** over level ground: $T = 2v_0\sin\theta/g$.
- **Range**: $R = v_0^2\sin 2\theta/g$, largest at $45^\circ$, and equal for complementary angles.
- **Maximum height**: $H = v_0^2\sin^2\theta/(2g)$.

The symmetry that produces “complementary angles share a range” is worth noticing: it is a symmetry of the product $\sin\theta\cos\theta$, not of the trajectory shape.

{{FIG:projectile}}

When the target is not at the same height, do not reach for the range formula. Write the trajectory equation, set $y = h$, and solve the quadratic for $x$; keep only the physically meaningful root, and remember that two roots can be a genuine pair of launch angles.

## 4. Relative motion and frame changes

Velocities add as vectors:

$$\mathbf v_{A\text{ rel ground}} = \mathbf v_{A\text{ rel }B} + \mathbf v_{B\text{ rel ground}}.$$

Galilean addition is exact for speeds much less than $c$; relativity replaces it later. In practice you use it as a *choice*: solve the problem in whichever frame makes the motion trivial, then transform the answer back.

{{FIG:relative}}

Two standard traps:

- **Crossing a current.** Minimum crossing time comes from aiming directly across (the current only carries you downstream); arriving directly opposite requires aiming upstream, and if the current is faster than your rowing speed it is impossible.
- **Closing speed.** Two objects approaching each other each at speed $v$ close at $2v$ in the ground frame but at $v$ in each other's frame. The collision outcome depends on relative speed, and that is a physics statement, not a bookkeeping one.

## 5. Choosing coordinates, and the limits of the model

Choose the origin and positive direction once, write it down, and never change it mid-solution. A common mark-losing error is mixing a sign convention halfway.

Three modelling decisions appear in almost every kinematics problem:

1. **Is air resistance negligible?** It is not, for light objects over long falls, or when the question mentions terminal velocity, raindrops or feathers. Including linear drag $f=-kv$ turns constant acceleration into an exponential approach to terminal velocity,
$$v(t)=v_T\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right),\qquad v_T=\frac{mg}{k},\quad \tau=\frac{m}{k}.$$
2. **Is the ground flat?** A range formula on a slope needs the intersection of a parabola and a line.
3. **Is the projectile a point?** Spin, shape and a finite size matter when the object is a ball, a disc or a balloon.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">The method in one paragraph.</span>
Write the coordinate choice. Draw the v–t sketch. Use slope for acceleration and area for displacement. Keep the axes separate for projectiles. Change frame when it simplifies the question. Check the answer in a limit — $g\to0$, $\theta\to0$, $v_0\to\infty$ — before you believe it.</div>

<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — From a v–t graph to real motion.</span> [Return →](02.01.html#p1)

(a) Stage 1 area $4.0\times2.0 = +8.0\ \mathrm{m}$. Stage 2 is a trapezium with average velocity $(4.0 + (-2.0))/2 = 1.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ over $3.0\ \mathrm{s}$: $+3.0\ \mathrm{m}$. Displacement $= +11.0\ \mathrm{m}$.

(b) In stage 2 the velocity crosses zero after travelling $4.0/(6.0/3.0)=2.0\ \mathrm{s}$, so the forward part has area $+\tfrac12(4.0)(2.0)=+4.0\ \mathrm{m}$ and the reverse part $-\tfrac12(2.0)(1.0) = -1.0\ \mathrm{m}$. Total distance $= 8.0 + 4.0 + 1.0 = 13.0\ \mathrm{m} \neq 11.0\ \mathrm{m}$ in magnitude of displacement. **Check:** distance ≥ |displacement|, as required.

(c) $a = (-2.0-4.0)/3.0 = -2.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. The turn-around occurs when $v=0$, at $t = 2.0 + 4.0/2.0 = 4.0\ \mathrm{s}$ after the start.

<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Two stones, one clock.</span> [Return →](02.01.html#p2)

(a) Let $t$ be measured from the release of the first stone. $v_1 = gt$; the second has $v_2 = 12 + g(t-1)$ for $t \ge 1$. Setting them equal: $gt = 12 + g(t-1) \Rightarrow 0 = 12 - g$, which is false. The second stone is *always* faster by $12 - g = 2.2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ while both are in flight — a relative-motion statement that needs no algebra once seen.

(b) The speed difference is constant, so there is no instant of equal speed; the question is a trap and the correct answer is that they never share a speed while both fall freely.

(c) The second stone starts $1.0\ \mathrm{s}$ later and $12\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ faster, so its displacement always differs from the first by the initial gap $\tfrac12 g(1.0)^2$ plus the accumulated lead $2.2t$; both terms are positive, so positions never coincide. **Check:** the limiting case $12 = g$ gives equal accelerations and equal speeds only if also equal start times — consistent.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Range on level ground and on a wall.</span> [Return →](02.01.html#p3)

(a) $R_{\max} = v_0^2/g = 400/9.8 = 40.8\ \mathrm{m}$ at $45^\circ$.

(b) $25 = \dfrac{400\sin 2\theta}{9.8} \Rightarrow \sin 2\theta = 0.6125 \Rightarrow 2\theta = 37.8^\circ$ or $142.2^\circ$, so $\theta \approx 18.9^\circ$ or $71.1^\circ$ — complementary, as expected.

(c) Trajectory: $y = x\tan\theta - \dfrac{9.8x^2}{2(400)\cos^2\theta}$. At $x = 20\ \mathrm{m}$: $6.0 < 20\tan\theta - 4.9\cos^{-2}\theta$. Writing $c=\cos^2\theta$ and $\tan\theta = \sqrt{(1-c)/c}$ and solving numerically gives $32^\circ \lesssim \theta \lesssim 70^\circ$. **Check:** $\theta = 45^\circ$ gives $y = 20 - 4.9 = 15.1\ \mathrm{m}$, comfortably above $6\ \mathrm{m}$, while $\theta = 10^\circ$ gives only about $3.5\ \mathrm{m}$ — the window is sensible.

<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — The river crossing.</span> [Return →](02.01.html#p4)

(a) Aim upstream at angle $\theta$ to the bank with $1.6\sin\theta = 1.2 \Rightarrow \theta = 48.6^\circ$. Cross-stream speed $1.6\cos\theta = 1.06\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, so $t = 80/1.06 = 75\ \mathrm{s}$.

(b) Aim straight across: cross-stream speed $1.6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $t = 50\ \mathrm{s}$, drifting $1.2\times50 = 60\ \mathrm{m}$ downstream.

(c) A straight crossing needs $v_{\text{row}}\sin\theta = u$ with $\sin\theta\le1$, impossible when $u > v_{\text{row}}$. At $u = v_{\text{row}}$ the only solution is $\theta = 90^\circ$, i.e. aiming straight upstream with zero cross-stream progress — the crossing takes infinite time. **Check:** the fast-crossing time $50\ \mathrm{s}$ is shorter than the straight-crossing time $75\ \mathrm{s}$, which is why ferry timetables care about the current.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Projectile over a wall.</span> [Return →](02.01.html#p5)

(a) The height at $x=d$ is $y(d) = d\tan\theta - \dfrac{gd^2}{2v_0^2\cos^2\theta}$. Requiring $y(d) \ge H$ gives
$$v_0^2 \ge \frac{gd^2}{2\cos^2\theta\,(d\tan\theta - H)}.$$

(b) Minimising the required $v_0^2$ over $\theta$ (differentiate the denominator) gives $\tan\theta = \dfrac{d}{d - 2H}$, the *envelope* result: the optimal launch aims above the target, because the extra height is bought cheaply.

(c) The standard maximum-range-envelope condition is $v_0^2 \ge g\left(y+\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\right)$; the square-root term is the straight-line distance from the launch point to the target, so the condition says the *reachable* boundary is an upward-opening paraboloid of revolution. **Check:** for $y=0$ it reduces to $v_0^2 \ge gx$, i.e. $x \le v_0^2/g = R_{\max}$, which is exactly right.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Reading an acceleration graph.</span> [Return →](02.01.html#p6)

(a) $a(t)$ is three horizontal segments: $+2$, $0$, $-3$. $v(t)$ rises linearly to a peak, stays flat, then falls linearly to zero — a trapezium. $x(t)$ has increasing positive slope, then constant slope, then decreasing slope flattening out — no inflection is lost, but its curvature changes sign.

(b) $v_{\max} = 2.0\times3.0 = 6.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ at $t = 3.0\ \mathrm{s}$, held until $t = 5.0\ \mathrm{s}$.

(c) The final stage lasts $6.0/3.0 = 2.0\ \mathrm{s}$, so the total is $7.0\ \mathrm{s}$. Position: $\tfrac12(2)(9) = 9.0\ \mathrm{m}$ in stage 1, $6.0\times2.0 = 12.0\ \mathrm{m}$ in stage 2, and $\tfrac12(6.0)(2.0) = 6.0\ \mathrm{m}$ in stage 3: $x_{\text{final}} = 27.0\ \mathrm{m}$. **Check:** total area under $v(t)$ equals the sum of the three areas, and the object never reverses, so displacement equals distance.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Drag changes everything.</span> [Return →](02.01.html#p7)

(a) Terminal speed is reached when drag balances weight: $mg = kv_T \Rightarrow v_T = mg/k$. The equation $m\dot v = mg - kv$ can be written $\dot v = (v_T - v)/\tau$ with $\tau = m/k$.

(b) Solving with $v(0)=0$ gives $v = v_T(1-e^{-t/\tau})$, and integrating, $y = v_Tt - v_T\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})$.

(c) At $t = 3\tau$, $e^{-3} = 0.0498$, so $y = v_T\tau(3 - 0.950) = 2.05\,v_T\tau$ while $v_Tt = 3v_T\tau$: only $68\%$ of the drag-free distance. **Check:** as $t\to\infty$, $y\to v_T t - v_T\tau$, i.e. the drop lags the terminal-speed line by a constant $v_T\tau$ — the classic drag “offset”.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Two-dimensional chase.</span> [Return →](02.01.html#p8)

(a) The relative position is $(3t, 40 - 5t)$, so $D(t)=\sqrt{9t^2 + (40-5t)^2}$.

(b) Minimise $D^2 = 34t^2 - 400t + 1600$: $t = 400/68 = 5.88\ \mathrm{s}$, and $D_{\min} = \sqrt{1600 - 400^2/(4\cdot34)} = \sqrt{1600-1176} = 20.6\ \mathrm{m}$.

(c) At $t = 5.88\ \mathrm{s}$ the second boat is at $(0, 40 - 29.4) = (0, 10.6)\ \mathrm{m}$. A boat leaving the origin must therefore have velocity $(0, 10.6/5.88) = (0, 1.80)\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. **Check:** the answer follows from the relative-velocity picture — the minimum-separation geometry is a right angle between the relative velocity and the line of sight.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Throwing from a cliff.</span> [Return →](02.01.html#p9)

(a) $-h = v_0\sin\theta\, t - \tfrac12 gt^2$, so $\tfrac12gt^2 - v_0\sin\theta\,t - h = 0$ and
$$t = \frac{v_0\sin\theta + \sqrt{v_0^2\sin^2\theta + 2gh}}{g}.$$
The negative root would place the launch in the past, so it is discarded on physical grounds.

(b) Substituting into $R = v_0\cos\theta\,t$ gives the stated expression.

(c) For large $h$ the square root is dominated by $\sqrt{2gh}$, independent of $\theta$, so $R \approx v_0\cos\theta\sqrt{2h/g}$, which is greatest at $\theta\to0$: from a very high cliff you should throw horizontally and let gravity do the work. **Check:** for $h=0$ the formula collapses to $R = v_0^2\sin2\theta/g$, as it must.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — A rotating reference frame.</span> [Return →](02.01.html#p10)

(a) In the rotating frame the radial acceleration contains the centrifugal term $+\omega^2r$ and the Coriolis term $2\omega\dot\theta$; because the groove constrains $\dot\theta = 0$ for the bead in the groove, only the centrifugal term survives.

(b) Therefore $\ddot r = \omega^2 r$, with solution $r = A\cosh\omega t + B\sinh\omega t$. With $r(0)=r_0$ and $\dot r(0)=0$, $r = r_0\cosh\omega t$.

(c) In the inertial frame the bead has tangential speed $\omega r_0$ and, with no tangential force, moves in a straight line; the groove curves away from it and must push the bead inward, so the required sideways force grows as $\omega^2 r$. The “centrifugal force” is the bookkeeping of that constraint.

(d) For $\omega t \ll 1$, $\cosh \omega t \approx 1 + \tfrac12\omega^2t^2$, so $\ddot r \approx 0$: over short times the bead barely notices the rotation, in agreement with the inertial-frame picture. **Check:** energy in the inertial frame, $\tfrac12m\omega^2r^2$, is conserved; in the rotating frame the same expression appears as the centrifugal potential.
</div>
