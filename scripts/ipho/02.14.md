## 1. The atom is mostly empty space

The discovery of the electron in 1897 left a puzzle: if negative electrons are inside a neutral atom, where is the positive charge? Thomson proposed a diffuse "plum pudding"; Rutherford's students fired alpha particles at gold foil and found that about one in eight thousand came straight back. A diffuse charge could never do that.

{{FIG:rutherford}}

Rutherford's conclusion is the nuclear model: a tiny, dense, positively charged **nucleus** of radius about $10^{-15}\ \mathrm{m}$ holds almost all the mass, and the electrons occupy a volume about $10^5$ times larger. The atom is $\sim10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m}$ across and is essentially empty.

| particle | symbol | charge | mass |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| proton | $^{1}_{1}\mathrm{p}$ | $+e$ | $1.673\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$ |
| neutron | $^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n}$ | $0$ | $1.675\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$ |
| electron | $^{0}_{-1}\mathrm{e}$ | $-e$ | $9.11\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$ |

A nuclide is written $^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}$: $Z$ protons define the element, $A-Z$ neutrons set the isotope, and $A$ is the nucleon number. **Isotopes** have the same $Z$ but different $A$ — chemically identical, but with different nuclear masses and stability.

## 2. Inside the nucleon

Protons and neutrons are not elementary. They contain three **quarks**: up ($+2/3\,e$) and down ($-1/3\,e$).

{{FIG:quarks}}

$$\text{proton} = uud: \quad \tfrac23+\tfrac23-\tfrac13 = +1, \qquad \text{neutron} = udd: \quad \tfrac23-\tfrac13-\tfrac13 = 0 .$$

The quarks are held by **gluons** through the strong interaction, and the same force, leaking out of the nucleons, binds nuclei together. Quarks never appear in isolation: pulling them apart creates new quark–antiquark pairs instead, which is why the only free particles we see are colour-neutral combinations.

## 3. Mass defect and binding energy

The mass of a nucleus is **less** than the sum of the masses of its separate nucleons. The difference, the **mass defect** $\Delta m$, is the energy that was released when the nucleus formed:

$$E_b = \Delta m\,c^2 = \left[Zm_p + (A-Z)m_n - m_{\text{nucleus}}\right]c^2 .$$

It is convenient to measure masses in atomic mass units, $1\ \mathrm{u} = 931.5\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$, so that the energy in MeV is just the mass defect in $\mathrm{u}$ times $931.5$.

The quantity that decides nuclear stability is the **binding energy per nucleon**, $E_b/A$.

{{FIG:binding-energy}}

- It rises steeply for the lightest nuclei, peaks at about $8.8\ \mathrm{MeV}$ near iron-56, and then falls slowly to about $7.6\ \mathrm{MeV}$ for uranium.
- **Fusion** of light nuclei moves products up the curve and releases energy — this powers stars.
- **Fission** of heavy nuclei moves fragments up the curve and also releases energy — this powers reactors.

{{FIG:fission-fusion}}

## 4. Radioactivity

An unstable nucleus decays spontaneously. Three modes matter:

- **Alpha decay:** $^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X} \to {}^{A-4}_{Z-2}\mathrm{Y} + {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$. The alpha is heavy and charged, so alpha radiation is easily stopped (by paper or a few centimetres of air) but does great damage if inhaled.
- **Beta minus decay:** $^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X} \to {}^{A}_{Z+1}\mathrm{Y} + e^- + \bar\nu_e$. A neutron turns into a proton; the antineutrino carries away the missing energy, which is why the beta spectrum is continuous.
- **Gamma emission:** an excited nucleus drops to a lower state, emitting a photon of MeV energy. $Z$ and $A$ are unchanged.

{{FIG:decay-chain}}

Every decay conserves charge, nucleon number, energy and momentum, and the rules for the first two make nuclear equations easy to balance.

## 5. The exponential law

Decay is a *random* process with a constant probability per unit time, so the number of nuclei obeys

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad A = \lambda N = A_0e^{-\lambda t} .$$

{{FIG:decay-curve}}

Three related constants, often confused:

$$\text{decay constant } \lambda, \qquad \text{half-life } T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln2}{\lambda}, \qquad \text{mean lifetime } \tau = \frac{1}{\lambda} .$$

Note $\tau = T_{1/2}/\ln 2 = 1.44\,T_{1/2}$. The **activity** $A$ is measured in becquerels ($1\ \mathrm{Bq} = 1$ decay per second); the older unit curie is $3.7\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{Bq}$.

Because $\lambda$ is constant, a sample never "runs out" at a definite time: the last few nuclei may survive for many half-lives, and the decay of a small sample looks visibly jagged.

## 6. Fission, fusion and radiation dose

Fission of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ releases about $200\ \mathrm{MeV}$ per event, fusion of deuterium and tritium about $17.6\ \mathrm{MeV}$ — but per kilogram, fusion releases several times more. Both are $10^6$ times more energetic than chemical burning, because nuclear binding energies are of order MeV per nucleon while chemical energies are of order eV per atom.

Radiation dose is energy deposited per kilogram: the gray ($1\ \mathrm{Gy} = 1\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$) measures absorbed dose, and the sievert weights it by the biological damage of the radiation type. Natural background is about $2$–$3\ \mathrm{mSv}$ per year; a chest X-ray is about $0.1\ \mathrm{mSv}$; $1\ \mathrm{Sv}$ in a short time causes acute illness.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">The nuclear checklist.</span>
Balance $Z$ and $A$ before anything else — most "hard" nuclear equations become bookkeeping → convert mass to energy with $931.5\ \mathrm{MeV}$ per u (or $c^2 = 9\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$) → for stability questions, ask whether $E_b/A$ increases → for decay, use $N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}$ with $T_{1/2} = \ln2/\lambda$ and never confuse half-life with mean lifetime → remember that the activity depends on both $\lambda$ and the number of nuclei present.</div>

(a) $Z = 92$ protons, $A - Z = 235 - 92 = 143$ neutrons, and $A = 235$ nucleons.

(b) $m = 235.04\ \mathrm{u}\times1.661\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg\,u^{-1}} = 3.90\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg}$.

Uranium-235 and uranium-238 are isotopes, with half-lives of $7\times10^{8}$ years and $4.5\times10^{9}$ years respectively. **Check:** $235\times931.5\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$ gives a rest energy of about $219\ \mathrm{GeV}$, consistent with $mc^2 = (3.90\times10^{-25})(9\times10^{16}) = 3.5\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{J}$.

<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Balancing nuclear equations.</span> [Return →](02.14.html#p2)

(a) $A$: $238 - 234 = 4$, $Z$: $92 - 90 = 2$, so the product is $^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$ — **alpha decay**.

(b) $A$ unchanged at $14$, $Z$ increases by $1$ and charge must balance, so the product is $e^- + \bar\nu_e$ — **beta minus decay** (an electron and an antineutrino).

(c) $A$: $2+3-4 = 1$, $Z$: $1+1-2 = 0$, so the product is a neutron, $^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n}$ — **fusion**, releasing $17.6\ \mathrm{MeV}$. **Check:** in each case the totals of $A$ and $Z$ on both sides match, which is the only test needed.

<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Mass defect of helium-4.</span> [Return →](02.14.html#p3)

(a) $\Delta m = 2(1.00728) + 2(1.00866) - 4.0015 = 4.03188 - 4.0015 = 0.03038\ \mathrm{u}$.

(b) $E_b = 0.03038\times931.5 = 28.3\ \mathrm{MeV}$, so $E_b/A = 7.08\ \mathrm{MeV}$ per nucleon — unusually high for such a light nucleus.

(c) The alpha particle has a closed shell of two protons and two neutrons, which is a particularly stable configuration. That is why heavy nuclei emit alphas rather than loose nucleons: the alpha carries away a large amount of energy for a small mass, and the daughter nucleus moves closer to stability. **Check:** the mass defect is about $0.75\%$ of the total mass, comparable with the $0.4\%$ figure quoted for fusion.

<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Carbon dating.</span> [Return →](02.14.html#p5)

(a) Activity is proportional to the number of $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ nuclei, so
$$\frac{A}{A_0} = e^{-\lambda t} \Rightarrow t = \frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\frac{A_0}{A} = \frac{5730}{0.693}\ln\frac{2.3}{1.5} = 8267\times0.427 = 3.5\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{years}.$$

(b) The method assumes that the $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ concentration in the atmosphere has been constant (it varies with solar activity and with burning of fossil fuel), that the sample has not exchanged carbon with its surroundings since death, and that no contamination has occurred.

(c) After $50\,000$ years (about $8.7$ half-lives) only $0.2\%$ of the original $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ remains, so the activity is close to background and the uncertainty becomes enormous. **Check:** at $3500$ years the fraction remaining is $e^{-0.693\times3500/5730} = 0.65$, and $1.5/2.3 = 0.65$, as it must.

<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Alpha particle energy.</span> [Return →](02.14.html#p7)

(a) $K = 5.5\ \mathrm{MeV} = 8.8\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{J}$ with $m_\alpha = 6.64\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$: $v = \sqrt{2K/m} = \sqrt{2(8.8\times10^{-13})/(6.64\times10^{-27})} = 1.6\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, about $5\%$ of $c$.

(b) Momentum conservation: $p_\alpha = m_\alpha v = 1.08\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$, so the recoiling nucleus has $v = p/M = 1.08\times10^{-19}/(220\times1.66\times10^{-27}) = 2.96\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(c) The alpha is heavy and doubly charged, so it ionises matter very densely and loses energy within a few centimetres of air or a sheet of paper. A gamma ray is uncharged and interacts only by occasional collisions, so it penetrates deeply — which is also why it is the most penetrating hazard. **Check:** the recoil kinetic energy, about $0.1\ \mathrm{MeV}$, is a small fraction of the alpha energy, as momentum conservation with a heavy daughter requires.

<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — The binding energy curve.</span> [Return →](02.14.html#p8)

(a) $E \approx A\left[(B/A)_{\text{fragments}} - (B/A)_{\text{parent}}\right] = 238(8.5 - 7.6) = 2.1\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{MeV}$.

(b) This is very close to the observed $200\ \mathrm{MeV}$: the estimate only uses the average binding energy per nucleon, and the agreement to about $5\%$ shows how much of nuclear physics follows from the curve alone.

(c) Per kilogram, fusion wins because the fractional mass change is larger: hydrogen converts about $0.7\%$ of its mass in the pp chain, whereas fission converts about $0.09\%$. <span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — A sealed source.</span> [Return →](02.14.html#p9)

(a) $N_0 = \dfrac{2.0\times10^{-3}}{60}(6.02\times10^{23}) = 2.0\times10^{19}$ atoms.

(b) $\lambda = \dfrac{0.693}{5.3\times3.16\times10^{7}} = 4.1\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so

(c) $20$ years is $3.77$ half-lives, so $A = A_0\,2^{-3.77} = 6.1\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{Bq}$. The nuclei remaining are $N = N_0\,2^{-3.77} = 1.5\times10^{18}$, so the number of decays is $N_0 - N = 1.85\times10^{19}$. **Check:** the total energy released is then about $1.85\times10^{19}\times\text{(a few MeV)} \approx 10\ \mathrm{GJ}$, and the *power* starts at $8.3\times10^{10}$ decays per second — a useful reminder that a source with a long half-life can still be intensely radioactive.

<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — A nuclear reactor.</span> [Return →](02.14.html#p10)

(a) $1.0\ \mathrm{GW} = 1.0\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}$ of thermal power, and each fission gives $3.2\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{J}$, so
$$\text{rate} = \frac{1.0\times10^{9}}{3.2\times10^{-11}} = 3.1\times10^{19}\ \text{fissions per second}.$$
Per day the mass consumed is $\dfrac{(3.1\times10^{19})(86\,400)}{6.02\times10^{23}}\times235\ \mathrm{g} = 1.05\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{g} \approx 1.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$.

(b) $P_{\text{electrical}} = 0.33\times1.0\ \mathrm{GW} = 3.3\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{W}$, so $330\ \mathrm{MW}$.

(c) Natural uranium is only $0.7\%$ $^{235}\mathrm{U}$, and a reactor needs a higher proportion to sustain a chain reaction with ordinary (light) water, so the fuel is enriched. The moderator slows the fast neutrons produced in fission to thermal energies, where the fission cross-section of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ is far larger — water, heavy water and graphite are the usual moderators. **Check:** $1\ \mathrm{kg}$ of $^{235}\mathrm{U}$ is about $8\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{J}$, so a $1\ \mathrm{GW}$ reactor burning a kilogram a day produces $8\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{J}$ per day, i.e. $9.5\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}$ — consistent with the thermal power.
