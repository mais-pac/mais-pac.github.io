## 1. Why uncertainties are not optional

A measurement without an uncertainty cannot be compared with a theory, with another measurement, or with a specification. "The resistance is $10.2\ \Omega$" says nothing; "the resistance is $(10.2 \pm 0.1)\ \Omega$" says that a claim of $10.5\ \Omega$ is excluded and a claim of $10.25\ \Omega$ is not.

Two things must be distinguished at all times:

- The **uncertainty** of a single reading (resolution, reading error, calibration).
- The **spread** of a set of repeated readings, which tells you how precise the *procedure* is.

## 2. The statistics of repeats

If a quantity is measured $N$ times with readings $x_i$, the best estimate is the mean

$$\bar x = \frac1N\sum x_i,$$

and the spread is described by the standard deviation

$$s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x_i-\bar x)^2}{N-1}} .$$

{{FIG:histogram}}

The uncertainty of the **mean** is smaller than the spread of individual readings:

$$\sigma_{\bar x} = \frac{s}{\sqrt N} .$$

That is the $1/\sqrt N$ law: quadrupling the number of readings only halves the uncertainty. It is worth knowing because it tells you when to stop repeating and improve the apparatus instead.

For a Gaussian distribution, $68\%$ of readings lie within $\pm\sigma$ of the mean, $95\%$ within $\pm2\sigma$, and $99.7\%$ within $\pm3\sigma$. In an exam, an uncertainty quoted as $\pm\Delta$ is usually intended as a rough $1\sigma$ figure.

## 3. Propagation in one line

For $f(x,y,\dots)$ with independent uncertainties, the honest combined uncertainty is

$$\Delta f = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Delta x\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\Delta y\right)^2},$$

which reduces to three memorisable rules:

- Sums and differences: **absolute** uncertainties add in quadrature.
- Products and quotients: **fractional** uncertainties add in quadrature.
- Powers: fractional uncertainty multiplied by the exponent, $\Delta f/f = |n|\,\Delta x/x$.

{{FIG:propagation}}

{{FIG:combine}}

{{LAB:uncertainty}}

A worked example makes the pattern clear. For the density of a cylinder, $\rho = 4m/(\pi d^2 h)$, the fractional uncertainty is

$$\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\Delta m}{m}\right)^2 + \left(2\frac{\Delta d}{d}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\Delta h}{h}\right)^2},$$

so a $1\%$ error in the diameter contributes $2\%$ — the single most common reason a density experiment misses its target.

## 4. Absolute versus relative

{{FIG:absolute-relative}}

The same instrument gives the same absolute uncertainty whatever the size of the object, so the *relative* uncertainty grows as the object shrinks. That single fact decides which instrument to use: a micrometer on a $2\ \mathrm{mm}$ wire gives $0.5\%$, the same micrometer on a $2\ \mathrm{cm}$ rod gives $0.05\%$, and a ruler on the same wire would give $50\%$.

## 5. Graphs, gradients and intercepts

The most powerful analysis technique in the practical exam is linearisation: arrange the theory into the form $y = mx + c$, plot, and read the gradient and intercept.

{{FIG:best-fit}}

- Draw the line of best fit by eye through the scatter, not by joining the first and last points.
- Estimate the **uncertainty in the gradient** by drawing the steepest and shallowest lines that are still consistent with the error bars; half the difference is the uncertainty.
- An intercept that theory says should be zero but is not is a **systematic error** in disguise — a zero error, a background, or an effect you forgot.

Examples worth learning: $T^2$ against $L$ for a pendulum (gradient $4\pi^2/g$), $1/v$ against $1/u$ for a lens (intercept $-1/f$), $\ln I$ against $x$ for absorption (gradient $-\mu$), $V$ against $I$ for a cell (intercept the emf, gradient $-r$).

## 6. Reporting a result

The conventions that earn marks:

- Round the uncertainty to **one significant figure** (two if the first digit is 1).
- Match the value to the same decimal place: $(9.81 \pm 0.02)\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, not $(9.8123 \pm 0.02)$.
- Include units on both, and state what the uncertainty means ($1\sigma$, resolution, or range).
- For a discrepancy, quote it in units of the combined uncertainty: $|x_1-x_2|/\sqrt{\Delta x_1^2+\Delta x_2^2}$. A ratio below about 2 is consistent; a ratio above 3 needs explaining.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">The uncertainty checklist.</span>
Write down every measured quantity with its uncertainty before doing any algebra → convert to fractional uncertainties → combine with the rules (sums add absolute, products add fractional, powers scale fractional) → propagate into the final quantity, including any squared or cubed variables → round to one significant figure in the uncertainty and match the value → compare your result with the expected value in units of the uncertainty and comment if the difference is large.</div>

<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Mean and standard deviation.</span> [Return →](03.02.html#p1)

(a) $\bar x = (12.4+12.6+12.5+12.3+12.7)/5 = 12.50\ \mathrm{mm}$.

(b) Deviations: $-0.1$, $+0.1$, $0$, $-0.2$, $+0.2$. Sum of squares $= 0.01+0.01+0+0.04+0.04 = 0.10$, so
$$s = \sqrt{0.10/4} = 0.16\ \mathrm{mm}.$$

(c) $\sigma_{\bar x} = 0.16/\sqrt5 = 0.07\ \mathrm{mm}$, so the result is $(12.50 \pm 0.07)\ \mathrm{mm}$. **Check:** the readings scatter by about $\pm0.2\ \mathrm{mm}$, and the mean is more reliable than any single reading — exactly what $1/\sqrt N$ predicts.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Simple propagation.</span> [Return →](03.02.html#p2)

(a) $P = 2(a+b) = 20.0\ \mathrm{cm}$; the uncertainties add, $2(0.1+0.2) = 0.6\ \mathrm{cm}$, or $2\sqrt{0.1^2+0.2^2} = 0.45\ \mathrm{cm}$ in quadrature: $(20.0 \pm 0.5)\ \mathrm{cm}$.

(b) $A = ab = 24.0\ \mathrm{cm^2}$. Fractional: $0.1/4.0 = 2.5\%$ and $0.2/6.0 = 3.3\%$, so $\Delta A/A = \sqrt{2.5^2+3.3^2} = 4.2\%$, giving $(24.0 \pm 1.0)\ \mathrm{cm^2}$.

(c) The area multiplies two quantities, so their relative uncertainties add; the perimeter adds lengths, so the *absolute* uncertainties add, but the perimeter is five times larger, making its relative uncertainty small. **Check:** $4.1\%$ of $24$ is about $1\ \mathrm{cm^2}$, consistent with the worst-case value $0.1\times6 + 0.2\times4 = 1.4\ \mathrm{cm^2}$ being a little larger.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — A density measurement.</span> [Return →](03.02.html#p3)

(a) $V = \pi d^2h/4 = \pi(2.00)^2(3.00)/4 = 9.42\ \mathrm{cm^3}$, so $\rho = 84.2/9.42 = 8.94\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.

(b) Fractional uncertainties: mass $0.1/84.2 = 0.12\%$, diameter $0.1/20.0 = 0.50\%$ (doubled to $1.0\%$), length $0.2/30.0 = 0.67\%$. Total $\sqrt{0.12^2+1.0^2+0.67^2} = 1.2\%$, so $\rho = (8.94 \pm 0.11)\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.

(c) The diameter and the length contribute about equally, and the mass hardly at all. Improving the diameter measurement (a micrometer instead of a vernier) would cut its contribution to $0.1\%$, leaving the length as the limit. **Check:** the value is close to copper ($8.96\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$), which is a useful sanity check.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Timing and the pendulum.</span> [Return →](03.02.html#p4)

(a) $T = 100.4/50 = 2.008\ \mathrm{s}$, with uncertainty $0.2/50 = 0.004\ \mathrm{s}$: $(2.008 \pm 0.004)\ \mathrm{s}$.

(b) $g = 4\pi^2L/T^2 = 4\pi^2(1.000)/(2.008)^2 = 9.79\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. Fractional: $2(0.004/2.008) = 0.20\%$ from $T$ and $0.2\%$ from $L$, so $\Delta g/g = 0.28\%$ and $g = (9.79 \pm 0.03)\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

(c) The accepted value $9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ differs by $0.02$, well inside the $0.03$ uncertainty — the results agree. **Check:** the measured value is slightly low, and in a real experiment this is often because the pendulum is a physical pendulum (extended bob) or because the amplitude was not small.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Gradients from a graph.</span> [Return →](03.02.html#p5)

(a) $g = 4\pi^2/m = 4\pi^2/4.05 = 9.74\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. The uncertainty in the gradient is $(4.11-3.99)/2 = 0.06$, so $\Delta g/g = 0.06/4.05 = 1.5\%$ and $g = (9.74 \pm 0.15)\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

(b) The best-fit line gives an intercept of $+0.02\ \mathrm{s^2}$ on the $T^2$ axis.

(c) Theory says the intercept should be zero, so a positive intercept of $0.02\ \mathrm{s^2}$ is about $1\%$ of the $T^2$ values — probably a small systematic effect, such as a reaction-time delay in starting and stopping the watch, or an error in measuring $L$ from the wrong point. **Check:** an intercept in $T^2$ corresponds to a constant delay, which is exactly what a stopwatch reaction time produces.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Combining independent uncertainties.</span> [Return →](03.02.html#p6)

(a) $f = 10.0\times20.0\times5.0 = 1000$. Fractional: $5\%$, $1\%$, $10\%$; combined $\sqrt{25+1+100} = 11.2\%$, so $f = (1000 \pm 110)$.

(b) $h = 10.0+20.0-5.0 = 25.0$ with $\Delta h = \sqrt{0.5^2+0.2^2+0.5^2} = 0.73$, so $h = (25.0 \pm 0.7)$ — a relative uncertainty of $2.9\%$.

(c) In $h$ the absolute uncertainties add but the value is a sum of the magnitudes, so the *relative* uncertainty is diluted; in $f$ the relative uncertainties add, which is much more punishing. **Check:** $110/1000 = 11\%$ against $0.7/25 = 2.9\%$, as stated.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Worst case versus quadrature.</span> [Return →](03.02.html#p7)

(a) Worst case: $2\% + 2\% = 4\%$.

(b) Quadrature: $\sqrt{2^2+2^2} = 2.8\%$.

(c) Worst case assumes both errors conspire in the same direction, which is unlikely if they are independent random errors; quadrature is the expected value for independent random contributions. Examiners usually accept either, provided the reasoning is stated, but the quadrature result is the standard for random errors, and worst case is used for tolerances that could genuinely add (manufacturing limits, for instance). **Check:** quoting a smaller uncertainty than justified is a serious error; quoting a larger one is merely conservative.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — The lens experiment.</span> [Return →](03.02.html#p8)

(a) The intercept equals $1/f$, so $f = 1/0.0500 = 20.0\ \mathrm{cm}$, with $\Delta f/f = 0.0010/0.0500 = 2\%$: $f = (20.0 \pm 0.4)\ \mathrm{cm}$.

(b) The gradient is $-0.98 \pm 0.03$, so $-1$ lies within about $0.7$ of the uncertainty — consistent with the theory, though slightly shallow.

(c) A gradient shallower than $-1$ suggests that the measured object and image distances are not exactly the distances from the lens: distances measured from the lens holder's centre, or a lens of non-negligible thickness, both produce such an offset. **Check:** the intercept method is more robust than measuring $f$ directly, because it uses all the data points at once.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — A resistivity measurement.</span> [Return →](03.02.html#p9)

(a) $A = \pi(0.25\times10^{-3})^2 = 1.96\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, so
$$\rho = \frac{RA}{L} = \frac{(2.14)(1.96\times10^{-7})}{1.000} = 4.2\times10^{-7}\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}.$$

(b) Fractional: $0.02/2.14 = 0.9\%$, $2(0.01/0.50) = 4.0\%$, $0.005/1.000 = 0.5\%$; combined $4.1\%$, so $\rho = (4.2 \pm 0.2)\times10^{-7}\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$.

(c) The diameter alone contributes $4.0\%$ of the $4.1\%$: it dominates completely, because it is squared and because $0.01\ \mathrm{mm}$ on $0.50\ \mathrm{mm}$ is a $2\%$ reading error. **Check:** the value is close to nichrome (about $1.1\times10^{-6}$) or to a carbon-steel alloy; the exact identity matters less than the honest uncertainty.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Deciding whether results agree.</span> [Return →](03.02.html#p10)

(a) Difference $= 0.4\ \Omega$; combined uncertainty $= \sqrt{0.1^2+0.2^2} = 0.22\ \Omega$.

(b) The discrepancy is $0.4/0.22 = 1.8$ times the combined uncertainty, which is under 2 — the results are consistent within errors, though not comfortably so.

(c) A third, more precise measurement would shrink the uncertainties and show whose value (if either) is off; if the two original measurements are both slightly mis-calibrated in opposite directions, the average of the two would be right by accident. "They agree within errors" must be justified by the calculation above, not asserted, because a claim of agreement is a quantitative statement about the overlap of two ranges. **Check:** had the difference been $0.8\ \Omega$, the ratio would be $3.6$ and a systematic error would be the natural explanation.
