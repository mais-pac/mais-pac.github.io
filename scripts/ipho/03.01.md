## 1. Measurement is comparison

Every measurement is a comparison with a standard, and every comparison has a limit. A result without an uncertainty is not a measurement — it is a guess with a decimal point.

The practical questions are always the same three:

- What is the **resolution** of the instrument (the smallest change it can show)?
- What is the **uncertainty** of this particular reading (resolution, reading error, drift, calibration)?
- How does the uncertainty **propagate** into the quantity I actually want?

## 2. Reading instruments properly

The most common marks lost in practical exams are not about physics at all: they are about reading an instrument and trusting it to the right number of digits.

{{FIG:vernier}}

A **vernier calliper** has a main scale of $1\ \mathrm{mm}$ divisions and a sliding scale of $10$ divisions covering $9\ \mathrm{mm}$. Exactly one vernier division coincides with a main-scale mark, and its index gives the fraction of a millimetre. The least count is

$$\text{least count} = 1 - \frac{9}{10} = 0.1\ \mathrm{mm} = \frac{\text{one main division}}{\text{number of vernier divisions}} .$$

{{FIG:micrometer}}

A **micrometer** uses a screw of pitch $0.50\ \mathrm{mm}$ with $50$ divisions on the thimble, so one division is $0.01\ \mathrm{mm}$. Its reading is "barrel $+$ thimble division", and its resolution is ten times better than a vernier.

Three habits that prevent most mistakes:

- **Check the zero.** Close the jaws fully and note the reading; subtract it from every measurement. A zero error of $+0.3\ \mathrm{mm}$ is invisible on a $50\ \mathrm{mm}$ object and fatal on a $1\ \mathrm{mm}$ one.
- **Avoid parallax.** Read the scale with your eye level with the pointer; a rule held away from the object introduces an error of the order of the viewing angle.
- **Use the repeatability, not the resolution, as your uncertainty.** A manual stopwatch with a $0.01\ \mathrm{s}$ display is good to about $0.2\ \mathrm{s}$ in practice.

## 3. Random and systematic errors

{{FIG:errors}}

- **Random errors** vary in sign and size between repeats. They shift individual readings both ways, so repeating and averaging reduces their effect: the uncertainty of the mean falls as $1/\sqrt N$.
- **Systematic errors** push every reading the same way — a mis-calibrated scale, a zero error, a stopwatch started late, a systematic effect like cooling during a measurement. Repeating does not help at all; only a calibration check, a different method or a different instrument finds them.

{{FIG:resolution}}

A useful way to expose a systematic error is to plot your results against a theory that *should* be a straight line through the origin. A non-zero intercept is a zero error; a slope that is consistently off is a scale error.

## 4. Uncertainty in a single measurement

For a single reading the uncertainty is usually taken as half the smallest division (or the whole division for a scale read between marks), plus any calibration uncertainty. Quote it to one significant figure and match the decimal places of the value:

$$L = (24.3 \pm 0.1)\ \mathrm{mm}, \qquad \text{not } 24.31 \pm 0.10 .$$

## 5. Propagation: the three cases that cover everything

For a quantity $f(x, y)$ with independent uncertainties $\Delta x$, $\Delta y$:

- **Sum or difference:** uncertainties add, $\Delta f = \Delta x + \Delta y$ (worst case) or $\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}$ (independent random).
- **Product or quotient:** *fractional* uncertainties add: $\dfrac{\Delta f}{f} = \dfrac{\Delta x}{x} + \dfrac{\Delta y}{y}$.
- **Power:** a factor $x^n$ multiplies the fractional uncertainty by $|n|$: $\dfrac{\Delta f}{f} = |n|\dfrac{\Delta x}{x}$.

The most useful special case is $f = x^2$ or $x^3$: a $1\%$ error in a length becomes $2\%$ in an area and $3\%$ in a volume. That is why measuring a small dimension directly (a wire's diameter, not its cross-section) is worth the trouble.

General form (calculus): $\Delta f = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Delta x\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\Delta y\right)^2}$.

## 6. Precision, accuracy and significant figures

- **Precision** = how closely repeated measurements agree (small random error).
- **Accuracy** = how close the result is to the true value (small systematic error).
- **Resolution** = the smallest increment the instrument can display.

The three are independent. A digital thermometer reading to $0.01^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is precise but may be $2^\circ\mathrm{C}$ off (inaccurate); a ruler is accurate but only precise to a millimetre; a four-digit display fed by a one-digit sensor has resolution without information.

Rules for significant figures in a final answer: keep the digits justified by the uncertainty, round the uncertainty to one significant figure, and never quote more than one extra digit in the value.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">The measurement checklist.</span>
State the resolution and the uncertainty for every instrument you use → check the zero before and after → identify whether your error is random (average it away) or systematic (calibrate it away) → propagate uncertainties with the rules for sums, products and powers → quote the result with a sensible number of digits and an explicit uncertainty → sanity-check the value against a rough estimate before believing the third digit.</div>

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Reading a vernier.</span> [Return →](03.01.html#p1)

(a) $10$ vernier divisions cover $9\ \mathrm{mm}$, so the least count is $10-9 = 1\ \mathrm{mm}$ divided by $10$, i.e. $0.1\ \mathrm{mm}$.

(b) Raw $= 23 + 6(0.1) = 23.6\ \mathrm{mm}$.

(c) Corrected $= 23.6 - 0.20 = 23.4\ \mathrm{mm}$, with an uncertainty of about $0.05\ \mathrm{mm}$ (half the least count) plus the $0.05\ \mathrm{mm}$ uncertainty in the zero correction: $(23.40 \pm 0.10)\ \mathrm{mm}$. **Check:** the correction is small compared with the reading ($0.2$ in $23.4$), but it would be $17\%$ of a $1.2\ \mathrm{mm}$ reading.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — A micrometer reading.</span> [Return →](03.01.html#p2)

(a) Reading $= 3.50 + 27(0.01) = 3.77\ \mathrm{mm}$.

(b) Resolution $= 0.01\ \mathrm{mm}$ (one thimble division), so the uncertainty is about $\pm0.005\ \mathrm{mm}$ to $\pm0.01\ \mathrm{mm}$.

(c) At $3.77\ \mathrm{mm}$: $0.005/3.77 = 0.13\%$. At $0.12\ \mathrm{mm}$: $0.005/0.12 = 4\%$ — thirty times worse. **Check:** this is the general rule: an absolute uncertainty becomes a large *relative* one for small readings, which is why measuring a thin wire or a short time is hard.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Random or systematic?</span> [Return →](03.01.html#p3)

(a) **Systematic** (always the same direction); start the watch consistently or use a light gate.

(b) **Random** (drifts both ways over the experiment); repeat, plot the temperature, or use a water bath.

(c) **Systematic** (a zero error); tare the balance before use.

(d) **Random** (the eye's judgement varies); use a magnifier, take many readings, or use a digital sensor.

(e) **Systematic and random together**: the average diameter may be off systematically if the wire is tapered, and the readings scatter randomly about it. Measure at several points along the wire and quote the spread. **Check:** only the systematic ones fail to improve with repetition, which is the operational test for the distinction.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Propagation through a formula.</span> [Return →](03.01.html#p4)

(a) $g = \dfrac{4\pi^2(0.994)}{(2.00)^2} = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

(b) Fractional uncertainties: $T$ contributes $2\times(0.02/2.00) = 2.0\%$, $L$ contributes $0.002/0.994 = 0.2\%$. Adding in quadrature, $\Delta g/g = \sqrt{2.0^2+0.2^2}\% \approx 2.0\%$, so $g = (9.81 \pm 0.20)\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

(c) The period dominates because it is squared and because a manual stopwatch is imprecise. Timing many oscillations (Problem 9) is the standard fix. **Check:** a $2\%$ uncertainty is far too coarse to distinguish $9.81$ from $9.78$, so the experiment as described cannot measure $g$ well — which is exactly the sort of thing an examiner wants you to notice.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Averaging.</span> [Return →](03.01.html#p5)

(a) $\sigma_{\bar x} = s/\sqrt N = 0.12/\sqrt{10} = 0.038\ \mathrm{mm}$, so the result is $(52.30 \pm 0.04)\ \mathrm{mm}$.

(b) To get $0.02\ \mathrm{mm}$: $\sqrt N = 0.12/0.02 = 6$, so $N = 36$ readings.

(c) Beyond about $10$–$20$ readings a systematic effect (the ruler's calibration, the object's non-uniformity, drift) usually exceeds the statistical uncertainty, so the $1/\sqrt N$ law stops working. **Check:** $36$ readings for a factor of $2$ improvement is a poor return, which is why better instruments beat more readings.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Calipers versus ruler.</span> [Return →](03.01.html#p6)

(a) $A = \pi d^2/4 = \pi(1.84\times10^{-3})^2/4 = 2.66\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2}$. The diameter has $0.01/1.84 = 0.54\%$ uncertainty, doubled to $1.1\%$ for the area: $A = (2.66 \pm 0.03)\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2}$.

(b) With $d = 2 \pm 1\ \mathrm{mm}$, $A = (3 \pm 3)\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ — a $100\%$ uncertainty, which is no measurement at all.

(c) Resistivity is $\rho = RA/L$, so the diameter enters squared (through $A$); a $50\%$ error in $d$ becomes a $100\%$ error in $\rho$. **Check:** the area ratio between the two methods is about $1.5$, so even the *value* disagrees, not just the precision — a ruler cannot measure a $2\ \mathrm{mm}$ wire.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Significant figures.</span> [Return →](03.01.html#p7)

(a) Mass: $0.1/54.3 = 0.18\%$; volume: $0.2/20.0 = 1.0\%$; total $\approx 1.0\%$ (the volume dominates).

(b) $\rho = 2.71\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ with $\pm 1\%$, i.e. $(2.71 \pm 0.03)\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$.

(c) The six digits claim a precision of one part in a million, while the data justify about one part in a hundred. Quoting unjustified digits is not a harmless habit: it misrepresents the quality of the experiment. **Check:** the rounded value, $2.7\times10^{3}$, is close to aluminium ($2700$), which is the kind of consistency check that reassures you the arithmetic is right.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — A systematic error hunt.</span> [Return →](03.01.html#p8)

(a) Mean $= 10.18\ \Omega$; the spread is about $\pm0.1\ \Omega$, so the standard uncertainty of the mean is roughly $0.1/\sqrt5 = 0.04\ \Omega$: $R = (10.18 \pm 0.05)\ \Omega$.

(b) The stated value is $9.8 \pm 0.1\ \Omega$. The difference, $0.38\ \Omega$, is about four times the combined uncertainty, so it is significant — there is a systematic problem, not bad luck.

(c) Plausible causes: (i) the ammeter has a resistance, so the measured current is slightly low — use a voltmeter across the resistor only, or correct for the ammeter; (ii) the voltmeter draws current, so the measured current is slightly high — repeat with a high-impedance meter; (iii) the resistor heats up and its resistance drifts — keep the current low and check for drift. A fourth possibility is a zero offset in either meter, testable by short-circuiting the ammeter and comparing the voltmeter with a known source. **Check:** every one of these produces a *consistent* offset, which is exactly why more repeats would never have revealed it.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Timing with a stopwatch.</span> [Return →](03.01.html#p9)

(a) Mean time for $20$ oscillations $= 40.2\ \mathrm{s}$; the reaction-time uncertainty is about $0.2\ \mathrm{s}$ for a single start-stop pair (and about $0.2\ \mathrm{s}$ in total, since both the start and the stop are uncertain). The period is $40.2/20 = 2.01\ \mathrm{s}$ with uncertainty $0.2/20 = 0.01\ \mathrm{s}$: $T = (2.01 \pm 0.01)\ \mathrm{s}$.

(b) Timing one oscillation would give $2.0 \pm 0.2\ \mathrm{s}$ — a $10\%$ uncertainty. Timing $N$ oscillations divides the absolute uncertainty by $N$, so a $20$-swing timing reduces it to $1\%$.

(c) $g = 4\pi^2L/T^2$, so $\Delta g/g = 2\Delta T/T = 2(0.01/2.01) = 1.0\%$: $g = (9.7 \pm 0.1)\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ for $L = 1.00\ \mathrm{m}$. **Check:** timing more oscillations (say $50$) is the cheapest improvement, but beyond a few hundred the pendulum's amplitude decays and the frequency drifts.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Designing a measurement.</span> [Return →](03.01.html#p10)

(a) Mass: a digital balance reading to $0.01\ \mathrm{g}$ — the uncertainty is $0.01/30 = 0.03\%$, negligible. Volume: the stone is irregular, so measure it by displacement in a narrow measuring cylinder or, better, by weighing it in water (Archimedes). A $25\ \mathrm{mL}$ cylinder reads to $0.5\ \mathrm{mL}$, giving $0.5/12 = 4\%$ for a $12\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ stone — too coarse for $1\%$; a $10\ \mathrm{mL}$ burette with $0.05\ \mathrm{mL}$ divisions gives $0.4\%$.

(b) Balance: $0.03\%$; burette displacement: about $0.4\%$; so the combined uncertainty is about $0.4\%$, comfortably inside the $1\%$ target.

(c) The volume limits the accuracy, because the stone is small and irregular. Improve it by using a larger sample, by using a burette rather than a cylinder, by coating a porous stone to stop water soaking in, and by repeating the displacement several times. **Check:** if the stone is porous, the volume measured is the envelope plus the water absorbed — a systematic error that no amount of repetition removes, which is why the coating matters.
</div>
