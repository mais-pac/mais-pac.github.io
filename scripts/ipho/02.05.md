## 1. Pressure and its consequences

Pressure is force per unit area, and in a fluid at rest it acts equally in every direction — a scalar, not a vector. Two results follow immediately:

- **Depth dependence.** $p = p_0 + \rho gh$. The shape of the container is irrelevant, only the depth.
- **Pascal's principle.** A pressure change applied at one point in an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished everywhere. That is the hydraulic press, and it trades force against distance, never energy.

{{FIG:pressure}}

## 2. Buoyancy from a pressure difference

The pressure on the bottom of a submerged block exceeds that on its top by $\rho_f g h$, so the net upward force is

$$F_b = \rho_f V g,$$

the weight of displaced fluid. That is Archimedes' principle, derived rather than asserted.

Three consequences are worth remembering:

- A body floats when its mean density is less than the fluid's, and it displaces its own weight.
- A body in equilibrium fully submerged feels an apparent weight reduced by $F_b$; this is the classical method of measuring density.
- A floating object's *stability* depends on the position of the metacentre, not on the centre of mass alone — the reason a wide boat is stable and a narrow one capsizes.

{{FIG:buoyancy}}

## 3. Continuity and Bernoulli

For a steady, incompressible flow, mass conservation gives the **continuity equation**:

$$A_1v_1 = A_2v_2 .$$

Along a streamline, energy conservation per unit volume gives **Bernoulli's equation**:

$$p + \tfrac12\rho v^2 + \rho gh = \text{constant}.$$

{{FIG:continuity}}

The three terms are pressure energy, kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy per unit volume. Reading Bernoulli as “fast flow means low pressure” is only safe when the pipe is level; when the pipe rises, the pressure *gains* $\rho gh$ even if the flow slows.

{{FIG:bernoulli}}

Two standard applications:

- **Torricelli's law.** For a small hole a depth $h$ below the free surface, the speed is $v = \sqrt{2gh}$ — the same as free fall from that height. The jet then follows projectile motion.
- **The Venturi effect.** A narrowing speeds the flow and lowers the pressure; this is how a carburettor, an atomiser and a Pitot-static airspeed indicator work.

{{LAB:torricelli}}

## 4. Surface tension and capillarity

Surface tension $\gamma$ is energy per unit area, or equivalently force per unit length along a surface. It explains why:

- **Small drops are spherical** — a sphere has the smallest area for a given volume.
- **A curved surface sustains a pressure difference:**
$$\Delta p = \frac{2\gamma}{r}\ \text{(one surface)},\qquad \Delta p = \frac{4\gamma}{r}\ \text{(a soap bubble, two surfaces)}.$$
- **Liquid rises in a narrow tube:**
$$h = \frac{2\gamma\cos\theta}{\rho g r},$$
so a narrow tube lifts water higher, and a non-wetting liquid (mercury, $\theta > 90^\circ$) is *depressed*.
- **Small objects float** — a needle on water is supported by the deformed surface, not by buoyancy.

{{FIG:surface}}

## 5. Real fluids: viscosity and Reynolds number

Real fluids resist flow. For slow flow through a pipe the volume rate is given by Poiseuille's law, $Q \propto r^4\,\Delta p/(\eta L)$ — the fourth power of the radius is why arteries narrow dangerously. Whether a flow is smooth (laminar) or turbulent is set by the **Reynolds number**

$$\mathrm{Re} = \frac{\rho v L}{\eta},$$

with a transition near $\mathrm{Re}\sim2000$ for pipe flow. For a sphere at low Reynolds number, Stokes' law gives drag $F = 6\pi\eta r v$, which produces the terminal velocity $v_T = \dfrac{2r^2(\rho_s-\rho_f)g}{9\eta}$ — a result that scales as $r^2$ and explains why fine dust settles slowly.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">The fluid checklist.</span>
Decide first whether the fluid is incompressible (all liquids, gases at low speed) → write continuity → write Bernoulli between two points *on the same streamline* → use $p = p_0+\rho gh$ for the static part → check the limits (zero speed, zero hole size, zero gravity) → only then consider viscosity or turbulence.</div>

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Pressure in the deep.</span> [Return →](02.05.html#p1)

(a) $p_{\text{gauge}} = \rho gh = 1030\times9.81\times10^{4} = 1.01\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{Pa}$, about $1000$ atmospheres.

(b) $F = pA$ with $A = \pi(0.15)^2 = 0.0707\ \mathrm{m^2}$: $F_{\text{gauge}} = 7.1\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{N}$; including the atmosphere adds only $1.0\times10^{5}\times0.0707 \approx 7\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{N}$, a $0.1\%$ correction.

(c) The glass sees the *difference* across it: the outside is at atmospheric pressure, so the load is essentially the gauge pressure. The total pressure would be relevant if the porthole were sealed and evacuated on one side and the whole assembly were at depth. **Check:** the gauge pressure dominates here by three orders of magnitude, which is why designers quote gauge values for deep-sea structures.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Floating and sinking.</span> [Return →](02.05.html#p2)

(a) Floating means displaced weight equals the block's weight: $\rho_fV_{\text{sub}}g = \rho_bVg$, so $V_{\text{sub}}/V = \rho_b/\rho_f = 600/1000 = 0.60$.

(b) In ethanol the ratio is $600/789 = 0.76$, so three-quarters of the block is submerged.

(c) The ratio $\rho_b/\rho_f$ contains no volume, so shape and size are irrelevant. In a liquid of exactly equal density the block floats fully submerged and neutral — any further increase in $\rho_f$ makes it sink. **Check:** the fractions must lie between 0 and 1, which they do for $\rho_b<\rho_f$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Torricelli and the jet.</span> [Return →](02.05.html#p3)

(a) Bernoulli between the free surface (speed $\approx0$) and the hole, both at the same pressure $p_0$:
$$p_0 + \rho g(h+H) = p_0 + \tfrac12\rho v^2 + \rho gH \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{2gh}.$$
The approximation is $A\gg a$, so the free surface descends negligibly fast.

(b) The jet leaves horizontally at height $H$: $t = \sqrt{2H/g}$ and $R = v t = 2\sqrt{hH}$.

(c) With $h + H = \ell$ fixed, $R = 2\sqrt{h(\ell-h)}$, maximal at $h = \ell/2$, i.e. the hole halfway down. For $a$ comparable with $A$ the surface speed cannot be neglected: the exact result is $v = \sqrt{2gh}\,\big/\sqrt{1-(a/A)^2}$. **Check:** $R$ is symmetric under $h\leftrightarrow H$, as the formula demands, and vanishes at both ends.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — The Venturi meter.</span> [Return →](02.05.html#p4)

(a) Continuity gives $v_2 = v_1A_1/A_2$; Bernoulli gives $\Delta p = \tfrac12\rho(v_2^2 - v_1^2)$. Substituting,
$$v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2\Delta p}{\rho\left[(A_1/A_2)^2-1\right]}},\qquad Q = A_1v_1 .$$

(b) For $A_2\to A_1$ the denominator vanishes, so $Q\to0$ for fixed $\Delta p$ — correct, since no pressure difference is generated. For $A_2\to0$ the prediction diverges, signalling that real compressibility and viscosity dominate.

(c) $(A_1/A_2)^2 = 6.25$, so $v_1 = \sqrt{2(12000)/(1.2\times5.25)} = \sqrt{3809} = 61.7\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $Q = 0.020\times61.7 = 1.23\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}$. **Check:** the units are m³/s and the speed is subsonic, so the incompressible treatment is borderline but defensible.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Capillary rise.</span> [Return →](02.05.html#p5)

(a) $h = 2\gamma/(\rho g r) \Rightarrow r = 2\gamma/(\rho gh) = 2(0.072)/(1000\cdot9.81\cdot0.08) = 1.83\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m}$, about $0.18\ \mathrm{mm}$.

(b) Halving the radius doubles the rise to $16\ \mathrm{cm}$.

(c) Mercury has a contact angle above $90^\circ$, so $\cos\theta<0$ and the formula predicts a *negative* height — a depression. The pressure just under the meniscus is $p_0 - 2\gamma/r$ (a lower pressure for a concave meniscus), and that is a local quantity while the rise is the hydrostatic response to it. **Check:** the rise is inversely proportional to $r$, consistent with the pressure difference scaling as $1/r$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — The hydraulic press.</span> [Return →](02.05.html#p6)

(a) The areas are in the ratio $(20/2)^2 = 100$, so the force is $200\times100 = 2.0\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{N}$.

(b) Volume conservation: $A_1d_1 = A_2d_2 \Rightarrow d_1 = 100\times0.05 = 5.0\ \mathrm{m}$.

(c) Work in: $200\times5.0 = 1000\ \mathrm{J}$. Work out: $2.0\times10^{4}\times0.05 = 1000\ \mathrm{J}$. Equal, so no energy is created: the press multiplies force and divides distance. **Check:** the mechanical advantage equals the area ratio, exactly as a lever's equals the arm ratio.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — The floating ice cube.</span> [Return →](02.05.html#p7)

(a) The ice displaces water whose weight equals the ice's weight, i.e. mass $m$. When it melts it becomes mass $m$ of water, occupying exactly the volume it had displaced. The level is unchanged.

(b) An air bubble reduces the ice's mass without reducing its volume, so it displaces less water and the level *falls* slightly on melting. A dense stone inside makes the ice displace extra water equal to the stone's weight; once released, the stone sinks and displaces only its own volume, which is less than its weight's worth of water — so the level falls too. In both cases the level falls; only pure ice leaves it unchanged.

(c) Track displaced volume against weight before and after; water is the reference, and any material denser than water “wastes” displacement once it sinks. **Check:** the pure-ice case is the unique case where displaced mass and melted mass are equal.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Bernoulli in a siphon.</span> [Return →](02.05.html#p8)

(a) Bernoulli between the free surface and the outlet, both at $p_0$: the height drop from the surface to the outlet is $h_2 - h_1$ (with the sign convention that the outlet is lower), giving $v = \sqrt{2g(h_2-h_1)}$.

(b) At the highest point, height $H$ above the free surface, Bernoulli gives $p = p_0 - \rho gH - \tfrac12\rho v^2$. For the water to stay liquid, $p>0$, hence $H \lesssim p_0/(\rho g) \approx 10.3\ \mathrm{m}$ (less with the kinetic term, and much less in practice because of dissolved gas).

(c) A bubble breaks the continuous liquid path: the pressure at the top cannot be transmitted through the gas in the same way, and the column separates. **Check:** setting $v=0$ in (b) returns the hydrostatic limit $p_0 = \rho gH$, the barometer height.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Stokes' law and terminal velocity.</span> [Return →](02.05.html#p9)

(a) At terminal speed the forces balance: $6\pi\eta rv_T = \tfrac43\pi r^3(\rho_s-\rho_f)g$, so $v_T = \dfrac{2r^2(\rho_s-\rho_f)g}{9\eta}$.

(b) $v_T\propto r^2$, so a bead twice as large falls four times faster.

(c) $v_T = \dfrac{2(10^{-3})^2(2650-1000)(9.81)}{9(10^{-3})} = 3.6\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Then $\mathrm{Re} = \rho v d/\eta = (1000)(3.6\times10^{-3})(2\times10^{-3})/10^{-3} = 7.2$, well below the turbulent transition and comfortably inside the Stokes regime. **Check:** the square-law scaling and the small Reynolds number together confirm that fine sediment settles slowly and smoothly.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — A barge, a bridge and a load.</span> [Return →](02.05.html#p10)

(a) $V_{\text{sub}} = M/\rho = 30\ \mathrm{m^3}$; with a waterplane area $LW = 100\ \mathrm{m^2}$ the draft is $0.30\ \mathrm{m}$.

(b) The extra $1.5\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{kg}$ adds $15\ \mathrm{m^3}$ of displacement, i.e. $0.15\ \mathrm{m}$ more draft (total $0.45\ \mathrm{m}$) if the load is centred. Placed at one end, the barge also acquires a trim angle: the centre of buoyancy must move under the combined centre of mass, so the end carrying the crane sits deeper. With a $20\ \mathrm{m}$ length and a load at $10\ \mathrm{m}$ from the centre, the trim is of order $\arctan(10\times0.15/20)\approx 4^\circ$ — noticeable, but nowhere near swamping for a barge whose freeboard is more than a metre.

(c) While the container hangs, its weight is carried by the cable, so the barge's draft is unchanged. As it is lowered and touches the water, the water supplies an upthrust that grows with the submerged volume, so the cable tension falls; the barge's draft is still unchanged because nothing rests on it. Once the container is set down on the deck, its full weight is transferred to the barge, the tension drops to zero, and the draft rises by $50/100 = 0.50\ \mathrm{m}$, giving a total draft of $0.95\ \mathrm{m}$. **Check:** the draft changes only at the moment of load transfer, which is exactly when the barge's weight changes.
</div>
