## 1. What thermodynamics is about

Mechanics follows one particle; thermodynamics follows $10^{23}$ of them, and refuses to track them individually. Instead it uses a handful of **state variables** — pressure $p$, volume $V$, temperature $T$, internal energy $U$, entropy $S$ — that depend only on the present state of the gas, not on how it got there.

The equation of state of an ideal gas ties three of them together:

$$pV = nRT = Nk_BT, \qquad R = 8.31\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}, \quad k_B = 1.38\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}} .$$

An ideal gas is one whose molecules have negligible volume and no forces between them except during collisions. Real gases approximate it well at low pressure and high temperature, and depart badly near condensation.

**Temperature** must be on the absolute (kelvin) scale in every gas equation. Absolute zero is where the pressure of a fixed amount of gas would extrapolate to zero: $-273.15^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

## 2. Kinetic theory: pressure from molecular collisions

Each collision with a wall transfers momentum, and a vast number of collisions per second constitutes a steady pressure. The result of the calculation is

$$p = \frac13\frac{Nm}{V}\overline{v^2} = \frac13\rho\,\overline{v^2},$$

and comparison with $pV = Nk_BT$ identifies

$$\tfrac12m\overline{v^2} = \tfrac32k_BT .$$

That is a profound statement: **temperature is the average translational kinetic energy per molecule**, and it is independent of the gas species. The three characteristic speeds differ by small numerical factors:

$$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}, \qquad v_{\text{mp}} = \sqrt{\frac{2k_BT}{m}}, \qquad \bar v = \sqrt{\frac{8k_BT}{\pi m}} .$$

{{FIG:maxwell}}

{{LAB:gas}}

The distribution of speeds is the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, with a long tail at high speeds. That tail is why evaporation happens below the boiling point, why chemical reactions have a temperature-dependent rate (the Arrhenius law), and why a gas at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ still contains a few molecules fast enough to escape the Earth's gravity — over geological time, that is how the atmosphere lost its hydrogen and helium.

## 3. The first law: energy bookkeeping

$$\Delta U = Q - W, \qquad W = \int p\,dV .$$

{{FIG:first-law}}

The internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on $T$: $U = \frac{f}{2}nRT$ with $f$ the number of degrees of freedom ($3$ for monatomic, $5$ for diatomic at ordinary temperatures). Then the four standard processes:

| process | condition | work | heat | result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| isothermal | $T$ const | $nRT\ln(V_2/V_1)$ | $Q = W$ | $\Delta U = 0$ |
| isobaric | $p$ const | $p\,\Delta V = nR\Delta T$ | $C_p n\Delta T$ | $W = nR\Delta T$ |
| isochoric | $V$ const | $0$ | $C_V n\Delta T$ | $\Delta U = Q$ |
| adiabatic | $Q = 0$ | $-\Delta U$ | $0$ | $pV^\gamma = $ const |

The ratio $\gamma = C_p/C_V = 1 + 2/f$ is $5/3$ for a monatomic gas and $7/5$ for a diatomic one — measurable in the speed of sound, and the reason a bicycle pump gets hot.

Two useful consequences of the adiabatic relation: a gas expanding adiabatically cools (the basis of refrigeration and of the condensation trail behind a wing), and a sound wave is an adiabatic compression, so $v_{\text{sound}} = \sqrt{\gamma p/\rho}$.

## 4. The second law and engines

The first law forbids perpetual motion machines of the first kind (creating energy). The second forbids the second kind: you cannot convert heat completely into work in a cyclic process.

{{FIG:carnot}}

The **Carnot efficiency** is the absolute ceiling for any engine working between a hot reservoir at $T_H$ and a cold sink at $T_C$:

$$\eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}, \qquad \eta = \frac{W}{Q_H} .$$

Real engines (steam turbines, car engines) reach perhaps $40\%$ of what the Carnot limit allows at their temperatures; the limit is set by the temperatures, not by engineering cleverness.

Entropy is the quantitative version of the second law:

$$dS = \frac{dQ_{\text{rev}}}{T}, \qquad \Delta S_{\text{universe}} \ge 0 .$$

{{FIG:entropy}}

For an ideal gas, $\Delta S = nC_V\ln(T_2/T_1) + nR\ln(V_2/V_1)$, which shows that free expansion into a vacuum (where $T$ is unchanged but $V$ grows) increases entropy — a process that is irreversible though not energy-changing. Boltzmann's formula

$$S = k_B\ln W$$

identifies entropy with the number of microscopic arrangements consistent with the macroscopic state, which is why the second law is statistical: it is not that a shattered glass *cannot* reassemble, only that the number of ways for it to stay shattered is astronomically larger.

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — The ideal gas law.</span> [Return →](02.15.html#p1)

(a) $T = 300\ \mathrm{K}$, so $V = \dfrac{nRT}{p} = \dfrac{(0.25)(8.31)(300)}{1.5\times10^{5}} = 4.2\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3} = 4.2\ \mathrm{L}$.

(b) $N = nN_A = (0.25)(6.02\times10^{23}) = 1.5\times10^{23}$ molecules.

(c) At constant $T$, $p\propto1/V$, so halving the volume doubles the pressure: $3.0\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{Pa}$. **Check:** $pV = nRT = 623\ \mathrm{J}$ at both states, as Boyle's law requires.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Molecular speeds.</span> [Return →](02.15.html#p2)

(a) $v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3RT/M} = \sqrt{3(8.31)(300)/(4.0\times10^{-3})} = \sqrt{1.87\times10^{6}} = 1.37\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(b) $v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3(8.31)(300)/(28\times10^{-3})} = \sqrt{2.67\times10^{5}} = 5.17\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(c) $v_{\text{rms}}\propto\sqrt{T/M}$, so equal speeds need $T_{\mathrm{N_2}} = 300\times(28/4.0) = 2.1\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{K}$. **Check:** helium is seven times lighter, so at the same temperature its molecules move $\sqrt7 = 2.65$ times faster; $1370/517 = 2.65$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Work in three processes.</span> [Return →](02.15.html#p3)

(a) Isothermal: $W = nRT\ln(V_2/V_1) = (1)(8.31)(300)\ln2 = 1.73\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{J}$.

(b) Isobaric from the same state: $p = nRT/V_1 = (1)(8.31)(300)/(0.010) = 2.49\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{Pa}$, so $W = p\Delta V = (2.49\times10^{5})(0.010) = 2.49\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{J}$, and $T_2 = 2T_1 = 600\ \mathrm{K}$.

(c) In the isothermal case the pressure falls as the gas expands, so the average pressure is lower and less work is done. The isobaric process needs more heat, because it must both do more work and raise the temperature ($\Delta U = \frac32nR\Delta T = 3.7\ \mathrm{kJ}$). **Check:** isothermal $Q = W = 1.73\ \mathrm{kJ}$; isobaric $Q = \frac52nR\Delta T = 6.2\ \mathrm{kJ}$, consistent with more work plus more internal energy.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — An adiabatic compression.</span> [Return →](02.15.html#p4)

(a) $pV^\gamma$ is constant with $\gamma = 5/3$, so
$$p_2 = p_1\left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{5/3} = (1.0\times10^{5})(8)^{5/3} = (1.0\times10^{5})(32) = 3.2\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{Pa}.$$

(b) $TV^{\gamma-1}$ is constant: $T_2 = 300\times8^{2/3} = 300(4) = 1200\ \mathrm{K}$.

(c) $W_{\text{on}} = \Delta U = \frac32nR\Delta T = \frac32(2.0)(8.31)(900) = 2.2\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{J}$. **Check:** the pressure rose by a factor $32 = 8^{5/3}$ and the temperature by $4 = 8^{2/3}$, and $pV/T$ is the same at both ends: $(10^5)(V)/(300) = (3.2\times10^6)(V/8)/(1200)$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — A Carnot engine.</span> [Return →](02.15.html#p5)

(a) $\eta_C = 1 - 300/500 = 0.40$, so $W_{\max} = 0.40\times2.0 = 0.80\ \mathrm{kJ}$.

(b) $Q_C = Q_H - W = 2.0 - 0.80 = 1.2\ \mathrm{kJ}$.

(c) With $\eta = 0.30$, $W = 0.60\ \mathrm{kJ}$, so $Q_C = 1.4\ \mathrm{kJ}$. The entropy changes are $\Delta S_H = -2000/500 = -4.0\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ (reservoir) and $\Delta S_C = +1400/300 = +4.67\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$, so the universe gains $0.67\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ per cycle. **Check:** for the reversible (Carnot) engine the two changes are equal and opposite, $0.67\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ less, exactly as the second law requires.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Kinetic theory estimates.</span> [Return →](02.15.html#p6)

(a) $n_V = \dfrac{p}{k_BT} = \dfrac{1.0\times10^{5}}{(1.38\times10^{-23})(300)} = 2.4\times10^{25}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$.

(b) $\ell \approx \dfrac{1}{\sqrt2\,n_V\pi d^2} = \dfrac{1}{1.414(2.4\times10^{25})\pi(3.0\times10^{-10})^2} = 1.0\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m}$ — about $100\ \mathrm{nm}$.

(c) $v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3k_BT/m} = \sqrt{3(1.38\times10^{-23})(300)/(4.8\times10^{-26})} = 5.1\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, so the collision frequency is $v/\ell = 5.1\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ — five billion collisions per second. **Check:** this is why diffusion in air is slow compared with the molecular speed: each molecule zigzags a huge distance to make progress of a centimetre.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Mixing and entropy.</span> [Return →](02.15.html#p7)

(a) No work is done and no heat crosses the insulation, so $U$ is unchanged and $T$ stays the same. The volume doubles and the amount of gas doubles, so $p$ is unchanged as well: the gas simply fills twice the space at the same density.

(b) Considering the *whole* gas, it expands from $V$ to $3V$? No: the two halves already occupy $V$ and $2V$; after mixing each molecule has access to $3V$. For each mole,
$$\Delta S = nR\ln\frac{V_f}{V_i} = 4.6\ln3\ \text{for the first} + 4.6\ln\frac{3}{2}\ \text{for the second} = 5.0 + 1.9 = 6.9\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.$$

(c) The reverse — all the gas spontaneously gathering in one half — is not forbidden by energy conservation, but the number of microscopic arrangements corresponding to the mixed state is enormously larger. The second law is statistical, and the probability of the reverse is of order $2^{-N}$ with $N\sim10^{23}$. **Check:** $\Delta S > 0$ and $\Delta U = 0$: entropy and energy are independent bookkeeping, which is precisely the content of the second law.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — A cyclic process.</span> [Return →](02.15.html#p8)

(a) Work is done only in the two isothermal steps:
$$W_{1} = nR(400)\ln2 = 2.31\ \mathrm{kJ}, \qquad W_{3} = nR(300)\ln\tfrac12 = -1.73\ \mathrm{kJ},$$
so the net work is $0.58\ \mathrm{kJ}$.

(b) Step 1: $Q_1 = W_1 = +2.31\ \mathrm{kJ}$. Step 2 (isochoric cooling from 400 to 300 K): $Q_2 = \frac32nR(-100) = -1.25\ \mathrm{kJ}$. Step 3: $Q_3 = W_3 = -1.73\ \mathrm{kJ}$. Step 4 (isochoric heating): $Q_4 = +1.25\ \mathrm{kJ}$. The net heat is $2.31 - 1.25 - 1.73 + 1.25 = 0.58\ \mathrm{kJ}$, matching the net work.

(c) $\eta = W/Q_{\text{in}} = 0.58/2.31 = 0.25$. The Carnot efficiency between 400 and 300 K is $1 - 300/400 = 0.25$ — equal, because this cycle is reversible. **Check:** the two isochoric steps exchange equal and opposite heat, so they do not affect the efficiency, only the peak temperature.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — The speed of sound.</span> [Return →](02.15.html#p9)

(a) For an adiabatic compression, $dp/d\rho = \gamma p/\rho$, and the wave equation $v = \sqrt{dp/d\rho}$ gives $v = \sqrt{\gamma p/\rho}$. Using $\rho = pM/(RT)$ gives $v = \sqrt{\gamma RT/M}$.

(b) $v = \sqrt{1.40(8.31)(293)/0.029} = \sqrt{1.18\times10^{5}} = 343\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(c) At fixed $T$, $\rho\propto p$, so $p/\rho$ is constant and the pressure cancels. Humidity matters because water vapour has molar mass $18$ against $29$ for air, so moist air is *less* dense: the speed of sound rises slightly on a humid day. **Check:** $343\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ is the standard value, and the ratio to the rms molecular speed is about $0.7$, as expected for a wave carried by collisions.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — A real engine.</span> [Return →](02.15.html#p10)

(a) Using the hottest and coldest temperatures, $\eta_C = 1 - 400/2200 = 0.82$.

(b) $\eta = W/Q_H = 60/200 = 0.30$.

(c) The Carnot value assumes the whole heat input arrives at the top temperature and the whole output leaves at the bottom one, with no friction and infinitely slow processes. In a real engine the combustion gases cool as they expand, the exhaust leaves far above ambient, there is friction and turbulence, and the cycle takes a finite time. The gap between $30\%$ and $82\%$ is the price of speed, materials and irreversibility. **Check:** even a very good power station reaches only about $45\%$ of its Carnot limit, so $30\%$ against $82\%$ is typical of a car engine rather than a sign of a mistake.
</div>
