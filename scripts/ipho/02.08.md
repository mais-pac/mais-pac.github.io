## 1. What makes a circuit hard

In electrostatics, charges sit still. In a circuit, charges are pushed round a closed path, and the difficulty is not the arithmetic but the *bookkeeping*: which quantity is shared, and which is divided up.

Three ideas organise everything in this topic:

- **Kirchhoff's rules.** Charge is conserved at every junction, and energy is conserved round every loop. Both stay true whether the elements are linear or not.
- **Components store or dissipate.** A resistor turns electrical energy into heat and has no memory. A capacitor stores energy in an electric field, an inductor in a magnetic field, and both have memory: their state cannot change instantaneously.
- **Time and frequency are two views of the same circuit.** A step input is described by exponentials with a time constant; a sinusoidal input is described by impedances and phasors. Olympiad problems use one or the other, and choosing well halves the work.

## 2. Kirchhoff's rules, done properly

Label every branch current with a direction. The direction you guess does not matter: a wrong guess simply produces a negative number, which is a correct answer, not an error.

- **Junction rule:** $\sum I_{\text{in}} = \sum I_{\text{out}}$. Charge cannot pile up at a point in a steady circuit.
- **Loop rule:** $\sum \Delta V = 0$ around any closed loop. Going *with* a current through $R$ drops the potential by $IR$; going through a source from $-$ to $+$ raises it by $\varepsilon$.

The two rules are not independent of the element laws — they only supply the equations, while $V = IR$, $Q = CV$ and $\varepsilon = -L\,dI/dt$ describe each element. A network with $b$ branches and $n$ junctions needs $b$ independent equations: $n-1$ junction equations and $b-n+1$ loop equations.

Two shortcuts worth practising until they are automatic:

- **Series/parallel reduction.** Elements in series share the same current and add their voltages (so $R$ add, $\frac1C$ add); elements in parallel share the same voltage and add their currents ($\frac1R$ add, $C$ add).
- **Superposition and symmetry.** In a linear circuit you may solve one source at a time and add the answers. If a network is symmetric, some branches must carry no current at all — often the fastest route to the answer.

## 3. The RC circuit: exponentials from a step

Put a battery, a resistor and a capacitor in series. The loop rule reads

$$\varepsilon = IR + \frac{q}{C}, \qquad I = \frac{dq}{dt},$$

which is a first-order differential equation. Its solution from an empty capacitor is

$$q(t) = C\varepsilon\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad I(t) = \frac{\varepsilon}{R}e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = RC .$$

{{FIG:rc-circuit}}

{{FIG:rc}}

After one time constant the capacitor has $63\%$ of its final charge and the current has fallen to $37\%$ of its initial value. After five, it is "finished" for any practical purpose.

Three facts carry most of the marks:

- At $t=0$ an uncharged capacitor behaves like a **wire** (it can carry any current, with no voltage across it).
- After a long time a capacitor behaves like a **break** (no steady current, so it carries the full source voltage).
- The energy accounting is surprising: the battery supplies $C\varepsilon^2$ while the capacitor stores only $\frac12C\varepsilon^2$. The other half is dissipated in the resistor *whatever* the value of $R$. With $R\to0$ the power rises and the time shrinks, and the product stays fixed.

Discharging works the same way with the source removed: $q = Q_0e^{-t/\tau}$, and the stored energy is lost to the resistor.

## 4. The RL circuit: inertia for current

Replace the capacitor with an inductor and the equation becomes

$$\varepsilon = IR + L\frac{dI}{dt} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I(t) = \frac{\varepsilon}{R}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad \tau = \frac{L}{R}.$$

{{FIG:rl}}

Now it is the *current* that cannot jump, and the roles of the two limits swap: at $t=0$ the inductor behaves like a **break** (it takes the whole source voltage, $L\,dI/dt = \varepsilon$, while $I=0$), and after a long time it behaves like a **wire**.

That behaviour is the origin of the spark when you unplug an inductive device: interrupting the current forces $dI/dt$ to be huge and negative, so $L\,dI/dt$ becomes a large voltage that can break down the air. It is also why a large inductor must never be disconnected abruptly.

## 5. LC oscillations and resonance

Join a charged capacitor to an inductor with no resistance. The capacitor discharges through the inductor, the inductor's back-emf keeps the current flowing until the capacitor is charged the other way, and the process repeats:

$$L\frac{d^2q}{dt^2} + \frac{q}{C} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q = Q_0\cos(\omega_0 t), \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} .$$

{{FIG:lc}}

The energy shuttles between the electric field of the capacitor and the magnetic field of the inductor, and in the ideal case the total stays constant. This is a harmonic oscillator in a different costume: $q$ plays the role of displacement, $I$ of velocity, $1/C$ of spring constant and $L$ of mass.

Add a resistance and the oscillation decays with time constant $2L/R$; the circuit is underdamped, critically damped or overdamped according to whether $R^2$ is smaller than, equal to, or larger than $4L/C$.

For a *sinusoidally driven* series RLC circuit the steady state is best found with impedances:

$$X_L = \omega L, \qquad X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C}, \qquad Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}, \qquad I_0 = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{Z} .$$

{{FIG:phasor}}

At the resonant frequency $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ the two reactances cancel, $Z = R$, and the current peaks. The sharpness of that peak is measured by the quality factor

$$Q = \frac{\omega_0L}{R} = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}, \qquad \text{bandwidth } \Delta\omega = \frac{\omega_0}{Q} .$$

{{FIG:resonance}}

At resonance the voltages across the capacitor and the inductor are each $Q$ times the supply voltage and exactly opposite in phase. Nothing is violated: their *sum* is zero at every instant, and the energy sloshing between them comes from the source only through the small resistive loss.

<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Discharging through two paths.</span> [Return →](02.08.html#p2)

(a) $U = \tfrac12CV^2 = \tfrac12(470\times10^{-6})(24)^2 = 0.135\ \mathrm{J}$.

<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Quality factor and bandwidth.</span> [Return →](02.08.html#p6)

(a) $Q = \dfrac{1}{R}\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{C}} = \dfrac{1}{10}\sqrt{\dfrac{50\times10^{-3}}{20\times10^{-6}}} = \dfrac{1}{10}(50) = 5.0$, and $\Delta f = f_0/Q = 159/5.0 = 32\ \mathrm{Hz}$.

(b) The current falls to $1/\sqrt2$ of its peak when $|X_L - X_C| = R$, so
$$f = f_0\left(\sqrt{1 + \tfrac{1}{4Q^2}} \pm \tfrac{1}{2Q}\right) \Rightarrow f \approx 159\,(1 \pm 0.1)\ \mathrm{Hz},$$
that is $143\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $175\ \mathrm{Hz}$ — separated by the bandwidth, as expected.

(c) As $R\to0$ the peak current $V/R$ diverges and the peak gets infinitely narrow; the power delivered is $I^2R = V^2/R$, which also diverges. In practice the resistance of the coil winding, the internal resistance of the source and the radiation resistance of the circuit are always present, and the components have voltage ratings that a high-$Q$ resonance easily exceeds. **Check:** $Q\Delta f = f_0$ by construction, and the two half-power frequencies are placed symmetrically about $f_0$ on a logarithmic scale to first order in $1/Q$.

<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — A capacitor, a switch and two resistors.</span> [Return →](02.08.html#p10)

(a) After a long time the capacitor carries no current, so $R_1$ and $R_2$ form a simple divider:
$$V_C = \varepsilon\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2} = 12\times\frac{60}{90} = 8.0\ \mathrm{V}, \qquad I = \frac{12}{90\times10^{3}} = 0.13\ \mathrm{mA}.$$

(b) While the capacitor charges, the battery sees $R_1$ in series with $R_2\parallel C$, so the charging time constant uses the resistance *seen by the capacitor*, which is $R_2$ in parallel with $R_1$ (the battery is a short circuit as far as the capacitor is concerned):
$$R_{\text{eff}} = \frac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2} = \frac{(30)(60)}{90}\ \mathrm{k\Omega} = 20\ \mathrm{k\Omega}, \qquad \tau = R_{\text{eff}}C = (2.0\times10^{4})(5.0\times10^{-6}) = 0.10\ \mathrm{s}.$$

(c) The capacitor heads for $8.0\ \mathrm{V}$ with this time constant, so
$$6.0 = 8.0\left(1-e^{-t/0.10}\right) \Rightarrow e^{-t/0.10} = 0.25 \Rightarrow t = 0.10\ln4 = 0.14\ \mathrm{s}.$$
The relevant resistance is the *Thévenin* resistance looking out of the capacitor's terminals, not the total loop resistance: the battery is an ideal source and short-circuits the path between the two ends of the divider, so $R_1$ and $R_2$ appear in parallel from the capacitor's point of view. **Check:** the time constant is shorter than $R_1C = 0.15\ \mathrm{s}$ and shorter than $R_2C = 0.30\ \mathrm{s}$, as a parallel combination must be.
