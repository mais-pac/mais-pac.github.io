## 1. From numbers to physics

A table of measurements is not a result. The physics comes out of the *pattern* in the table: a gradient, an intercept, a curve that flattens, a difference that grows linearly. Data analysis is the discipline of extracting that pattern and saying honestly how well you know it.

Three operations cover almost everything you will be asked to do:

- **Linearise** the theory, so that the pattern is a straight line.
- **Fit** the line, using all the points and their uncertainties.
- **Propagate** the uncertainty of the fit into the physical quantity you actually want.

## 2. Linearisation

Almost every school experiment reduces to $y = mx + c$. The trick is to plot the combination of measured quantities that theory says is linear.

{{FIG:linearise}}

| experiment | plot | gradient | intercept |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| pendulum | $T^2$ vs $L$ | $4\pi^2/g$ | should be 0 |
| lens | $1/v$ vs $1/u$ | $-1$ | $1/f$ |
| Ohm's law with a real cell | $V$ vs $I$ | $-r$ | $\varepsilon$ |
| cooling | $\ln(T-T_0)$ vs $t$ | $-k$ | $\ln(T_{\text{start}}-T_0)$ |
| absorption | $\ln I$ vs $x$ | $-\mu$ | $\ln I_0$ |
| gas law | $p$ vs $1/V$ | $nRT$ | 0 |

For a power law $y = ax^n$, take logarithms:

$$\log y = \log a + n\log x,$$

so a log–log plot has gradient $n$ and intercept $\log a$. For an exponential $y = y_0e^{-kx}$, a semi-log plot (log $y$ against $x$) is a straight line of gradient $-k$.

{{FIG:log-plot}}

## 3. The line of best fit

By eye, the line should pass through the "middle" of the scatter with roughly equal numbers of points above and below, and it must not be forced through the origin unless theory demands it.

{{FIG:error-bars}}

Numerically, the **least-squares** fit minimises $\sum (y_i - mx_i - c)^2$, which gives

$$m = \frac{\sum (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum (x_i-\bar x)^2}, \qquad c = \bar y - m\bar x .$$

The uncertainty in the gradient is

$$\Delta m = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (y_i - mx_i-c)^2}{(N-2)\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}} ,$$

which grows with the scatter and shrinks with the spread of $x$ — the mathematical statement of "span the range".

{{FIG:span}}

The **coefficient of determination** $R^2 = 1 - \text{SS}_{\text{res}}/\text{SS}_{\text{tot}}$ measures how much of the variation the line explains. $R^2 = 1$ is a perfect line; above $0.99$ is excellent for experimental data; below $0.9$ suggests either large scatter or the wrong model. $R^2$ is not a substitute for plotting: a curved set of points can have a high $R^2$ and a completely wrong gradient.

## 4. Residuals: how to tell a model is wrong

A residual is $y_i - (mx_i+c)$. If the fit is right and the errors are random, the residuals should wander above and below zero with no pattern. If they show a systematic shape — all positive at one end, all negative at the other — then either the model is wrong (the relationship is curved, not linear) or one variable has a systematic error.

That single diagnostic is often the highest-scoring part of a practical question, because it shows you understand that fitting is a *test* of the model, not just an exercise in drawing.

## 5. Reporting the analysis

For a graph question, the expected answer has a shape:

1. State what is plotted against what, and why that linearises the theory.
2. Quote the gradient (and intercept) with units and an uncertainty obtained from the extreme acceptable lines.
3. Convert the gradient into the physical quantity, propagating the uncertainty.
4. Compare with the accepted value, in units of the uncertainty, and comment on any discrepancy.
5. Mention at least one source of systematic error and how it would shift the gradient or intercept.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">The data-analysis checklist.</span>
Linearise before you plot: write the theory as $y = mx + c$ and identify $y$, $x$, $m$ and $c$ → plot with error bars, label axes with units → draw the best line by eye or fit it by least squares → get the uncertainty from the steepest and shallowest acceptable lines → convert the gradient into the wanted quantity and propagate its uncertainty → check the residuals for a pattern → compare with the accepted value, and explain any discrepancy physically rather than apologising for it.</div>

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Linearising a spring.</span> [Return →](03.03.html#p1)

(a) Square the theory: $T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{k}m$, so plot $T^2$ against $m$; the gradient is $4\pi^2/k$.

(b) $k = 4\pi^2/0.82 = 48\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$.

(c) The spring itself has mass, and the clamp and hook contribute a small fixed mass; the effective mass is $m + m_{\text{eff}}$, so $T^2 = (4\pi^2/k)(m+m_{\text{eff}})$ and the intercept is positive. **Check:** the intercept divided by the gradient gives $m_{\text{eff}}$, typically a few grams — a nice extra result from the same graph.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — A log–log plot.</span> [Return →](03.03.html#p2)

(a) $s = \frac12at^2$ is a power law $s = Kt^n$ with $n = 2$ and $K = a/2$.

(b) The log–log gradient is the exponent: $2$.

(c) The intercept is $\log(a/2)$, so $a = 2\times10^{\text{intercept}}$. **Check:** if the data gave a gradient of $1.94$ rather than $2$, the residuals and the gradient would both suggest either air resistance (which reduces the effective exponent slightly at large $t$) or timing error.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Least squares by hand.</span> [Return →](03.03.html#p3)

(a) $\bar x = 2.5$, $\bar y = 5.0$.

(b) Deviations: $x-\bar x$: $-1.5, -0.5, 0.5, 1.5$; $y-\bar y$: $-2.9, -1.1, 1.2, 2.8$. Then
$$\sum (x-\bar x)(y-\bar y) = 4.35+0.55+0.60+4.20 = 9.70, \qquad \sum (x-\bar x)^2 = 2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25 = 5.0,$$
so $m = 9.70/5.0 = 1.94$.

(c) $c = \bar y - m\bar x = 5.0 - 4.85 = 0.15$. The scatter of the points is about $\pm0.1$, so an intercept of $0.15$ is marginally significant and worth investigating (a systematic offset in the measuring instrument is the usual culprit). **Check:** the gradient $1.94$ is close to $2$, which is the value the data were constructed to suggest.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Uncertainty in a gradient.</span> [Return →](03.03.html#p4)

(a) $\Delta m = \sqrt{\dfrac{0.036}{(4-2)(5.0)}} = \sqrt{0.0036} = 0.060$.

(b) $0.060/1.94 = 3.1\%$.

(c) Widening the range increases $\sum(x-\bar x)^2$ quadratically in the span, while adding points increases it only linearly. Four points spread over $0.2$–$4.0$ would be far better than twenty crammed into $1.9$–$2.1$. **Check:** this is the quantitative version of the figure on the span of readings.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Residuals as a diagnostic.</span> [Return →](03.03.html#p5)

(a) Residuals positive at both ends and negative in the middle form a *U* shape, which is the signature of fitting a straight line to a curve that is concave upwards: the true relationship is not linear over that range.

(b) For a filament lamp the resistance rises with temperature, so $V$ grows faster than $I$; plotting $R = V/I$ against $I$ (or against $T$ if measured) exposes the dependence instead of hiding it in a curve.

(c) Over a small range of currents the temperature changes little, the resistance is nearly constant, and the straight line is a good local approximation — which is why Ohm's law works so well for small signals. **Check:** the U-shaped residuals are the classic exam answer; a random scatter would have no such order.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Interpreting R².</span> [Return →](03.03.html#p6)

(a) $0.9995$ and $0.998$ are both excellent — indistinguishable in practice, and the difference certainly does not decide which model is right. $0.87$ indicates substantial scatter or a poor model.

(b) A parabola sampled only near its vertex looks almost straight, and a straight-line fit through those points can easily give $R^2 > 0.99$ while the true curvature is real.

(c) $R^2$ measures how much variance the line explains, not whether the model is correct: it can be high for the wrong functional form over a narrow range, and it says nothing about systematic errors. Always look at the residuals. **Check:** $R^2$ also rises automatically when points are added, so it is not comparable across datasets of different size.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Cooling data.</span> [Return →](03.03.html#p7)

(a) $T - 20 = (T_0-20)e^{-kt}$, so $\ln(T-20) = \ln(T_0-20) - kt$: a straight line of gradient $-k$.

(b) $\ln(60) - \ln(30) = k(480)$, so $k = \ln2/480 = 1.44\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

(c) At $t = 1200\ \mathrm{s}$, excess $= 60e^{-1.73} = 10.6\ \mathrm{K}$, so $T = 30.6^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The intercept is $\ln 60$, which is just the starting excess — it carries no extra physics unless the starting temperature is unknown. **Check:** the half-life of the excess temperature is $480\ \mathrm{s}$, matching the two given values — that is what the exponential means.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Two variables with uncertainties.</span> [Return →](03.03.html#p8)

(a) $g = 4\pi^2L/T^2$, and $T$ enters squared, so its fractional uncertainty is doubled, whereas $L$ enters to the first power.

(b) $L$: $0.002/1.000 = 0.2\%$. $T$: $2(0.004/2.006) = 0.40\%$. The $T$ contribution is about twice the $L$ one, and in quadrature the total is $0.45\%$ — dominated by $T$ but not overwhelmingly.

(c) To balance them, time more oscillations (say $100$, reducing the $T$ contribution to $0.08\%$) and measure $L$ with a metre rule to $1\ \mathrm{mm}$: then both contributions are about $0.3\%$. **Check:** the uncertainty is set by the worse of the two, so improving the already-good variable wastes effort.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Weighted fitting.</span> [Return →](03.03.html#p9)

(a) Simple mean: $(5.0+8.0)/2 = 6.5$.

(b) Weights $1/\sigma^2$: $1.0$ and $4.0$, so the weighted mean is
$$\frac{5.0(1.0)+8.0(4.0)}{5.0} = \frac{37.0}{5.0} = 7.4.$$

(c) The second measurement is four times more precise, so it carries four times the weight; the weighted mean sits closer to $8.0$, as it should. The uncertainty of the weighted mean is $1/\sqrt{1.0+4.0} = 0.45$, so the result is $(7.4 \pm 0.5)$. **Check:** the simple mean would claim an uncertainty of about $0.45$ too, but from inconsistent data — the weighted version uses the information honestly.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — A complete analysis.</span> [Return →](03.03.html#p10)

(a) $R$ is proportional to $L$, so plot $R$ against $L$; the gradient is $R/L$. Using the first and last points, $(4.26-0.86)/(1.00-0.20) = 4.25\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$; a least-squares fit through all five gives essentially the same value, about $4.26\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, with an uncertainty of about $0.03\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ from the scatter of the points.

(b) $A = \pi d^2/4 = \pi(0.40\times10^{-3})^2/4 = 1.26\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, and $\rho = (R/L)A = (4.26)(1.26\times10^{-7}) = 5.4\times10^{-7}\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$.

(c) Fractional uncertainties: gradient $0.03/4.26 = 0.7\%$, diameter $2(0.01/0.40) = 5.0\%$. The diameter dominates completely, so the gradient measurement is already good enough and the diameter needs a micrometer used at several points along the wire. **Check:** the result is close to nichrome, and the dominant uncertainty is a diameter measurement that takes ten seconds to improve — the classic practical-exam insight.
</div>
