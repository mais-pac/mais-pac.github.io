## 1. Why vectors exist

A quantity that is not a single number needs a direction as well as a size. Force, velocity, momentum, electric field and angular momentum are all vectors; mass, energy, charge and time are not. The whole point of treating them as vectors is that they add by the parallelogram rule, not by simple arithmetic.

The single most useful sentence in this topic: **choose axes parallel and perpendicular to the motion or to the unknown force, resolve everything onto them, and then treat each direction as an independent one-dimensional problem.** Almost every "hard" mechanics problem becomes trivial once the axes are chosen well.

## 2. Components and the algebra of vectors

{{FIG:components}}

For a vector of magnitude $A$ at angle $\theta$ to the $x$-axis, the components are $A_x = A\cos\theta$ and $A_y = A\sin\theta$, with

$$A = \sqrt{A_x^2+A_y^2}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{A_y}{A_x}.$$

In components everything is linear:

$$\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B} = (A_x+B_x,\; A_y+B_y), \qquad k\mathbf{A} = (kA_x,\; kA_y).$$

{{FIG:addition}}

The magnitude of the sum follows from the cosine rule, with $\phi$ the angle *between* the vectors:

$$|\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}|^2 = A^2+B^2+2AB\cos\phi .$$

Two special cases are worth remembering: parallel vectors ($\phi = 0$) add directly, antiparallel ones ($\phi = 180^\circ$) subtract, and perpendicular ones give $|\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}| = \sqrt{A^2+B^2}$ and no cross-talk between components.

{{FIG:plane}}

## 3. The dot and cross products

Two different products appear constantly, and they answer two different questions:

| product | definition | answer | typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| scalar $\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}$ | $AB\cos\phi = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z$ | a number | work, flux, component along a direction |
| vector $\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}$ | $AB\sin\phi$, direction by the right-hand rule | a vector | torque, magnetic force, angular momentum |

The dot product is the "how much lies along" product. Its cross product partner is the "how much is perpendicular" product, with magnitude $|\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}| = AB\sin\phi$ — the area of the parallelogram the two vectors span, which is why the cross product appears whenever an area, a torque, or a rotation is involved.

Both products are invariant under a rotation of the axes. That is their practical value: choose whatever axes make the components easy and the physics does not change.

## 4. Complex numbers: the same plane, with multiplication

A complex number $z = x + iy$ is a vector in a plane, but a richer one, because two complex numbers can be multiplied. Writing $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$ with $r = |z|$ and $\theta = \arg z$,

$$z_1z_2 = r_1r_2\left[\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) + i\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\right].$$

So multiplication multiplies the lengths and adds the angles. That makes complex numbers the natural language for anything that rotates: AC circuits, phasors, wave superposition, and the solution of linear differential equations.

A few consequences that carry straight into physics:

- $|z|^2 = zz^*$ is the square of the amplitude, so the intensity of a wave is the squared modulus of its phasor.
- $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ turns a phase shift into a multiplication.
- The $n$th roots of unity, $e^{2\pi ik/n}$, are evenly spaced on the unit circle — the mathematics behind symmetry arguments and Fourier analysis.

{{FIG:impedance}}

For an AC circuit with resistance $R$, inductive reactance $X_L = \omega L$ and capacitive reactance $X_C = 1/\omega C$, the impedance is $Z = R + i(X_L-X_C)$. The current lags or leads the voltage by $\arg Z$, and the magnitude is $|Z| = \sqrt{R^2+X^2}$. Every "two components at right angles" formula in physics has this shape for the same reason: the components are independent, so the magnitudes combine in quadrature.

<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Components and a resultant.</span> [Return →](04.02.html#p1)

(a) $50\ \mathrm{N}$: $(50\cos30^\circ, 50\sin30^\circ) = (43.3, 25.0)$. $40\ \mathrm{N}$: $(40\cos120^\circ, 40\sin120^\circ) = (-20.0, 34.6)$.

(c) Because the two forces are $90^\circ$ apart, the resultant is $\sqrt{50^2+40^2} = 64.0\ \mathrm{N}$ — the diagonal of the rectangle, always shorter than the sum of the sides. **Check:** the angle between the forces is $120^\circ-30^\circ = 90^\circ$, which is exactly the case where Pythagoras applies.

<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Straight-line motion in two dimensions.</span> [Return →](04.02.html#p2)

(a) The velocity components are $4.0$ across and $3.0$ downstream, so $v = 5.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(b) Crossing time $t = 60/4.0 = 15\ \mathrm{s}$, giving a downstream drift of $3.0\times15 = 45\ \mathrm{m}$.

(c) To land opposite, the upstream component must cancel the current: $4.0\sin\alpha = 3.0$, so $\alpha = 48.6^\circ$ upstream of the perpendicular. The crossing speed is then $4.0\cos48.6^\circ = 2.65\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and the crossing takes $22.6\ \mathrm{s}$ — longer than before. **Check:** a heading into the stream always costs time; that is the trade-off the equation makes explicit.

<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Dot and cross products.</span> [Return →](04.02.html#p3)

(a) $\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B} = 3(-1) + 4(2) = 5$.

(b) $A = 5$, $B = \sqrt5 = 2.24$, so $\cos\phi = 5/(5\times2.236) = 0.447$ and $\phi = 63.4^\circ$.

<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Equilibrium with three forces.</span> [Return →](04.02.html#p4)

(a) Let the tensions be $T_1$ at $40^\circ$ and $T_2$ at $60^\circ$ on the other side: horizontally $T_1\cos40^\circ = T_2\cos60^\circ$, vertically $T_1\sin40^\circ + T_2\sin60^\circ = 30$.

(b) From the first, $T_2 = 1.532T_1$; substituting gives $T_1(0.643 + 1.327) = 30$, so $T_1 = 15.2\ \mathrm{N}$ and $T_2 = 23.3\ \mathrm{N}$.

(c) As both angles approach $0$ the vertical components can no longer support the weight, so the tensions diverge — the classic "no horizontal rope can hold a weight" limit. **Check:** the steeper wire carries more tension, which is the opposite of intuition and a good exam trap.

<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — A phasor calculation.</span> [Return →](04.02.html#p5)

(b) $|Z| = 50\ \Omega$ and $\phi = \tan^{-1}(40/30) = 53.1^\circ$.

(c) $I_0 = V_0/|Z| = 2.0\ \mathrm{A}$, and since the reactance is inductive the current lags the voltage by $53.1^\circ$. **Check:** the numbers form a 3–4–5 triangle, the standard textbook case — and the phase angle is between $0$ and $90^\circ$, as an inductive circuit must give.

<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Superposition with phasors.</span> [Return →](04.02.html#p6)

(a) Two phasors of equal length at $60^\circ$ add to the diagonal of a rhombus.

(b) $R = 2A\cos30^\circ = 1.732A$ — the same structure as the vector sum, since phasors are vectors with a phase interpretation.

(c) Intensity $\propto R^2 = 3A^2$, so the intensity is three times that of a single wave. **Check:** at $0^\circ$ the factor would be $4$, at $180^\circ$ zero: the intensity varies from $4I_0$ to $0$, and $3I_0$ sits between.

<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Work as a dot product.</span> [Return →](04.02.html#p7)

(a) $W = Fd\cos35^\circ = 20\times5.0\times0.819 = 82\ \mathrm{J}$.

(b) The normal force is $N = mg - F\sin35^\circ = 39.2 - 11.5 = 27.7\ \mathrm{N}$, so friction is $6.9\ \mathrm{N}$ and the work it does is $-6.9\times5.0 = -35\ \mathrm{J}$.

(c) Only the component along the displacement does work, and $20\cos35^\circ = 16.4\ \mathrm{N} < 20\ \mathrm{N}$. **Check:** the net work is $82-35 = 47\ \mathrm{J}$, which should equal the change in kinetic energy if the block accelerates from rest.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — A relative-velocity overtaking problem.</span> [Return →](04.02.html#p8)

(a) $\mathbf{v}_{B/A} = \mathbf{v}_B - \mathbf{v}_A = (-20, 15)\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(b) Magnitude $\sqrt{400+225} = 25\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$; the direction is $\tan^{-1}(15/20) = 36.9^\circ$ north of west (equivalently $53.1^\circ$ west of north), so B moves away from A towards the north-west.

(c) The passenger in B sees A travelling at $25\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ in the opposite direction, $36.9^\circ$ south of east. **Check:** relative velocity is antisymmetric, $\mathbf{v}_{A/B} = -\mathbf{v}_{B/A}$, which is a useful consistency check on any such calculation.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Minimum force using components.</span> [Return →](04.02.html#p9)

(a) Along the plane: $W\sin\theta$ down the slope. Perpendicular: $W\cos\theta$ into the surface.

(b) Applying a force $F$ at angle $\alpha$ above the plane, the normal force is $N = W\cos\theta - F\sin\alpha$, and the condition for no sliding is $F\cos\alpha = W\sin\theta - \mu N$ (the force helping to hold it up). Solving,
$$F = \frac{W(\sin\theta - \mu\cos\theta)}{\cos\alpha + \mu\sin\alpha},$$
minimised when $\tan\alpha = \mu$, giving $F_{\min} = W\sin(\theta-\phi)$ with $\tan\phi = \mu$. Note the result is negative when $\theta < \phi$: the block then does not slide at all, because friction alone holds it.

(c) With $\mu = 0$ the formula gives $F_{\min} = W\sin\theta$, applied parallel to the plane ($\alpha = 0$); this is just the down-slope component of the weight, and it vanishes only when the plane is horizontal. **Check:** the $\tan\alpha = \mu$ optimum is the same "friction angle" result as the horizontal-pull problem, which is why the two are usually taught together.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Complex numbers and roots of unity.</span> [Return →](04.02.html#p10)

(a) $|e^{i\theta}|^2 = (\cos\theta)^2+(\sin\theta)^2 = 1$, so the point lies on the unit circle for every $\theta$.

(b) $z^3 = 1$ gives $z = e^{2\pi ik/3}$ for $k = 0, 1, 2$: that is $1$, $-\frac12 + i\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ and $-\frac12 - i\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$, three points at $120^\circ$ intervals on the unit circle.

(c) The sum is $1 + \left(-\frac12+i\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) + \left(-\frac12-i\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = 0$. Three equal forces at $120^\circ$ therefore cancel exactly — the reason a three-phase supply needs no neutral wire when the load is balanced, and the reason a tripod with symmetric legs carries a symmetric load. **Check:** the same argument with $n$th roots shows that $n$ equal vectors at $360^\circ/n$ intervals always sum to zero.
</div>
