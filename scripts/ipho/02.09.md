## 1. What an oscillation is

An oscillation happens whenever a system is pushed away from a stable equilibrium and something pulls it back, with the pull growing as the displacement grows. The simplest and by far the most important model is

$$F = -kx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -kx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -\omega^2 x, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} .$$

This is **simple harmonic motion** (SHM), and its solution is

$$x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad v = -A\omega\sin(\omega t + \phi), \qquad a = -A\omega^2\cos(\omega t + \phi) = -\omega^2x .$$

Two features make SHM special: the period $T = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ does **not** depend on the amplitude, and the motion is the projection of uniform circular motion — which is why phasors, sines and cosines all appear in the same problems.

{{FIG:shm}}

{{FIG:spring}}

The general recipe for any mechanical system near a stable equilibrium is a small-displacement expansion. Write the potential energy, expand $U(x) \approx U(0) + \tfrac12U''(0)x^2$, and read off the effective stiffness $k_{\text{eff}} = U''(0)$. For a mass on a spring, a pendulum, a ball in a bowl or a molecule in a bond, this gives the same equation with different constants.

## 2. Energy and the amplitude

For a spring, the potential energy is $\tfrac12kx^2$ and the kinetic energy is $\tfrac12mv^2$; their sum

$$E = \tfrac12kA^2 = \tfrac12mv_{\max}^2$$

is constant. That gives the useful relations $v = \omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$ and $v_{\max} = \omega A$.

{{FIG:energy-shm}}

Because the restoring force is conservative, an oscillation about equilibrium lasts for ever in the ideal case. Anything that dissipates energy — friction, air resistance, electrical resistance — damps it.

## 3. The pendulum and other "not-quite-springs"

For a simple pendulum of length $L$, the tangential force is $-mg\sin\theta$, so

$$mL\ddot\theta = -mg\sin\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot\theta = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta .$$

{{FIG:pendulum}}

Only for small angles, where $\sin\theta\approx\theta$, does this become SHM with $\omega = \sqrt{g/L}$ and $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ — and notably *no dependence on the mass*. For large swings the period grows: the next term in the expansion gives

$$T \approx T_0\left(1 + \frac{\theta_0^2}{16}\right),$$

so a $60^\circ$ swing is about $7\%$ slower than the small-angle formula predicts. That is why pendulum clocks are driven at small amplitude.

A **physical pendulum** (any rigid body pivoted about a point a distance $d$ from its centre of mass) obeys

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{mgd}{I}},$$

where $I$ is the moment of inertia about the pivot. A uniform rod of length $L$ pivoted at one end has $I = \frac13mL^2$ and $d = L/2$, so $T = 2\pi\sqrt{2L/(3g)}$ — the same as a simple pendulum of length $\frac23L$.

## 4. Damping

Adding a velocity-dependent drag $-b\dot x$ changes the equation to

$$\ddot x + 2\gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = 0, \qquad \gamma = \frac{b}{2m},$$

with three outcomes:

- **Underdamped** ($\gamma < \omega_0$): $x = Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_dt + \phi)$ with $\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}$. The amplitude decays exponentially with time constant $1/\gamma$, while the frequency drops slightly.
- **Critically damped** ($\gamma = \omega_0$): the fastest possible return to equilibrium with no overshoot. This is what a well-designed door closer or galvanometer does.
- **Overdamped** ($\gamma > \omega_0$): a slow, creeping return with no oscillation.

{{FIG:damping}}

The **quality factor** $Q = \omega_0/(2\gamma)$ measures how many oscillations survive: roughly $Q/\pi$ of them before the amplitude falls by $e$. Energy decays twice as fast as amplitude, $E\propto e^{-2\gamma t}$.

## 5. Driven oscillations and resonance

Now push the oscillator with a sinusoid of frequency $\omega$:

$$\ddot x + 2\gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos\omega t .$$

After transients die away the response is sinusoidal at the *driving* frequency, with amplitude

$$A(\omega) = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2 + 4\gamma^2\omega^2}} .$$

{{FIG:driven}}

- At low frequency the oscillator follows the force almost in phase, with $A\approx F_0/(m\omega_0^2)$ — the static response.
- At resonance the amplitude peaks, near $\omega_0$ for weak damping, with $A_{\max}\approx QF_0/(m\omega_0^2)$. Only damping limits the height.
- At high frequency the inertia dominates, the amplitude falls as $1/\omega^2$, and the response is almost exactly out of phase.

Resonance is not a curiosity: it is how a radio selects one station, how a microwave heats water, how a swing is pushed, and how the Tacoma Narrows bridge was destroyed. The same mathematics describes an RLC circuit with $L\leftrightarrow m$, $1/C\leftrightarrow k$, $R\leftrightarrow b$.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">The oscillation checklist.</span>
Find the equilibrium, then the restoring force for a small displacement → identify $\omega$ from $\ddot x = -\omega^2x$ (never memorise a formula you can derive in two lines) → use energy for amplitudes and speeds, forces for accelerations → for real systems, ask whether damping is weak (exponentially decaying sine) or strong (no oscillation) → for driven systems, compare the driving frequency with $\omega_0$ and remember $Q$ sets the peak height and the width.</div>

<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — A mass on a spring.</span> [Return →](02.09.html#p1)

(a) $\omega = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{80/0.50} = 12.6\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, so $T = 2\pi/\omega = 0.50\ \mathrm{s}$ and $f = 1/T = 2.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$.

(b) $v_{\max} = \omega A = (12.6)(0.12) = 1.5\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $a_{\max} = \omega^2A = (160)(0.12) = 19\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

(c) $v = \omega\sqrt{A^2-x^2} = 12.6\sqrt{0.12^2 - 0.06^2} = 12.6(0.104) = 1.3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. **Check:** at $x = A/2$ the speed is $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}v_{\max} = 0.866(1.5) = 1.3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, as expected from the sine of $60^\circ$.

<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — A pendulum clock.</span> [Return →](02.09.html#p2)

(a) $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ gives $L = \dfrac{gT^2}{4\pi^2} = \dfrac{(9.81)(4.000)}{39.48} = 0.994\ \mathrm{m}$.

(b) $T_{\text{Moon}} = 2.000\sqrt{\dfrac{9.81}{1.62}} = 2.000(2.46) = 4.92\ \mathrm{s}$ — the clock would run about two and a half times too slow.

(c) The clock loses $10\ \mathrm{s}$ in $86\,400\ \mathrm{s}$, a fractional gain of period $10/86\,400 = 1.16\times10^{-4}$. Since $T\propto\sqrt{L}$, the length must change by twice that fraction, $2.3\times10^{-4}$ — about $0.23\ \mathrm{mm}$ on a metre, which is why the bob has a fine adjusting screw. **Check:** shortening the pendulum makes the period smaller, so the clock gains; the correction is to shorten it here.

<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — A physical pendulum.</span> [Return →](02.09.html#p3)

(a) $I = \tfrac13mL^2 = \tfrac13(0.60)(0.80)^2 = 0.128\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, $d = 0.40\ \mathrm{m}$, so
$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgd}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.128}{(0.60)(9.81)(0.40)}} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.0544} = 1.47\ \mathrm{s}.$$

(b) A simple pendulum with the same period has length $\ell = \dfrac{I}{md} = \dfrac{0.128}{0.24} = 0.533\ \mathrm{m}$, that is $\tfrac23L$ as the text states.

(c) Pivoted at its centre, $d = 0$ and the gravitational torque vanishes for small displacements: there is no restoring force and no oscillation (the rod is in neutral equilibrium). The general formula shows this as $T\to\infty$. **Check:** $T = 1.47\ \mathrm{s}$ is shorter than the $1.80\ \mathrm{s}$ of a simple pendulum of the full length $0.80\ \mathrm{m}$, as a physical pendulum must be, since its mass is concentrated nearer the pivot than the bob of a "simple" pendulum.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Energy in SHM.</span> [Return →](02.09.html#p4)

(a) $E = \tfrac12kA^2 = \tfrac12(200)(0.15)^2 = 2.25\ \mathrm{J}$.

(b) $U = \tfrac12kx^2 = \tfrac12(200)(0.05)^2 = 0.25\ \mathrm{J}$, so $K = 2.0\ \mathrm{J}$.

(c) $K = 3U$ together with $K+U = E$ gives $4U = E$, so $U = E/4$ and $x = A/2 = 7.5\ \mathrm{cm}$ (on either side). **Check:** at $x = A/2$, $U = \tfrac14E$ and $K = \tfrac34E$; the ratio is indeed 3.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Damping.</span> [Return →](02.09.html#p5)

(a) $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{32/0.20} = 12.6\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$; $\gamma = b/(2m) = 0.32/0.40 = 0.80\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$; $Q = \omega_0/(2\gamma) = 12.6/1.6 = 7.9$.

(b) $\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2} = \sqrt{160 - 0.64} = 12.6\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, only $0.2\%$ below $\omega_0$: the frequency is very insensitive to weak damping, while the amplitude is not.

(c) $A = A_0e^{-\gamma t}$ gives $t = \dfrac{\ln10}{\gamma} = \dfrac{2.303}{0.80} = 2.9\ \mathrm{s}$. Energy goes as the square of the amplitude, so the fraction left is $(0.1)^2 = 1\%$. **Check:** $Q = 7.9$ means about $Q/\pi = 2.5$ oscillations before the amplitude falls by $e$, and $2.9\ \mathrm{s}$ is indeed several periods of $0.5\ \mathrm{s}$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — A driven oscillator.</span> [Return →](02.09.html#p6)

(a) $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{40/0.10} = 20\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$.

(b) At resonance,
$$A = \frac{F_0/m}{2\gamma\omega_0} = \frac{5.0}{2(1.0)(20)} = 0.125\ \mathrm{m}.$$
At $\omega = 60\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$,
$$A = \frac{5.0}{\sqrt{(400-3600)^2 + 4(1)^2(3600)}} = \frac{5.0}{\sqrt{1.024\times10^7}} = 1.6\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m}.$$

(c) When $\omega\gg\omega_0$ the $(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2\approx\omega^4$ term dominates the denominator, so $A\approx F_0/(m\omega^2)$. The force reverses direction long before the mass can follow it, so the mass barely moves: the displacement is then just the acceleration $F_0/m$ divided by $\omega^2$. **Check:** the ratio of the two amplitudes is $0.125/0.0016 \approx 80$, close to $Q$-like enhancement plus the $1/\omega^2$ decay.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — A buoy in water.</span> [Return →](02.09.html#p7)

(a) Pushing the buoy down by $x$ displaces an extra volume $Ax$, so the extra buoyancy is $\rho gAx$ upwards:
$$M\ddot x = -\rho gAx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{\rho gA}{M}} = \sqrt{\frac{(1000)(9.81)(0.20)}{60}} = \sqrt{32.7} = 5.7\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}},$$
so $T = 1.1\ \mathrm{s}$.

(b) For a large amplitude the sides of the cylinder may leave the water or the top may dip below the surface, so the restoring force stops being proportional to $x$; in the usual case where the buoy rises out of the water, the effective stiffness falls and the period *increases*.

(c) A wide buoy has a large metacentric height: the centre of buoyancy moves far when the buoy tilts, giving a large righting moment. A tall narrow one has its centre of mass high and its waterplane small, so a slight tilt moves the buoyancy centre very little and the righting moment is weak or even negative. **Check:** $T = 1.1\ \mathrm{s}$ rises as $1/\sqrt{A}$ and falls as $1/\sqrt{M}$, both as intuition suggests.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Two springs.</span> [Return →](02.09.html#p8)

(a) With one spring on each side, displacing the block by $x$ stretches one by $x$ and compresses the other by $x$; both push back, so the total restoring force is $2kx$ and the effective stiffness is $2k$:
$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{2k}} .$$

(b) Two springs in parallel, both attached to the same face, also give $2kx$ in total, so the period is the same, $2\pi\sqrt{m/(2k)}$. What matters is the *sum* of the restoring forces for a given displacement, not the geometry.

(c) A single spring of stiffness $2k$ is mechanically the same as the pair, so nothing would change. If instead one of the two springs is removed, the stiffness halves and the period grows by $\sqrt2$. **Check:** with the two-spring arrangement the period is shorter than with one spring by exactly $\sqrt2$, as the formula shows.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — The large-angle pendulum.</span> [Return →](02.09.html#p9)

(a) $T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g} = 2\pi\sqrt{1.00/9.81} = 2.01\ \mathrm{s}$.

(b) $T\approx T_0\left(1 + \dfrac{\theta_0^2}{16}\right)$ with $\theta_0 = 60^\circ = 1.047\ \mathrm{rad}$:
$$T \approx 2.01\left(1 + \frac{1.096}{16}\right) = 2.01(1.0685) = 2.15\ \mathrm{s},$$
in agreement with the exact value: the correction accounts for the difference almost entirely.

(c) Energy: the bob is released a height $h = L(1-\cos\theta_0) = 1.00(1-0.5) = 0.50\ \mathrm{m}$ above the bottom, so $v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{9.81} = 3.13\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The SHM estimate $v_{\max} = \omega A$ uses $A = L\theta_0 = 1.047\ \mathrm{m}$ and $\omega = \sqrt{g/L} = 3.13\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, giving $3.28\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ — close, but too large, because SHM assumes the arc is a straight line while the real path is an arc of the *circle* and the real force is weaker than $mg\theta$. **Check:** as $\theta_0\to0$ the two speeds converge, which is the signature of a correct small-angle approximation.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Resonance in a real system.</span> [Return →](02.09.html#p10)

(a) Four springs in parallel: $k_{\text{tot}} = 4(2.5\times10^4) = 1.0\times10^5\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$, so
$$\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{1.0\times10^5}{1200}} = 9.1\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}, \qquad f_0 = 1.45\ \mathrm{Hz}.$$

(b) $\gamma = b/(2m) = 1200/2400 = 0.50\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so $Q = \omega_0/(2\gamma) = 9.1$ and $\zeta = \gamma/\omega_0 = 0.055$: strongly underdamped, far from critical, which is why a car bounces a few times after a bump rather than settling instantly.

(c) The worst ride occurs when the ridge-passing frequency equals $f_0$: at speed $v$ the frequency is $v/4.0$, so $v = 4.0\times1.45 = 5.8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \approx 21\ \mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}$. Lowering $Q$ (better shock absorbers) or changing $k$ or $m$ shifts or flattens the resonance; in practice suspension is designed so that the body frequency is around $1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and well damped, while the wheels have a separate, higher resonance. **Check:** the number $1.45\ \mathrm{Hz}$ is typical of a real car, which supports the estimate.
</div>
