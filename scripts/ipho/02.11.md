## 1. Light as a ray: the limit that works

Geometrical optics is the short-wavelength limit of wave optics. When every object and aperture is much larger than $\lambda$, light travels in straight lines in a uniform medium, and the whole subject reduces to two rules: **reflection** (angle in equals angle out) and **refraction**.

At a boundary between media of refractive index $n_1$ and $n_2$,

$$n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2, \qquad v = \frac{c}{n}, \qquad \lambda_{\text{medium}} = \frac{\lambda_0}{n} .$$

{{FIG:refraction}}

The frequency is fixed by the source and does not change when light enters a new medium; the wavelength does. That is the origin of dispersion: $n$ depends slightly on $\lambda$, so different colours bend by slightly different amounts and a prism produces a spectrum.

Two consequences are worth memorising:

- **Total internal reflection.** Going from dense to rare, the refracted ray runs along the surface when $\theta_1 = \theta_c$ with $\sin\theta_c = n_2/n_1$. Beyond that angle nothing is transmitted and the surface mirrors perfectly. For glass in air, $\theta_c\approx42^\circ$.
- **Apparent depth.** A pool looks shallower than it is by a factor $1/n$, because rays from the bottom bend away from the normal as they leave the water.

{{FIG:tir}}

## 2. Lenses and image formation

A thin lens forms an image according to

$$\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}, \qquad m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = -\frac{v}{u}, \qquad P = \frac{1}{f}\ \text{(dioptres, } f \text{ in metres)} .$$

{{FIG:lens}}

The **real-is-positive** convention is the simplest to use: distances measured on the outgoing side of the lens are positive, $f>0$ for a converging lens, and a negative $v$ means a virtual image on the same side as the object. A negative $m$ means an inverted image.

{{FIG:magnify}}

Learn the six standard cases rather than the algebra:

| object position | image | nature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $u>2f$ | between $f$ and $2f$ | real, inverted, diminished (camera, eye) |
| $u=2f$ | at $2f$ | real, inverted, same size |
| $f<u<2f$ | beyond $2f$ | real, inverted, magnified (projector) |
| $u=f$ | at infinity | parallel rays (collimator) |
| $u<f$ | same side, further out | virtual, upright, magnified (magnifier) |
| diverging lens | same side, closer in | virtual, upright, diminished |

{{LAB:thin-lens}}

The **magnifying glass** works in the last row: the image is virtual, so you look *through* the lens, and the angular magnification is $M = 25/f$ (with $f$ in cm) for the relaxed eye.

## 3. Interference and diffraction

Light is a wave, so two beams can add or cancel. The condition is a path difference:

$$\Delta = d\sin\theta = m\lambda \quad\text{(bright)}, \qquad \Delta = \left(m+\tfrac12\right)\lambda \quad\text{(dark)} .$$

{{FIG:double-slit}}

For a double slit with spacing $d$ observed on a screen a distance $L$ away, the fringe spacing is

$$y = \frac{\lambda L}{d},$$

which is the classic measurement of a wavelength — Young's experiment did exactly this in 1801 and settled the wave theory.

A **diffraction grating** is the same physics with $N$ slits: the maxima are at the same angles, $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$, but they become $N$ times narrower and $N^2$ times brighter as $N$ grows. That is why gratings, not prisms, are the standard tool in spectroscopy.

{{FIG:grating}}

Single-slit diffraction gives a broad central maximum of half-width $\theta\approx\lambda/a$ for a slit of width $a$, with weaker secondary maxima on each side. This sets the **resolution limit** of any optical instrument:

$$\theta_{\min} \approx 1.22\frac{\lambda}{D}$$

for a circular aperture of diameter $D$ — the Rayleigh criterion, and the reason a telescope's aperture, not its magnification, decides how much detail it can show.

## 4. Interferometers and thin films

An interferometer splits a beam, sends the two halves along different paths, and recombines them. A change of path length of $\lambda/2$ flips a bright fringe to a dark one, so counting fringes measures distances to a fraction of a wavelength — the basis of the Michelson interferometer, of gravitational-wave detectors, and of optical coherence.

$$2nt = \left(m+\tfrac12\right)\lambda \quad\text{(bright reflection from a film of thickness } t\text{)}$$

Thin films show colours because the two reflected waves have a path difference set by $2nt$; a soap bubble's colours change with thickness, and an anti-reflection coating is designed with $nt = \lambda/4$ so the two reflections cancel.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">The optics checklist.</span>
Draw the ray diagram first, always — it catches sign errors before they happen → for refraction, decide which side is denser and whether total internal reflection is possible → for lenses, use $1/f=1/u+1/v$ with the real-is-positive convention, then check the answer against the table of six cases → for interference, the whole problem is the path difference → for gratings, $d\sin\theta=m\lambda$ with $d$ the *slit spacing*, not the slit width → check the small-angle approximation $\sin\theta\approx\tan\theta\approx\theta$ whenever $y\ll L$.</div>

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Refraction into glass.</span> [Return →](02.11.html#p1)

(a) $1.00\sin40^\circ = 1.50\sin\theta_2$, so $\sin\theta_2 = 0.643/1.50 = 0.429$ and $\theta_2 = 25.4^\circ$ — the ray bends towards the normal.

(b) $v = c/n = 3.00\times10^{8}/1.50 = 2.00\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and $\lambda = \lambda_0/n = 600/1.50 = 400\ \mathrm{nm}$.

(c) $\sin\theta_c = 1/1.50 = 0.667$, so $\theta_c = 41.8^\circ$. **Check:** the frequency is $c/\lambda_0 = 5.0\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$ in both media, and $v/\lambda$ gives the same value, as it must.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — A converging lens.</span> [Return →](02.11.html#p2)

(a) $\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{15} - \dfrac{1}{40} = \dfrac{8-3}{120} = \dfrac{5}{120}$, so $v = 24\ \mathrm{cm}$.

(b) $m = -v/u = -24/40 = -0.60$, so $h_i = -0.60\times3.0 = -1.8\ \mathrm{cm}$: the image is inverted and $1.8\ \mathrm{cm}$ tall.

(c) Real, inverted, diminished — the camera and the human eye use exactly this case, with the object beyond $2f$. **Check:** $v = 24\ \mathrm{cm}$ lies between $f = 15$ and $2f = 30$, consistent with the table for $u>2f$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — The magnifying glass.</span> [Return →](02.11.html#p3)

(a) $\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{8} - \dfrac{1}{6} = \dfrac{3-4}{24} = -\dfrac{1}{24}$, so $v = -24\ \mathrm{cm}$ and $m = -v/u = +4.0$.

(b) $v$ is negative, so the image is **virtual**, on the same side as the object, upright and four times larger. You look *through* the lens, with your eye on the far side, and the image appears to be $24\ \mathrm{cm}$ behind the lens.

(c) $M = 25/f = 25/8.0 = 3.1$ for a relaxed eye. **Check:** the virtual image is further away than the object, which is why the angular size grows even though the lens does not add any light.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Total internal reflection and optical fibres.</span> [Return →](02.11.html#p4)

(a) $\sin\theta_c = \dfrac{n_{\text{clad}}}{n_{\text{core}}} = \dfrac{1.48}{1.52} = 0.974$, so $\theta_c = 77.0^\circ$ measured from the normal to the core–cladding boundary, i.e. only $13^\circ$ from the fibre axis.

(b) A ray entering at angle $\alpha$ to the axis is refracted to angle $\theta$ with $\sin\alpha = n_{\text{core}}\sin\theta$, and must then hit the wall at $90^\circ-\theta > \theta_c$. The limiting case gives $\sin\alpha_{\max} = \sqrt{n_{\text{core}}^2-n_{\text{clad}}^2} = \sqrt{2.310-2.190} = 0.346$, so $\alpha_{\max} = 20^\circ$ — a numerical aperture of $0.35$.

(c) A thin fibre keeps the ray meeting the wall at many small angles of incidence rather than letting the beam wander; more importantly, a thin core makes the fibre single-mode, so all rays travel nearly the same path length and a pulse does not smear out. **Check:** the index difference is small ($2.6\%$), which is what makes $\theta_c$ so close to grazing — a large index step would guide more light but spread the pulse badly.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Young's double slit.</span> [Return →](02.11.html#p5)

(a) $y = \dfrac{\lambda L}{d} = \dfrac{(589\times10^{-9})(2.0)}{0.40\times10^{-3}} = 2.9\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m} = 2.9\ \mathrm{mm}$.

(b) $y_3 = 3y = 8.8\ \mathrm{mm}$.

(c) By definition $y_3$ is where $\Delta = 3\lambda$; explicitly, $\Delta = d\,y_3/L = (0.40\times10^{-3})(8.84\times10^{-3})/2.0 = 1.77\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m} = 3\times589\ \mathrm{nm}$. **Check:** the fringe spacing is small enough that the small-angle approximation is excellent, with $\theta_3 = 8.8\times10^{-3}/2.0 = 4.4\ \mathrm{mrad}$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — A diffraction grating.</span> [Return →](02.11.html#p6)

(a) $d = 1/(500\ \mathrm{mm^{-1}}) = 2.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m}$.

(b) $\sin\theta_m = m\lambda/d = m(600\times10^{-9})/(2.0\times10^{-6}) = 0.300m$: $\theta_1 = 17.5^\circ$ and $\theta_2 = 36.9^\circ$.

(c) For $m=3$, $\sin\theta = 0.90$, which is *less* than 1 — so a third order does exist at $\theta = 64.2^\circ$. The highest order is given by $\sin\theta\le1$: $m\le d/\lambda = 3.33$, so orders $m = 0, 1, 2, 3$ appear, and at $m=3$ the beam leaves at $64^\circ$, close to the plane of the grating, where it is very faint. **Check:** the largest order is always $\lfloor d/\lambda\rfloor$, and here the integer part of $3.33$ is $3$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Two lenses in contact.</span> [Return →](02.11.html#p7)

(a) $\dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{20} + \dfrac{1}{-30} = \dfrac{3-2}{60} = \dfrac{1}{60}$, so $f = +60\ \mathrm{cm}$ and $P = 1/0.60 = +1.67\ \mathrm{D}$ (the powers are $+5.0\ \mathrm{D}$ and $-3.3\ \mathrm{D}$).

(b) $\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{60} - \dfrac{1}{50} = \dfrac{5-6}{300}$, so $v = -300\ \mathrm{cm}$: a virtual image $3.0\ \mathrm{m}$ on the object's side, with $m = -v/u = +6.0$.

(c) Powers add because they are the reciprocals of focal lengths, and thin lenses in contact share the same incoming and outgoing rays: their deflections add. Focal lengths would combine through the awkward harmonic rule $\frac1f = \frac1{f_1}+\frac1{f_2}$. **Check:** the combination is weaker than either lens, since $60\ \mathrm{cm} > 20\ \mathrm{cm}$ — a diverging lens always weakens a converging one.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — A thin film.</span> [Return →](02.11.html#p8)

(a) At the air–film surface the reflection has a $\pi$ phase change; at the film–air surface it does not. Constructive interference therefore needs
$$2nt = \left(m+\tfrac12\right)\lambda \Rightarrow t = \frac{\lambda}{4n} = \frac{550\ \mathrm{nm}}{4(1.33)} = 103\ \mathrm{nm}$$
for the thinnest film.

(b) Solve $2nt = (m+\tfrac12)\lambda$ for $\lambda$ with $2nt = 2(1.33)(103\ \mathrm{nm}) = 274\ \mathrm{nm}$: $\lambda = 274/(m+0.5)$ gives $548\ \mathrm{nm}$ for $m=0$, $183\ \mathrm{nm}$ for $m=1$ — so no second visible wavelength; the two wavelengths that would match are ultraviolet. (A thicker film would show several visible colours at once.)

(c) As the film thins to a few nanometres, $2nt\ll\lambda$ for all visible light and the condition becomes destructive for every wavelength, so nothing is reflected: a black film. **Check:** this is exactly the design of an anti-reflection coating, with $t = \lambda/(4n)$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — The Rayleigh criterion.</span> [Return →](02.11.html#p9)

(a) $\theta_{\min} = 1.22\dfrac{\lambda}{D} = 1.22\dfrac{550\times10^{-9}}{0.20} = 3.4\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{rad}$.

(b) $s = \theta_{\min}\times3.8\times10^{8} = 1.3\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{m}$ — about $1.3\ \mathrm{km}$, so the telescope can resolve large features but not individual buildings.

(c) Magnification spreads the same blurred image over a larger angle; it adds no information. The resolution is set by diffraction at the aperture, so only a larger $D$ (or a shorter $\lambda$) reduces $\theta_{\min}$ — which is why astronomy pushes to bigger mirrors and why radio telescopes must be enormous. **Check:** doubling the aperture halves $\theta_{\min}$, and a $0.20\ \mathrm{m}$ telescope is close to the practical limit set by atmospheric seeing.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — A prism at minimum deviation.</span> [Return →](02.11.html#p10)

(a) With $r_1 + r_2 = A$ and $D = i_1 + i_2 - A$, differentiating $D$ with respect to $i_1$ and setting $dD/di_1 = 0$ gives $di_1 = di_2$, which together with Snell's law forces $i_1 = i_2$ and hence $r_1 = r_2 = A/2$.

(b) $r_1 = 30^\circ$, so $\sin i_1 = 1.50\sin30^\circ = 0.750$ and $i_1 = 48.6^\circ$. Then
$$D_{\min} = 2i_1 - A = 97.2^\circ - 60^\circ = 37.2^\circ .$$

(c) The refractive index depends on wavelength, so each colour has its own $D_{\min}$ — that is dispersion, and the prism spreads white light into a spectrum. A grating does it better because its dispersion $d\theta/d\lambda = m/(d\cos\theta)$ is much larger and is nearly uniform in wavelength, whereas a prism's is nonlinear and limited by the material. **Check:** at minimum deviation the ray inside the prism is parallel to the base — the standard laboratory way of setting a prism on a spectrometer.
</div>
