## 1. The failures of classical physics

By 1900 three experiments refused to fit classical physics:

- **Black-body radiation.** The spectrum of a hot object peaks at a wavelength that depends on temperature, and classical equipartition predicted infinite energy at short wavelengths — the "ultraviolet catastrophe".
- **The photoelectric effect.** Light above a threshold frequency ejects electrons from a metal instantly, however faint; below the threshold, no electrons at all, however bright. A wave theory predicts the opposite: weak light should take time, and bright light should always work.
- **Line spectra.** Atoms emit only certain sharp frequencies, not a continuous spectrum.

All three were resolved by one idea: energy is exchanged in discrete lumps.

## 2. Photons

Planck introduced quantization to fix the black-body spectrum; Einstein took it literally and proposed that light *is* composed of photons of energy

$$E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}, \qquad h = 6.63\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s} .$$

It is often convenient to use $hc = 1240\ \mathrm{eV\,nm}$, so that $\lambda = 1240\ \mathrm{nm}$ corresponds to $1\ \mathrm{eV}$ — a mental shortcut worth having at your fingertips.

The photoelectric equation is then pure bookkeeping:

$$K_{\max} = hf - \phi,$$

where $\phi$ is the **work function** of the metal — the minimum energy needed to remove an electron. A photon either has enough energy or it does not: doubling the intensity doubles the *number* of photons but not the energy of each one.

{{FIG:photoelectric}}

{{LAB:photoelectric}}

Consequences, all verified:

- A threshold frequency $f_0 = \phi/h$, below which nothing happens.
- $K_{\max}$ rises linearly with $f$, with slope $h$ — the standard way to measure Planck's constant.
- The photocurrent is proportional to intensity (above threshold), not the energy of each electron.
- Emission is essentially instantaneous.

**The Compton effect** confirmed the particle picture further: X-rays scattered from electrons change wavelength by

$$\Delta\lambda = \frac{h}{m_ec}(1-\cos\theta),$$

exactly as if a photon of momentum $p = h/\lambda = E/c$ had bounced off a free electron like a billiard ball.

## 3. Matter waves

De Broglie proposed the reverse: if waves can be particles, particles can be waves, with

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}\quad\text{or}\quad \lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mK}} \ \text{ for kinetic energy } K .$$

{{FIG:de-broglie}}

These are not decorative: electron microscopes resolve atomic structure because $0.1\ \mathrm{keV}$ electrons have $\lambda\approx0.12\ \mathrm{nm}$, comparable with the spacing of atoms, and electron diffraction from crystals shows the same patterns as X-rays.

{{FIG:bohr}}

The Bohr model becomes natural in this language: an electron can orbit the nucleus only if a whole number of wavelengths fits round the orbit, $2\pi r = n\lambda$. Combined with $mvr = nh/(2\pi)$, this gives

$$r_n = n^2a_0, \qquad E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \mathrm{eV}}{n^2}, \qquad a_0 = 0.053\ \mathrm{nm}.$$

## 4. Spectra

Downward transitions emit a photon of energy equal to the level difference,

$$hf = E_i - E_f, \qquad \frac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2}-\frac{1}{n_i^2}\right), \qquad R = 1.097\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} .$$

{{FIG:energy-levels}}

The series have names: **Lyman** ($n_f=1$, ultraviolet), **Balmer** ($n_f=2$, visible — the familiar red 656 nm, blue-green 486 nm), **Paschen** ($n_f=3$, infrared). The same lines appear in emission (bright) and absorption (dark), and the pattern identifies the element.

{{FIG:spectra}}

## 5. The uncertainty principle and wave functions

If a particle is a wave, it cannot have both a sharp position and a sharp momentum, because a narrow pulse in space must be built from a wide spread of wavelengths:

$$\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}, \qquad \Delta E\,\Delta t \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}, \qquad \hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi}.$$

{{FIG:uncertainty}}

This is not about imperfect instruments, and it is not about "disturbing" the particle. It is a statement about what a wave *is*. Its consequences are concrete:

- **Zero-point energy:** a particle in a box can never be at rest; its lowest energy is finite.
- **Confinement:** an electron held within a region of size $a$ has a minimum momentum about $\hbar/a$, hence a minimum kinetic energy that grows as $a$ shrinks — the reason atoms have a definite size and white dwarfs do not collapse at zero pressure.
- **Virtual particles and line widths:** the energy–time relation explains why short-lived states have broad spectral lines.

The full theory replaces orbits with a **wave function** $\psi$ whose squared modulus gives the probability of finding the particle. For the hydrogen atom the allowed wave functions give the familiar orbitals, and for tunnelling through a barrier of height $U$ the transmission probability depends exponentially on the barrier width:

$$T \approx e^{-2\kappa d}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m(U-E)}}{\hbar}.$$

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">The quantum checklist.</span>
Convert everything to electronvolts early: $hc = 1240\ \mathrm{eV\,nm}$ does most of the arithmetic → for photons, $E = hc/\lambda$ and $p = E/c$ → for photoelectric problems, check the threshold *before* computing anything → for de Broglie wavelengths, use $p = h/\lambda$ and $K = p^2/2m$ (or the relativistic form for high energies) → for spectra, an energy-level diagram turns every problem into a subtraction → never use $\lambda f = c$ for an electron: that is for light only.</div>

<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Measuring Planck's constant.</span> [Return →](02.13.html#p3)

(a) $eV = hc/\lambda - \phi$, so subtracting the two measurements:
$$e(1.20 - 0.50) = hc\left(\frac{1}{300}-\frac{1}{400}\right)\times10^{9}.$$
The bracket is $8.33\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, so $h = \dfrac{(1.60\times10^{-19})(0.70)}{(3.00\times10^{8})(8.33\times10^{5})} = 4.5\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$ — the right order, and a real experiment with more points does better.

(b) $\phi = hc/\lambda - eV = (1240/400)\ \mathrm{eV} - 0.50\ \mathrm{eV} = 3.10 - 0.50 = 2.60\ \mathrm{eV}$.

(c) $\lambda_0 = 1240/2.60 = 477\ \mathrm{nm}$. The slope of $K$ against $f$ is $h$ for every metal; only the intercept $-\phi$ changes, which is why the graph is a family of parallel lines. **Check:** with the approximate $h$ found in (a), $hc/e = 1350\ \mathrm{eV\,nm}$; then $1350/477 = 2.83\ \mathrm{eV}$, close to $2.60\ \mathrm{eV}$ given the crude two-point data.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — De Broglie wavelength.</span> [Return →](02.13.html#p4)

(a) $K = 100\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.60\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm{J}$, so $p = \sqrt{2mK} = \sqrt{2(9.11\times10^{-31})(1.60\times10^{-17})} = 5.40\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$ and $\lambda = h/p = 1.23\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m} = 0.123\ \mathrm{nm}$.

(b) At $100\ \mathrm{keV}$ the electron is relativistic ($\gamma\approx1.2$): $E_{\text{total}} = 0.611\ \mathrm{MeV}$, and $pc = \sqrt{E^2-(mc^2)^2} = \sqrt{0.611^2-0.511^2} = 0.335\ \mathrm{MeV}$, so $p = 1.79\times10^{-22}$ and $\lambda = 3.7\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m} = 3.7\ \mathrm{pm}$.

(c) $p = h/\lambda = 5.4\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$ gives $v = p/m = 5.4\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ for a gram — a distance smaller than a proton's radius per age of the universe. **Check:** everyday objects have $\lambda\sim10^{-34}\ \mathrm{m}$, which is why diffraction of a thrown ball is never observed.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Hydrogen lines.</span> [Return →](02.13.html#p5)

(a) $\dfrac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\dfrac{1}{4}-\dfrac{1}{9}\right) = R(0.1389) = 1.52\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, so $\lambda = 6.56\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m} = 656\ \mathrm{nm}$ — the red Balmer line.

(b) The shortest Balmer wavelength is the series limit $n_i\to\infty$: $\dfrac{1}{\lambda} = R/4$, so $\lambda = 4/R = 365\ \mathrm{nm}$.

(c) $\Delta E = 13.6\left(1-\tfrac14\right) = 10.2\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.63\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm{J}$. Momentum conservation gives the atom $p = E/c = 5.4\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$, so its recoil speed is $p/m_H = 3.2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. **Check:** the recoil energy, $p^2/2m_H\approx5\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm{J}$, is negligible compared with the photon energy — the reason the recoil is normally ignored in atomic spectra but not in Mössbauer spectroscopy.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — The Bohr model.</span> [Return →](02.13.html#p6)

(a) Setting the Coulomb force equal to $mv^2/r$ and using $mvr = nh/2\pi$:
$$\frac{ke^2}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r}, \quad v = \frac{nh}{2\pi mr} \Rightarrow r_n = \frac{n^2h^2\varepsilon_0}{\pi me^2} = n^2a_0,$$
and substituting the constants gives $a_0 = 5.29\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m}$.

(b) $v_1 = \dfrac{h}{2\pi m_ea_0} = \dfrac{6.63\times10^{-34}}{2\pi(9.11\times10^{-31})(5.29\times10^{-11})} = 2.19\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = 0.0073c$ — slow enough that a non-relativistic treatment is defensible, but fast enough that the electron is quite relativistic in heavier atoms.

(c) $E_2 = -13.6/4 = -3.40\ \mathrm{eV}$. **Check:** the ionisation energy from $n=2$ is then $3.40\ \mathrm{eV}$, matching the Balmer series limit at $365\ \mathrm{nm}$ ($1240/365 = 3.40$).
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Compton scattering.</span> [Return →](02.13.html#p7)

(a) The Compton wavelength is $h/(m_ec) = 2.43\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m}$, so
$$\Delta\lambda = 2.43\times10^{-12}(1-\cos90^\circ) = 2.43\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m},$$
giving $\lambda' = 0.0500 + 0.0024 = 0.0524\ \mathrm{nm}$.

(b) The photon energy falls from $1240/0.0500 = 24.8\ \mathrm{keV}$ to $1240/0.0524 = 23.7\ \mathrm{keV}$, so the electron receives $1.1\ \mathrm{keV}$.

(c) For visible light $\lambda\approx500\ \mathrm{nm}$, a shift of $0.0024\ \mathrm{nm}$ is a relative change of $5\times10^{-6}$ — below the resolution of ordinary spectrometers, and in any case the electron in a metal is not free. **Check:** the fractional shift is $\Delta\lambda/\lambda = 2.43\ \mathrm{pm}/50\ \mathrm{pm}\approx5\%$, large for X-rays and negligible for light — exactly the pattern that made Compton's result convincing.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — The uncertainty principle.</span> [Return →](02.13.html#p8)

(a) $\Delta p\approx\hbar/(2\Delta x) = (1.05\times10^{-34})/(2\times1.0\times10^{-10}) = 5.3\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$.

(b) $K\approx\dfrac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m} = \dfrac{(5.3\times10^{-25})^2}{2(9.11\times10^{-31})} = 1.5\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J} = 0.96\ \mathrm{eV}$. This is smaller than $13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$, so confinement does not destroy the atom — but it is the right order, which is why atoms have sizes of order $a_0$ and not smaller.

(c) For a proton, $\Delta p = 1.05\times10^{-34}/(2\times10^{-15}) = 5.3\times10^{-20}$, so $K\approx\dfrac{(5.3\times10^{-20})^2}{2(1.67\times10^{-27})} = 8.4\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{J} = 5.3\ \mathrm{MeV}$ — comparable with nuclear binding energies per nucleon, which is why nuclei are about $1\ \mathrm{fm}$ across and why confining a proton more tightly costs MeV. **Check:** using $\Delta x\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$ with $\Delta x = 10^{-15}\ \mathrm{m}$ reproduces the known scale of nuclear binding, a strong indication that the estimate is sound.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — A photon and an electron with the same energy.</span> [Return →](02.13.html#p9)

(a) $\lambda = 1240/2000 = 0.62\ \mathrm{nm}$, $p = E/c = (2.0\times10^{3})(1.60\times10^{-19})/(3.0\times10^{8}) = 1.07\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$.

(b) $p = \sqrt{2mK} = \sqrt{2(9.11\times10^{-31})(3.20\times10^{-16})} = 2.41\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$ and $\lambda = h/p = 2.75\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m} = 0.0275\ \mathrm{nm}$. The check: $K/mc^2 = 2\ \mathrm{keV}/511\ \mathrm{keV} = 0.004$, so the non-relativistic formula is good to about $0.2\%$.

(c) For a photon $p = E/c$; for a massive particle $p = \sqrt{2mE}$, which is much larger for the same energy because the mass is not zero. The electron's wavelength is therefore 23 times shorter, and it is the electron that can resolve atomic spacings — the reason electron microscopes reach sub-nanometre resolution while optical microscopes stop at about $200\ \mathrm{nm}$. **Check:** $0.62/0.0275 = 22.5$, close to $\sqrt{2mc^2/E} = \sqrt{2(511)/2} = 22.6$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — An electron in a box.</span> [Return →](02.13.html#p10)

(a) The walls force a node at each end, so $L = n\lambda/2$ and $\lambda_n = 2L/n$. Then $p = h/\lambda = nh/(2L)$ and
$$E_n = \frac{p^2}{2m} = \frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}.$$

(b) $h^2/(8mL^2) = \dfrac{(6.63\times10^{-34})^2}{8(9.11\times10^{-31})(1.0\times10^{-9})^2} = 6.03\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{J} = 0.377\ \mathrm{eV}$. So $E_1 = 0.38\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $E_2 = 4E_1 = 1.51\ \mathrm{eV}$.

(c) $\Delta E = 3E_1 = 1.13\ \mathrm{eV}$, so $\lambda = 1240/1.13 = 1.1\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{nm}$ — the near infrared. **Check:** the zero-point energy $E_1$ is not zero, as the uncertainty principle demands, and the box confirms it: a smaller box raises $E_1$ as $1/L^2$, so the electron in a $0.1\ \mathrm{nm}$ box would have $E_1$ of order $38\ \mathrm{eV}$.
</div>
