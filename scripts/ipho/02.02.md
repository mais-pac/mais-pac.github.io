## 1. What equilibrium actually means

An object is in equilibrium when it does not accelerate. For an extended body that requires two vector conditions:

$$\sum \mathbf F = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \sum \boldsymbol\tau = 0 \quad \text{about any point.}$$

The second condition is what makes statics harder than particle dynamics, because now the *point of application* of each force matters. The torque of a force about a point is

$$\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf r \times \mathbf F, \qquad |\tau| = rF\sin\varphi,$$

where $\varphi$ is the angle between the position vector and the force. Equivalently, torque is force times perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action.

{{FIG:torque}}

Because the choice of pivot is free, choose it cleverly: put it where unknown forces act, so their torque is zero and they disappear from the equation. This is not a trick, it is the reason statics problems are solvable at all.

## 2. Free-body diagrams that earn marks

A reliable recipe:

1. Isolate **one** body. Draw it as a simple outline.
2. Draw every force **on** it, as an arrow from its point of application: weight at the centre of mass, normal reaction perpendicular to a surface, tension along a string away from the body, friction along the surface opposing relative motion (or the tendency of it).
3. Choose axes. Along a slope, use parallel and perpendicular components, never horizontal and vertical.
4. Write the two force equations and one torque equation.
5. Check that you have as many equations as unknowns.

Common errors that cost marks: putting the normal force at an angle that is not perpendicular to the surface; adding a "centrifugal force" to a static problem; and forgetting the weight of a beam the question said was uniform.

## 3. Friction: static, kinetic, and the critical angle

Static friction adjusts itself up to a maximum,

$$f_s \le \mu_s N,$$

and kinetic friction is roughly constant, $f_k = \mu_k N$ with $\mu_k < \mu_s$. The inequality is the whole difficulty: you often have to *test* whether a configuration is possible rather than solve for a number.

On an incline at angle $\theta$, resolving along and perpendicular to the slope gives

$$N = mg\cos\theta, \qquad f = mg\sin\theta .$$

Sliding begins when $mg\sin\theta = \mu_s mg\cos\theta$, that is

$$\tan\theta_c = \mu_s .$$

The mass cancels. That is why a coin and a truck slide at the same angle on the same surface — and why the result is a clean experimental method for measuring $\mu_s$.

## 4. Centre of mass, and when a body acts like a point

The centre of mass is the mass-weighted mean position,

$$\mathbf R = \frac{\sum m_i \mathbf r_i}{\sum m_i} \quad\text{or}\quad \mathbf R = \frac{1}{M}\int \mathbf r\, dm .$$

For translation, the whole body behaves as a point mass at $\mathbf R$; for rotation, it does not — you need the moment of inertia. For toppling questions, the body is on the edge between stable and unstable when the vertical through its centre of mass passes through the pivot point.

{{FIG:com}}

## 5. Stability: the potential tells you everything

Equilibrium is **stable** if a small displacement produces a restoring force, **unstable** if it grows, and **neutral** if nothing happens. In energy language, expand the potential about an equilibrium $x_0$:

$$U(x) = U(x_0) + \underbrace{U'(x_0)}_{=0}(x-x_0) + \tfrac12 U''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2 + \cdots$$

- $U'' > 0$: a parabolic well, stable, with $\omega = \sqrt{U''/m}$.
- $U'' < 0$: a maximum, unstable, displacement grows exponentially.
- $U'' = 0$: inconclusive from this order; look at the next term.

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — A balanced rule.</span> [Return →](02.02.html#p1)

(a) The rule's own weight acts at the $50\ \mathrm{cm}$ mark, i.e. at the pivot, so it contributes no torque. Torque balance: $200(50-20) = 150(50-x) \Rightarrow 6000 = 150(50-x) \Rightarrow 50-x = 40$, so $x = 10\ \mathrm{cm}$.

(b) Vertical balance: $N = (200 + 120 + 150)g = 470 \times 9.81 = 4.61\ \mathrm{kN}$.

(c) Moving the pivot to $30\ \mathrm{cm}$ puts the rule's weight $20\ \mathrm{cm}$ from the pivot, adding a torque that must be balanced; also the $200\ \mathrm{g}$ mass is now at the pivot and contributes nothing. The balance point changes and the reaction is unchanged at $4.61\ \mathrm{kN}$ because vertical forces have not changed. **Check:** distances from the pivot, not from the left end, are what torque balance uses.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — The ladder problem.</span> [Return →](02.02.html#p2)

(a) Let $R_w$ be the wall reaction and $N_f$ the floor reaction. Vertically $N_f = mg$. Torques about the foot: the wall reaction $R_w$ acts at height $L\sin\theta$ with lever arm $L\sin\theta$; the weight acts at the midpoint with horizontal lever arm $\tfrac{L}{2}\cos\theta$:
$$R_w L\sin\theta = mg\tfrac{L}{2}\cos\theta \Rightarrow R_w = \tfrac{1}{2}mg\cot\theta.$$

(b) Horizontally, $f = R_w = \tfrac12 mg\cot\theta$.

(c) Slip occurs when $f > \mu N_f = \mu mg$, i.e. $\tfrac12 mg\cot\theta > \mu mg$, giving $\tan\theta < 1/(2\mu)$. For $\mu = 0.45$: $\tan\theta_c = 1/0.9 = 1.11$, so $\theta_c = 48^\circ$. **Check:** with a very rough floor ($\mu\to\infty$) the ladder never slips, and with a frictionless floor it always does — both limits match the formula.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Beam with two loads.</span> [Return →](02.02.html#p3)

(a) Take torques about the left support. The beam's weight $40g$ acts at $3.0\ \mathrm{m}$; loads $60g$ at $1.5\ \mathrm{m}$ and $30g$ at $4.5\ \mathrm{m}$. Then
$$6R_R = g(40\cdot3.0 + 60\cdot1.5 + 30\cdot4.5) = g(120 + 90 + 135) = 345g,$$
so $R_R = 57.5g = 564\ \mathrm{N}$, and $R_L = 130g - 57.5g = 72.5g = 711\ \mathrm{N}$.

(b) A single $90\ \mathrm{kg}$ mass producing the same reactions must have the same total, which it does ($90 = 40+60+30$ is false — the total load is $130\ \mathrm{kg}$, so no single $90\ \mathrm{kg}$ mass can reproduce the reactions; this is the point of the question). With the total load fixed at $130\ \mathrm{kg}$, it must be placed where the torque sum is unchanged, at $x$ with $130x = 345 \Rightarrow x = 2.65\ \mathrm{m}$.

(c) A non-uniform beam whose weight acts at a different point changes the torque balance but not the total vertical force; the reactions redistribute. **Check:** $R_L + R_R = 130g$ exactly, as vertical balance demands.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Three forces, one triangle.</span> [Return →](02.02.html#p4)

(a) $\mathbf F_1 + \mathbf F_2 + \mathbf F_3 = 0$ is exactly the statement that they close a triangle when placed head to tail, by the definition of vector addition.

(b) If their lines of action were not concurrent, take torques about one intersection point of two of them: two torques vanish and the third cannot, contradicting equilibrium. Hence all three lines meet at a point.

(c) The triangle has interior angles $50^\circ$ (at the $40^\circ$ string), $25^\circ$ and $105^\circ$. By the sine rule,
$$T_1 = \frac{W\sin 65^\circ}{\sin 105^\circ} = 0.939W, \qquad T_2 = \frac{W\sin 40^\circ}{\sin 105^\circ} = 0.665W .$$
**Check:** the string making the smaller angle with the horizontal carries the larger tension, which is the physical meaning of “nearly vertical is safe”.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Sliding versus toppling.</span> [Return →](02.02.html#p5)

(a) Sliding starts when $F = \mu mg$. Toppling starts when the weight line passes through the lower edge: about that edge, $Fy = mg(w/2)$. Equal force at the threshold gives $y_c = w/(2\mu)$.

(b) For $y > y_c$ the toppling force $mgw/(2y)$ is smaller than the sliding force $\mu mg$, so toppling wins.

(c) $y_c = 0.35/(2\times0.5) = 0.35\ \mathrm{m}$; pushing at the top ($y = 1.8\ \mathrm{m}$) is far above this, so the shelf topples. **Check:** the criterion depends only on the ratio $w/(2\mu)$, so a wide enough shelf slides instead — the reason tall furniture is anchored.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Hanging by a thread.</span> [Return →](02.02.html#p6)

(a) Vertical balance for the load with two symmetric tensions: $2T\sin\theta = mg \Rightarrow T = \dfrac{mg}{2\sin\theta}$.

(b) $T \le 1200\ \mathrm{N}$ gives $\sin\theta \ge 245.3/(2400) = 0.102$, so $\theta \ge 5.9^\circ$.

(c) As $\theta\to0$, $T\to\infty$: a horizontal rope must supply an arbitrarily large tension to produce a finite vertical component. **Check:** at $\theta = 30^\circ$, $T = mg = 245\ \mathrm{N}$, so the rope is comfortable; the danger lies entirely in the small-angle regime, which is why clothes lines sag.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Contact forces between stacked blocks.</span> [Return →](02.02.html#p7)

(a) The top block is driven by friction from the middle one: $ma \le \mu mg \Rightarrow a \le \mu g$. So the whole stack's maximum common acceleration is $\mu g$.

(b) The interface that fails first is the one with the smallest available friction relative to the mass it must accelerate. The upper interface must accelerate the top block only ($\mu mg$ available for mass $m$, so $a \le \mu g$). The lower interface sees the middle block *plus* the top block, but the normal force there is $2mg$ if the blocks stack vertically and the friction available is $\mu(2mg)$, giving $a \le \mu g$ as well in the idealised model. With equal coefficients the two interfaces reach their limit together; if the blocks are stacked side by side against a vertical wall the normal forces differ and the lower pair slips first.

(c) Just before slip, $a = \mu g$ and $F = 3m\mu g$ (accelerating all three with the floor friction neglected or included as stated). **Check:** the answer scales linearly with $g$ and $\mu$, and $F\propto m$, both dimensionally required.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Centre of mass of a composite shape.</span> [Return →](02.02.html#p8)

(a) Square area $4a^2$ centred at $0$; hole area $\pi a^2/4$ at $x = a$. Treat the hole as negative mass:
$$x_{cm} = \frac{4a^2\cdot0 - (\pi a^2/4)a}{4a^2 - \pi a^2/4} = -\frac{\pi a}{4(4 - \pi/4)}\cdot\frac{a}{a} = -\frac{\pi a}{16-\pi} \approx -0.244a .$$
The lamina's centre of mass has moved *away* from the hole, as it must.

(b) For an added half-disc of radius $a$ on the right edge, its centroid is $4a/(3\pi)$ from the flat edge, so at $x = a + 4a/(3\pi) = 1.424a$ with area $\pi a^2/2$. Then
$$x_{cm} = \frac{4a^2\cdot0 + (\pi a^2/2)(1.424a)}{4a^2 + \pi a^2/2} = \frac{0.712\pi a}{4+\pi/2} \approx 0.400a .$$

(c) As the hole radius $\to0$, both results tend to $0$; as the hole grows to the full square, the first formula diverges, signalling that the remaining lamina is not a valid body to balance. **Check:** signs are opposite for removal and addition, as they must be.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Small oscillations from a potential.</span> [Return →](02.02.html#p9)

(a) $U'(x) = U_0\left(-\dfrac{a}{x^2} + \dfrac{a^2}{x^3}\right) = \dfrac{U_0 a}{x^3}(a - x)$. Zero at $x = a$.

(b) $U''(x) = U_0\left(\dfrac{2a}{x^3} - \dfrac{3a^2}{x^4}\right)$, so $U''(a) = U_0\left(\dfrac{2}{a^2} - \dfrac{3}{a^2}\right) = -\dfrac{U_0}{a^2} < 0$. The second derivative is negative, so the stationary point is a *maximum* and therefore unstable, not stable. A particle displaced from $x=a$ runs away; the “oscillation” question is a trap, and the correct conclusion is that no stable small oscillation exists about $x=a$.

(c) $U(x) = U_0\left(\dfrac{a}{x} - \dfrac{a^2}{2x^2}\right)$ tends to $0$ as $x\to\infty$ and to $-\infty$ as $x\to0^+$, with a maximum $U_0/2$ at $x = a$. A particle with $E > U_0/2$ therefore comes in from infinity, passes the barrier and falls into $x\to0$; with $E<U_0/2$ it turns around at the outer turning point. **Check:** $U'(a)=0$ and $U''(a)<0$ are consistent, and the shape $-$ rising from $-\infty$, peaking, decaying to $0$ — matches the algebra.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — A tripod.</span> [Return →](02.02.html#p10)

(a) The three legs are equivalent by a $120^\circ$ rotation, so their vertical components must be equal: $3N\cos\alpha = W \Rightarrow N\cos\alpha = W/3$ per leg, where $N$ is the force along each leg.

(b) Each leg's force has a horizontal inward component $N\sin\alpha = \dfrac{W\tan\alpha}{3}$ directed toward the axis; the three horizontal components cancel by symmetry, but the head still feels an inward squeeze.

(c) If the legs pointed inward the ground would have to *pull*, which it cannot, so the legs must splay outward and the ground pushes inward on the feet. The horizontal force per foot is $\dfrac{W\tan\alpha}{3}$, which friction must balance: $\mu \ge \dfrac{\tan\alpha}{3}$. **Check:** with $\alpha = 20^\circ$ this needs $\mu \ge 0.12$, which is why tripods have rubber feet and why a very splayed tripod creeps on smooth floors.
</div>
