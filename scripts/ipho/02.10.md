## 1. What a wave is

A wave is a disturbance that carries energy and momentum from one place to another **without carrying matter**. The medium — a string, water, air, or the electromagnetic field — oscillates about its own equilibrium while the pattern travels.

Two speeds are easy to confuse:

- The **wave speed** $v$: how fast a crest moves. It is set by the medium (tension and mass density for a string, bulk modulus and density for a fluid).
- The **particle speed**: how fast a piece of the medium moves. It is $v_{\text{particle}} = \omega A$ at most, and it reverses every half cycle.

For a sinusoidal wave,

$$y(x,t) = A\sin(kx - \omega t), \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}, \quad \omega = 2\pi f, \quad v = f\lambda = \frac{\omega}{k} .$$

{{FIG:wave}}

Two numbers carry the whole wave: $\lambda$ tells you how the pattern is laid out in space, $f$ how it is laid out in time, and $v$ links them. In a given medium $v$ is fixed by the material, so a higher frequency means a shorter wavelength.

Important special cases:

- **String:** $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ with $T$ the tension and $\mu$ the mass per unit length.
- **Sound in a gas:** $v = \sqrt{\gamma p/\rho} = \sqrt{\gamma RT/M}$, which is why sound travels faster in warm air, faster in helium, and not at all in vacuum.
- **Light:** $v = c/n$ in a medium of refractive index $n$; the frequency is unchanged when light enters glass, so the wavelength shortens.

## 2. Superposition, interference and standing waves

Waves obey the **principle of superposition**: at any point the displacement is the sum of the displacements of the individual waves. That is not obvious — it follows from the fact that the wave equation is linear — and it is the source of almost all of wave optics and acoustics.

{{FIG:superposition}}

- Two waves of the same frequency add to a single wave of that frequency. If they are in phase the amplitudes add (**constructive**); if opposite, they subtract (**destructive**).
- Two waves of slightly different frequency produce **beats**: the amplitude rises and falls at the difference frequency, $f_{\text{beat}} = |f_1 - f_2|$.
- A wave meeting its own reflection produces a **standing wave**: fixed nodes where the two always cancel, and antinodes where they always reinforce.

{{FIG:standing}}

On a string of length $L$ fixed at both ends, the ends must be nodes, so only wavelengths with $L = n\lambda/2$ fit:

$$f_n = \frac{nv}{2L}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

The $n=1$ mode is the fundamental, and the higher modes are the harmonics — the whole basis of musical instruments.

## 3. Sound: intensity, level and the decibel

Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave. Its intensity is power per unit area,

$$I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2} \quad\text{(a point source radiating in all directions)},$$

so intensity falls as the inverse square of the distance. Because the ear responds logarithmically, intensity is usually quoted as a level:

$$L = 10\log_{10}\frac{I}{I_0}\ \mathrm{dB}, \qquad I_0 = 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}} .$$

{{FIG:intensity}}

The rules worth memorising: a factor of 10 in intensity is $10\ \mathrm{dB}$; a factor of 2 is about $3\ \mathrm{dB}$; a factor of 4 in distance is $-12\ \mathrm{dB}$. Adding a second identical source adds $3\ \mathrm{dB}$, not $6$.

## 4. The Doppler effect

If the source or the observer moves, the *observed frequency* differs from the emitted one. The reason is simple geometry: the crests emitted while the source approaches are packed closer together, so they arrive more often.

$$f_{\text{obs}} = f_{\text{source}}\,\frac{v \pm v_{\text{obs}}}{v \mp v_{\text{source}}} .$$

{{FIG:doppler}}

The sign convention is the only thing to get right: use the upper signs when the two are approaching (higher frequency), the lower signs when they are receding (lower pitch, like the drop in pitch of a passing ambulance).

Note the asymmetry in sound: moving the source and moving the observer give *different* shifts, because sound travels through a medium that defines a preferred frame. For light there is no medium, the two cases are symmetric, and the correct formula is relativistic:

$$f_{\text{obs}} = f_{\text{source}}\sqrt{\frac{1 \pm \beta}{1 \mp \beta}}, \qquad \beta = \frac{v}{c}.$$

For $v\ll c$ this reduces to $\Delta f/f \approx v/c$ — the **redshift** of receding galaxies and the **blueshift** of approaching stars.

{{LAB:doppler}}

## 5. Resonance in pipes and strings

A pipe closed at one end has a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end, so it fits an odd number of quarter wavelengths:

$$L = \frac{(2n-1)\lambda}{4} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_n = \frac{(2n-1)v}{4L}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

{{FIG:pipe}}

A pipe open at both ends has antinodes at both ends and fits any number of half wavelengths, $f_n = nv/(2L)$: it has all the harmonics, which is why an open flute sounds brighter than a stopped clarinet.

The **end correction** matters in real instruments: the air just outside an open end also vibrates, so the effective length is about $L + 0.6r$ for a pipe of radius $r$. In an exam, if the numbers do not quite fit, an end correction is the first thing to suspect.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">The waves checklist.</span>
Which quantity is fixed by the medium? (usually $v$) → use $v = f\lambda$ to convert between space and time → superposition for anything with two waves (interference, beats, standing waves) → boundary conditions decide which wavelengths fit (node at a fixed end, antinode at an open end) → for a moving source, work out whether the crest spacing or the arrival rate changes, and use the Doppler formula with the correct signs → check the answer in the limits $v_{\text{source}}\to0$ and $v_{\text{source}}\to v$.</div>

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — A wave on a string.</span> [Return →](02.10.html#p1)

(a) $v = \sqrt{T/\mu} = \sqrt{80/(2.0\times10^{-3})} = \sqrt{4.0\times10^{4}} = 2.0\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(b) $\lambda = v/f = 200/120 = 1.67\ \mathrm{m}$.

(c) $v_{\text{particle,max}} = \omega A = 2\pi(120)(2.0\times10^{-3}) = 1.5\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, about $0.8\%$ of the wave speed. **Check:** the two speeds are conceptually different — the crest moves at $200\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ while the string itself moves only a fraction of a metre per second, and in a sound wave the ratio is even more extreme.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Harmonics of a string.</span> [Return →](02.10.html#p2)

(a) $f_1 = v/(2L)$ gives $v = 2Lf_1 = 2(0.65)(196) = 255\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

(b) $f_n = nf_1$, so $392\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $588\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $784\ \mathrm{Hz}$ — an octave, then a fifth above the octave, then two octaves.

(c) Pressing at the midpoint halves the vibrating length to $0.325\ \mathrm{m}$, so the fundamental becomes $v/(2\times0.325) = 392\ \mathrm{Hz}$, exactly twice the open-string fundamental: an octave higher. **Check:** the harmonic series of the shortened string is $392, 784, \dots$, which overlaps the second harmonic of the full string — the reason a guitar's harmonic at the 12th fret sounds the same as the stopped note.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Sound intensity and decibels.</span> [Return →](02.10.html#p3)

(a) $I = \dfrac{P}{4\pi r^2} = \dfrac{2.0}{4\pi(3.0)^2} = 1.77\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$.

(b) $L = 10\log_{10}\dfrac{1.77\times10^{-2}}{10^{-12}} = 10\log_{10}(1.77\times10^{10}) = 102\ \mathrm{dB}$.

(c) A drop of $20\ \mathrm{dB}$ is a factor of $10^{-2}$ in intensity, and since $I\propto1/r^2$ it means $r$ has grown by a factor of 10: $r = 30\ \mathrm{m}$, where $I = 1.77\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. **Check:** $10\log_{10}(1.77\times10^6)\approx62\ \mathrm{dB}$, which is $102-40$, consistent with the inverse square law over a factor of 10 in distance (two factors of 2 in amplitude, four in intensity).
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Beats.</span> [Return →](02.10.html#p4)

(a) $f_{\text{beat}} = |444-440| = 4.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$.

(b) The listener hears a $442\ \mathrm{Hz}$ tone (the mean) whose loudness rises and falls four times a second. Adding a third fork at $442\ \mathrm{Hz}$ gives beats at $2.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$ against each of the others, a more complex and slower pulsation rather than a single clean beat.

(c) The tuner sounds the reference and the string together and slackens or tightens the string until the beats slow down and vanish, which happens when the frequencies coincide exactly. Beats turn a frequency difference into a *time* interval, which the ear judges far more accurately than pitch. **Check:** the beat frequency is the difference, not the sum: two waves of $440$ and $444\ \mathrm{Hz}$ reinforce twice per cycle of the difference, giving four maxima per second.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — A pipe closed at one end.</span> [Return →](02.10.html#p5)

(a) A closed pipe resonates at odd multiples of the fundamental. Here $960/320 = 3$, so the two resonances are the first and third harmonics of a pipe whose fundamental is $320\ \mathrm{Hz}$ — indeed $3\times320 = 960$.

(b) $L = \dfrac{v}{4f_1} = \dfrac{340}{4(320)} = 0.266\ \mathrm{m}$.

(c) The series is $f_n = (2n-1)f_1 = 320, 960, 1600, 2240\ \mathrm{Hz}$. There is no resonance at $640\ \mathrm{Hz}$ because that would require a wavelength of $340/640 = 0.53\ \mathrm{m}$, and $L = \lambda/2$ puts an antinode at the closed end — impossible, since the air cannot move there. **Check:** $1600 = 5f_1$, and $5\times0.266 = 1.33\ \mathrm{m} = 5\lambda/4$ with $\lambda = 340/1600 = 0.2125\ \mathrm{m}$, as required.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — The Doppler effect for an ambulance.</span> [Return →](02.10.html#p6)

(a) $f_{\text{app}} = f\dfrac{v}{v-v_s} = 700\times\dfrac{340}{315} = 756\ \mathrm{Hz}$.

(b) $f_{\text{rec}} = 700\times\dfrac{340}{365} = 652\ \mathrm{Hz}$.

(c) The drop is $104\ \mathrm{Hz}$, about $14\%$ of $700\ \mathrm{Hz}$. The change is abrupt because the sign of $v_s$ in the formula flips as the ambulance passes: the frequency is not a continuous function of position at the moment of closest approach, although the *received* signal is continuous — the sound emitted at the moment of passing arrives with an intermediate frequency. **Check:** $756/652 = 1.16$, and the two shifts are roughly $\pm v_s/v = \pm 7\%$, consistent with the small-$v$ approximation.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Moving source and moving observer.</span> [Return →](02.10.html#p7)

(a) Both effects increase the frequency:
$$f_{\text{obs}} = f\,\frac{v+v_o}{v-v_s} = 500\times\frac{340+20}{340-30} = 500\times\frac{360}{310} = 581\ \mathrm{Hz}.$$

(b) With a stationary source and an observer at $50\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$:
$$f = 500\times\frac{340+50}{340} = 574\ \mathrm{Hz}.$$

(c) The answers differ by $7\ \mathrm{Hz}$ because sound travels through air, which singles out a rest frame: a moving source changes the crest spacing in the air, while a moving observer only changes how often the crests are met. Light has no such frame, and the two cases give identical shifts, which is the starting point of special relativity. **Check:** at speeds much smaller than $v$ both formulas give $f(1 + (v_s+v_o)/v) = 500(1+50/340) = 574\ \mathrm{Hz}$, so the difference is a second-order effect.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Interference from two speakers.</span> [Return →](02.10.html#p8)

(a) $\lambda = v/f = 340/200 = 1.7\ \mathrm{m}$.

(b) Directly in front of one speaker, the distances are $4.0\ \mathrm{m}$ and $\sqrt{4.0^2+3.0^2} = 5.0\ \mathrm{m}$, so $\Delta = 1.0\ \mathrm{m}$, which is $0.59\lambda$ — neither a maximum nor a minimum, but close to the minimum at $\lambda/2 = 0.85\ \mathrm{m}$.

(c) Walking far to either side, the path difference tends to the separation $3.0\ \mathrm{m} = 1.76\lambda$. Maxima occur where $\Delta = n\lambda$, i.e. $\Delta = 0, 1.7, 3.4\ \mathrm{m}$; since $\Delta$ runs from $-1.76\lambda$ to $+1.76\lambda$, the values $n = -1, 0, 1$ are crossed, giving three maxima. **Check:** midway between the speakers $\Delta = 0$ always, so the centre line is a maximum whatever the frequency.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Standing waves and resonance in a tube.</span> [Return →](02.10.html#p9)

(a) Open at both ends: $f_n = nv/(2L) = n(340)/(1.0) = 340n\ \mathrm{Hz}$, so $340$, $680$ and $1020\ \mathrm{Hz}$.

(b) Closed at one end: $f_n = (2n-1)v/(4L) = (2n-1)(170)\ \mathrm{Hz}$, so $170$, $510$ and $850\ \mathrm{Hz}$.

(c) The fundamental of the closed tube is $v/(4L)$, exactly half that of the open tube, $v/(2L)$: closing one end allows a quarter wavelength instead of a half, so the same length holds a wave of twice the wavelength and half the frequency. **Check:** the closed tube's second resonance, $510\ \mathrm{Hz}$, is three times its fundamental, and the open tube's second, $680\ \mathrm{Hz}$, is twice — the even harmonic is missing exactly because of the node at the closed end.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — A wave on a string with a boundary.</span> [Return →](02.10.html#p10)

(a) The boundary is a change of impedance, and part of the pulse is transmitted into the heavier string and part is reflected. Since the heavier string is "harder to move", the boundary behaves partly like a fixed end: the reflected pulse is **inverted**.

(b) With the lighter string second, the boundary behaves partly like a free end and the reflected pulse is **upright**. Mathematically the reflected amplitude is proportional to $Z_1 - Z_2$ with $Z = \sqrt{T\mu}$, so the sign flips when the order of the strings is swapped.

(c) A fixed end is the limit of an infinitely heavy second string: total reflection with inversion. A free end is the limit of a vanishingly light one: total reflection without inversion. Real boundaries fall in between, transmitting a fraction of the energy. **Check:** in both limits the transmitted amplitude goes to zero, but the phase of the reflection is opposite — and that phase difference is exactly what decides whether a standing wave has a node or an antinode at the boundary.
</div>
