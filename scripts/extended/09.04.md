## 1. Instruments extend the eye

Astronomical instruments collect more light, resolve finer detail, separate wavelengths and record time. The main choices are aperture, focal length, detector, filter, mount and calibration. A visual observer, a photometrist and a spectroscopist may choose different instruments for the same target.

The practical question is not “What is the biggest telescope?” but “What measurement do I need, and what noise or systematic error will limit it?”

## 2. Exposure time and signal-to-noise

For a photon-counting detector, the signal grows with exposure time. Noise comes from source photons, sky background, dark current and read noise. A simplified model, with $S,B,D$ the aperture totals and $n_{
m pix}$ the number of pixels in the aperture, is

$$
\mathrm{SNR}=\frac{S}{\sqrt{S+B+D+n_{\rm pix}\sigma_{\rm read}^2}},
$$

If the source dominates, SNR $\propto\sqrt{t}$. If the sky dominates, SNR still grows as $\sqrt{t}$ but more slowly. Read noise matters most for short exposures. Doubling exposure does not double SNR; it increases it by $\sqrt2$ in the photon-noise limit.

## 3. Filters and photometric systems

Filters define the bandpass. A V filter approximates visual magnitude; B and R give colour information; narrowband filters isolate emission lines. Photometry requires a zero point, extinction correction and often colour terms. Raw counts are not magnitudes.

Differential photometry compares a target with stable comparison stars in the same field. It cancels many transparency and instrumental trends, but it cannot remove differences in colour, airmass or pixel response if the stars are not treated consistently.

## 4. Spectroscopy and resolution

A spectrograph spreads light by wavelength. The resolving power is

$$
R=\frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda}.
$$

High resolution separates lines and measures velocities; low resolution collects more photons per resolution element and is better for faint sources. The Doppler shift gives velocity: $\Delta\lambda/\lambda=v/c$ for nonrelativistic speeds.

## 5. Calibration and observing strategy

Bias, dark and flat frames remove instrumental signatures. Standard stars set the zero point. Wavelength lamps or sky lines calibrate spectra. Tracking and guiding prevent trailing. For time-series work, keep exposure time, filter, focus and comparison stars consistent.

Plan exposures before observing: estimate SNR, check saturation, choose cadence, and allow time for calibration. A well-planned small telescope can outperform a poorly planned large one.

## 6. Safety and responsibility

Never point an unfiltered telescope at the Sun. Solar observation requires certified front-aperture filters and proper supervision. Lasers, heavy mounts, cold nights and remote sites also require safety planning. Good technique includes protecting the observer as well as the data.

<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Exposure for SNR 5.</span> [Return →](09.04.html#p2)

With source and sky rates both 100 e⁻/s, SNR $=100t/\sqrt{200t}=\sqrt{50t}$. Setting this to 5 gives $50t=25$, so $t=0.5$ s. **Check:** the sky doubles the variance, so more time is needed than for a source-only measurement.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Pixel scale.</span> [Return →](09.04.html#p3)

The scale is $206.265\times4/500=1.65''$/pixel. **Check:** this is coarse for 1″ seeing but may be acceptable for wide-field photometry.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Spectral resolution.</span> [Return →](09.04.html#p4)

$\Delta\lambda=500/5000=0.1$ nm. The velocity resolution is $c/R=3.0\times10^5/5000=60$ km/s. **Check:** higher R means smaller $\Delta\lambda$ and finer velocity resolution.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Doppler shift.</span> [Return →](09.04.html#p5)

$v=c\Delta\lambda/\lambda=3.0\times10^5\times0.05/500=30$ km/s. **Check:** a 0.01% wavelength shift corresponds to 0.01% of the speed of light.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Calibration purpose.</span> [Return →](09.04.html#p6)

Bias corrects the electronic zero level; dark corrects thermal signal; flat corrects pixel-to-pixel sensitivity, vignetting and dust shadows. **Check:** calibration removes instrumental structure before scientific measurement.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Differential photometry.</span> [Return →](09.04.html#p7)

A nearby comparison star shares much of the same atmospheric transparency, airmass and instrumental response, so dividing by it cancels common trends. It can fail if the comparison is variable, too far away, very different in colour, or affected by different pixel response or crowding. **Check:** differential photometry removes common-mode noise, not all systematics.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Saturation.</span> [Return →](09.04.html#p8)

The maximum exposure is $50000/10000=5$ s. A shorter exposure may be chosen to stay in the linear regime, avoid blooming, preserve time resolution or allow for seeing changes that concentrate more light into the brightest pixel. **Check:** saturation destroys photometric information.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Cadence.</span> [Return →](09.04.html#p9)

You need at least 40 exposures: 20 during the eclipse and 10 on each side. At 60 s each, the minimum **integration** time is 40 minutes. The **elapsed** session must still cover the 2 h eclipse plus 1 h of baseline on each side: about 4 h, or roughly 40 exposures spread through that window. During the eclipse, 20 points require a cadence of at most 6 minutes. Readout, filtering, guiding, calibration and weather gaps can increase the session length. **Check:** integration time and elapsed time are different budgets.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Instrument choice.</span> [Return →](09.04.html#p10)

Prioritize stable photometry and sufficient aperture to reach high SNR. High spectral resolution would spread the light too thin for a 0.02 mag measurement unless the star is very bright. A wide field helps find comparison stars but is secondary. Control systematics such as flat-field errors, colour-dependent extinction, focus drift and comparison-star variability. **Check:** 0.02 mag corresponds to a flux change $1-10^{-0.4(0.02)}\approx1.82\%$ (about 2%), so photon noise must be well below that level.
</div>
