## 1. The naked eye is a scientific instrument

Before telescopes, astronomy was done with eyes, patience and geometry. The eye has a wide field, excellent motion detection and a limiting magnitude near 6 under dark skies. It is also subjective: dark adaptation, fatigue, age, light pollution and experience all matter. A good naked-eye observer records conditions, not just impressions.

Useful angular scales: one finger at arm’s length is roughly 1°, three fingers 5°, a fist 10°, and a spread hand 20°. These are rough; calibrate your own hand against known star separations.

## 2. Magnitudes and limiting magnitude

The magnitude scale is logarithmic and inverted: smaller numbers are brighter. A difference of 5 magnitudes is a factor of 100 in flux. Under excellent skies, the naked-eye limit is about 6.0–6.5; from a city it may be 3 or worse.

The number of stars brighter than magnitude $m$ grows rapidly. A simple Euclidean model gives $N(<m)\propto10^{0.6m}$, though the real sky is not uniform. Lowering the limit from 6 to 3 removes far more than half the stars.

{{FIG:limiting-magnitude}}

## 3. What to record

A useful observing log includes date and time, location, sky transparency, seeing or stability, limiting magnitude, target, method, sketch or description, and uncertainty. For variable stars, record comparison stars and estimates. For meteors, record time, direction, brightness and duration. For the Moon or planets, record phase, orientation and features.

A sketch is not art; it is data. Mark north and east, label bright stars, and note the field size. A written description should be reproducible by another observer.

## 4. Techniques that improve the eye

Dark adaptation takes 20–30 minutes. Avoid white light; use dim red light. Averted vision uses the more sensitive rod-rich peripheral retina to detect faint objects. Scan slowly rather than staring. Shield stray light. Let the eye rest between estimates.

Do not look at the Sun without certified solar filtration. Naked-eye solar observation is dangerous and is not part of ordinary night-sky practice.

## 5. Estimating angles and brightness

Use known asterisms as rulers. The Big Dipper’s pointer stars are about 5° apart; the distance from Dubhe (the outer pointer star) to Polaris is about 28°. The Moon’s diameter is about 0.5°. For brightness, compare with stars of known magnitude and interpolate. Record the comparison stars so the estimate can be recalibrated.

## 6. From impression to measurement

A naked-eye observation becomes scientific when it is repeatable and calibrated. State the limiting magnitude, the comparison stars, the angular reference and the uncertainty. If conditions change, repeat the estimate. The goal is not to see everything; it is to know what you saw and how well you know it.

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Hand angles.</span> [Return →](09.01.html#p1)

Six fist widths span $6\times10^\circ=60^\circ$. **Check:** the hand scale is approximate; individual calibration is needed for precise work.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Magnitude difference.</span> [Return →](09.01.html#p2)

The difference is 5 mag, so the flux ratio is $10^{0.4\times5}=100$. Star A is 100 times brighter. **Check:** smaller magnitude means brighter.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Star count.</span> [Return →](09.01.html#p3)

The ratio is $10^{0.6(6-3)}=10^{1.8}=63$. **Check:** a three-magnitude improvement reveals far more than twice as many stars because the magnitude scale is logarithmic.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Dark adaptation.</span> [Return →](09.01.html#p4)

White light bleaches rod photopigments and undoes much of the dark adaptation, which takes tens of minutes to rebuild. Averted vision looks slightly away from a faint object so its light falls on the rod-rich peripheral retina, which is more sensitive in dim light. **Check:** faint objects often disappear when stared at directly.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Angular separation.</span> [Return →](09.01.html#p5)

$28^\circ/5^\circ=5.6$ pointer separations. **Check:** this is the familiar “follow the pointers about five and a half times” rule for finding Polaris.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Meteor rate.</span> [Return →](09.01.html#p6)

The rate is $12/(40/60)=18$ meteors/hour. This is not a true zenithal hourly rate because the radiant may not be at the zenith, the limiting magnitude may differ from the standard, and the observer’s field of view may be obstructed. **Check:** raw counts must be corrected for geometry and sky conditions.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Log entries.</span> [Return →](09.01.html#p7)

A good log includes date and time, location, sky conditions and limiting magnitude, target and method, comparison stars or angular references, and a sketch or description with uncertainty. **Check:** another observer should be able to understand and repeat the observation.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Variable-star estimate.</span> [Return →](09.01.html#p8)

If “halfway” means the arithmetic mean of the two comparison fluxes, the magnitude is $-2.5\log_{10}[(10^{-0.4\times5.0}+10^{-0.4\times5.6})/2]=\mathbf{5.26}$ mag. A midpoint in magnitude would instead be 5.30 mag. **Check:** visual estimates are approximate; record the comparison stars and state what “halfway” means.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Moon size.</span> [Return →](09.01.html#p9)

$10^\circ/0.5^\circ=20$ Moon diameters. **Check:** the Moon is much smaller than most beginners expect; a fist covers many lunar disks.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Light-pollution estimate.</span> [Return →](09.01.html#p10)

The factor is $10^{0.6(6.0-3.5)}=10^{1.5}=31.6$. The model assumes a uniform Euclidean distribution of stars and no extinction or Galactic structure. **Check:** the real increase is large but not exactly this factor because the sky is not uniform.
</div>
