## 1. Two coordinate systems, one sky

Catalogues use equatorial coordinates: right ascension $\alpha$ and declination $\delta$. Observers use horizontal coordinates: altitude $h$ and azimuth $A$. Altitude is measured above the horizon; azimuth is measured from north through east. The bridge between them is the hour angle

$$
H=\mathrm{LST}-\alpha,
$$

where LST is local sidereal time. $H=0$ means the object is on the local meridian.

{{FIG:horizon-coords}}

## 2. Altitude and azimuth

For latitude $\phi$, the altitude is

$$
\sin h=\sin\phi\sin\delta+\cos\phi\cos\delta\cos H.
$$

Azimuth can be found from

$$
\tan A=\frac{-\cos\delta\sin H}{\sin\delta\cos\phi-\cos\delta\cos H\sin\phi},
$$

with the quadrant chosen correctly. In practice, use an atan2 function or a spherical-astronomy diagram. At upper culmination, $H=0$ and the altitude is $h=90^\circ-|\phi-\delta|$.

## 3. Rise, set and circumpolar stars

An object rises or sets when $h=0$. The hour angle at the horizon satisfies

$$
\cos H_0=-\tan\phi\tan\delta.
$$

If $\cos H_0<-1$, the object is circumpolar and never sets. If $\cos H_0>1$, it never rises. Otherwise it is above the horizon for $2H_0/15$ hours, with $H_0$ in degrees.

At latitude 48° N, stars with $\delta>42^\circ$ are circumpolar; stars with $\delta<-42^\circ$ never rise.

## 4. Airmass and extinction

The zenith distance is $z=90^\circ-h$. For moderate zenith distances, airmass is approximately

$$
X\approx\sec z=\frac{1}{\sin h}.
$$

At $h=30^\circ$, $X=2$. Near the horizon the plane-parallel approximation fails: $\sec z$ diverges and overestimates the finite atmospheric path. A common empirical fit is the Kasten–Young (1989) formula

$$
X=\frac{1}{\sin h+0.50576(h+6.07995)^{-1.6364}},
$$

with $h$ in degrees. It gives about 38 at the horizon, but real extinction also depends on aerosols, water vapour, curvature and the observer’s altitude. Extinction increases with airmass, so observations are best near the meridian and at high altitude.

## 5. Sidereal time and planning

Sidereal time tracks the stars, not the Sun. A sidereal day is about 23 h 56 m of solar time. Stars rise about 4 minutes earlier each solar day. To plan an observation, compute the LST at the desired time, find the hour angle, then compute altitude and airmass. Choose a window when the target is high and the Moon is far away.

## 6. Estimating in the field

If you know a bright star’s RA and Dec, you can estimate the LST from its hour angle. If you know the latitude, Polaris gives a rough north reference and its altitude approximates the latitude. Hand angles and a watch can turn a catalogue position into a practical pointing estimate.

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Hour angle.</span> [Return →](09.03.html#p1)

$H=8-6=2$ h, or $30^\circ$. Positive hour angle means the star is west of the meridian. **Check:** LST greater than RA means the object has already crossed the meridian.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Meridian altitude.</span> [Return →](09.03.html#p2)

At upper culmination, $h=90^\circ-|48^\circ-20^\circ|=62^\circ$. **Check:** the star is south of the zenith because its declination is less than the latitude.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Altitude at hour angle.</span> [Return →](09.03.html#p3)

Use $\sin h=\sin48^\circ\sin20^\circ+\cos48^\circ\cos20^\circ\cos30^\circ$. Numerically, $\sin h=0.254+0.545=0.799$, so $h=53.0^\circ$. **Check:** the altitude is lower than the meridian value of 62°, as expected for $H=30^\circ$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Airmass.</span> [Return →](09.03.html#p4)

At 30°, $X=1/\sin30^\circ=2.0$. At 60°, $X=1/\sin60^\circ=1.15$. **Check:** airmass grows rapidly near the horizon.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Circumpolar limit.</span> [Return →](09.03.html#p5)

The limit is $\delta>90^\circ-\phi=42^\circ$. Stars north of +42° are circumpolar at latitude 48° N. **Check:** Polaris, near +89°, is circumpolar from almost anywhere in the northern hemisphere.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Time above horizon.</span> [Return →](09.03.html#p6)

$\cos H_0=-\tan48^\circ\tan20^\circ=-0.404$, so $H_0=113.8^\circ$. The time above the horizon is $2H_0/15=15.2$ h. **Check:** a northern star is above the horizon for more than half a day from a northern site.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Zenith distance.</span> [Return →](09.03.html#p7)

$z=90^\circ-70^\circ=20^\circ$. The airmass is $1/\sin70^\circ=1.06$. **Check:** near the zenith, airmass is close to 1.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Sidereal drift.</span> [Return →](09.03.html#p8)

Stars transit about 3 min 56 s earlier each solar day, so after 30 days the transit is about 1 h 58 min earlier, near 20:02. **Check:** this is why the night sky changes with the seasons.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Azimuth at culmination.</span> [Return →](09.03.html#p9)

A star with declination +20° transits due south, azimuth 180°, because its declination is less than the latitude. A star with declination +60° transits due north, azimuth 0°, because it is north of the zenith. **Check:** the zenith has declination equal to the latitude.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Observing window.</span> [Return →](09.03.html#p10)

The hour angle is $20-18=2$ h, or 30° west of the meridian. Using $\sin h=\sin\phi\sin\delta+\cos\phi\cos\delta\cos H$ gives $h=60.86^\circ$. A quadrant-aware azimuth formula gives $A=242.8^\circ$ (west-southwest), and the Kasten–Young airmass is $X=1.145$. The target is well placed, though past transit. **Check:** high altitude and small airmass make this a good observing window.
</div>
