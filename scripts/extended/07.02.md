## 1. A telescope is a light bucket and an image maker

The two most important telescope numbers are aperture $D$ and focal length $f$. Aperture determines how much light is collected and, in principle, how fine the diffraction limit is. Focal length determines image scale. The focal ratio is

$$
N=\frac{f}{D}.
$$

A 200 mm telescope with a 1000 mm focal length is f/5. For visual use, magnification is $M=f_{\rm objective}/f_{\rm eyepiece}$, and the exit pupil is $D/M$. An exit pupil larger than the dark-adapted eye pupil wastes light; a very small exit pupil makes the image dim and diffraction effects more noticeable.

## 2. Resolution: diffraction, seeing and pixels

For a circular aperture, the Rayleigh criterion is

$$
\theta=1.22\frac{\lambda}{D}.
$$

At 550 nm this is approximately $138.4/D_{\rm mm}$ arcseconds. A 200 mm telescope has a diffraction limit near 0.69″. From the ground, atmospheric seeing often broadens the image to 1–3″, so the effective resolution is usually the worse of diffraction and seeing.

Detector pixel scale also matters:

$$
s=206.265\,\frac{p_{\mu\mathrm m}}{f_{\mathrm{mm}}}\quad \mathrm{arcsec/pixel}.
$$

Undersampling loses detail; oversampling spreads light over many pixels and can reduce signal-to-noise per pixel.

{{FIG:resolution}}

## 3. Detectors count photons

Modern detectors convert photons into electrons with quantum efficiency QE. The signal from a source is proportional to collecting area, exposure time, throughput and QE. Noise comes from the source itself, sky background, dark current and read noise. Here $S,B,D$ are total electrons in the photometric aperture, $n_{
m pix}$ is the number of pixels in that aperture, and $\sigma_{
m read}$ is the read noise per pixel. For independent contributions,

$$
\mathrm{SNR}=\frac{S}{\sqrt{S+B+D+n_{\rm pix}\sigma_{\rm read}^2}},
$$

In the source-dominated limit, SNR grows as $\sqrt{t}$. In the background-dominated limit, it still grows as $\sqrt{t}$, but with a smaller coefficient. Read noise is especially important for short exposures.

## 4. Mounts, tracking and field rotation

The sky appears to rotate at about 15″ per second of time near the celestial equator. An untracked telescope trails stars quickly. Equatorial mounts track by rotating about one axis aligned with Earth’s pole; alt-azimuth mounts need two-axis motion and may suffer field rotation. Guiding corrections keep the target on the same pixels during long exposures.

Tracking quality is part of the instrument. A large telescope with poor tracking can produce worse data than a smaller telescope with stable guiding.

## 5. Calibration turns counts into measurements

Raw frames contain instrumental signatures. Bias frames remove electronic offset; dark frames remove thermal signal; flat fields correct pixel-to-pixel sensitivity, vignetting and dust shadows. Standard stars or catalogue photometry set the zero point. Spectroscopy needs wavelength calibration and often flux calibration.

A measurement is not complete when the exposure ends. It is complete when the calibration chain and uncertainty budget are known.

## 6. Choose the instrument for the question

Visual observation, imaging, photometry and spectroscopy have different requirements. A double-star observer needs resolution and stable seeing; a variable-star observer needs stable photometry and time coverage; a spectroscopist needs throughput and wavelength stability. There is no universally “best” telescope.

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Basic telescope numbers.</span> [Return →](07.02.html#p1)

The focal ratio is $1000/200=5$, so f/5. Magnification is $1000/20=50\times$. Exit pupil is $200/50=4$ mm. **Check:** a 4 mm exit pupil is comfortable for dark-adapted visual observing.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Diffraction and seeing.</span> [Return →](07.02.html#p2)

The Rayleigh limit is $138.4/200=0.692''$. Since the seeing is 1.5″, the practical resolution is about 1.5″, not 0.69″. **Check:** the atmosphere usually dominates for modest ground-based apertures.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Light grasp.</span> [Return →](07.02.html#p3)

Light grasp scales as area, so the ratio is $(200/50)^2=16$. The magnitude gain is $2.5\log_{10}16=3.01$ mag. **Check:** a fourfold aperture increase gives a large but not infinite gain; sky background and detector noise still matter.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Pixel scale.</span> [Return →](07.02.html#p4)

The scale is $206.265\times5/1000=1.03''$/pixel. A 1.5″ seeing disk spans only about 1.5 pixels, so it is undersampled for accurate shape or precision photometry. **Check:** two to three pixels across the FWHM is a common practical target.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Signal-to-noise.</span> [Return →](07.02.html#p5)

The total variance is $10000+2500+100+25=12625$. The noise is $\sqrt{12625}=112.4$ electrons, so SNR $=10000/112.4=89.0$. **Check:** the source dominates, but sky and read noise still reduce the ideal $\sqrt{10000}=100$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Doubling the exposure.</span> [Return →](07.02.html#p6)

If source noise dominates, SNR $\propto\sqrt{t}$, so doubling time increases SNR by $\sqrt2=1.414$. Doubling aperture quadruples collected source photons, so in the same source-dominated limit SNR doubles, not merely increases by $\sqrt2$. **Check:** aperture changes photon rate; exposure changes integration time.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Trailing without tracking.</span> [Return →](07.02.html#p7)

The trail length is $15''/\mathrm{s}\times60\ \mathrm{s}=900''=15'$, a quarter of a degree. This is far larger than typical seeing or pixel scales, so stars become long streaks and photometry or astrometry is ruined. **Check:** even a few seconds of untracked exposure can trail noticeably at long focal length.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Photon budget.</span> [Return →](07.02.html#p8)

The collecting area is $\pi(0.10)^2=0.0314\ \mathrm{m^2}$. A 10th-magnitude star is $10^{-4}$ times the zero-magnitude flux. Detected electrons are $10^{10}\times0.0314\times0.1\times10^{-4}\times0.8\times10=2.51\times10^4$. **Check:** the answer is tens of thousands of electrons, plausible for a short small-telescope exposure.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Calibration frames.</span> [Return →](07.02.html#p9)

Bias frames measure the electronic zero level. Dark frames measure thermal signal accumulated during an exposure. Flat frames measure relative pixel response, vignetting and dust shadows. The flat field is the correction that directly addresses pixel-to-pixel sensitivity and dust shadows. **Check:** calibration removes instrumental structure before scientific interpretation.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Choose an instrument.</span> [Return →](07.02.html#p10)

A 150 mm telescope has a Rayleigh limit of $138.4/150=0.92''$, so it meets the diffraction requirement for a 1.0″ pair. But 1.0″ seeing is comparable to the separation, so a clean split is not guaranteed. Better seeing, adaptive optics, lucky imaging, interferometry or space observation would help more than magnification alone. Magnification enlarges the image but cannot restore information blurred by the atmosphere.
</div>
