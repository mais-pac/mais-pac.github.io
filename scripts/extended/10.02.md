## 1. A graph is an argument

A graph should make a relationship visible: proportionality, a threshold, a power law, an outlier or a systematic trend. Choose axes that match the physical question. Label units, state the scale, and show error bars when possible. A graph without units is a picture, not a measurement.

Linear graphs are especially useful because slope and intercept have clear meanings. If the physics predicts $y=mx+b$, then $m$ and $b$ can be compared with theory.

{{FIG:linearization-plot}}

## 2. Linearization

Many laws are not linear in their original variables but become linear after a transformation. For $y=ax^n$ with $x,y>0$, plot $\log y$ against $\log x$; the slope is $n$. For exponential decay $N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}$ with $N>0$, plot $\ln N$ against $t$; the slope is $-\lambda$. For a small-amplitude simple pendulum, $T^2=(4\pi^2/g)L$, so plot $T^2$ against $L$.

The transformation must be applied to uncertainties too. A log plot compresses large values and expands small ones; error bars become asymmetric in the original variable.

## 3. Least-squares fitting

For a straight line $y=mx+b$, the least-squares slope and intercept are

$$
m=\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2},\qquad b=\bar y-m\bar x.
$$

This minimizes the sum of squared vertical residuals. Ordinary unweighted least squares assumes the $x$ values are exact or much more precise than $y$, independent $y$ errors with equal variance, and a correctly specified linear mean. If variances differ, use weights; if both axes have uncertainty, a more general fit may be needed.

The coefficient of determination is

$$
R^2=1-\frac{\sum(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2}.
$$

A high $R^2$ does not prove the model is correct. It only says the line explains much of the variance.

## 4. Residuals and outliers

Plot residuals $y_i-\hat y_i$ against $x$ or $\hat y$. Random residuals support the model. Curvature suggests a missing term. A funnel shape suggests changing variance. A single extreme residual may be an outlier, but investigate before deleting it.

Outliers can dominate a least-squares fit because residuals are squared. Robust fitting or a stated clipping rule can help, but transparency is essential.

{{FIG:residuals}}

## 5. Uncertainty in slope and intercept

For a simple unweighted fit with residual standard deviation $s=\sqrt{\sum (y_i-\hat y_i)^2/(n-2)}$ (two fitted parameters),

$$
\sigma_m=\frac{s}{\sqrt{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}},\qquad \sigma_b=s\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+\frac{\bar x^2}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}}.
$$

A wider $x$ range reduces slope uncertainty. More points help, but a few well-placed points at the extremes can be more valuable than many clustered points.

## 6. From fit to physical conclusion

After fitting, check the intercept against physical expectation. A nonzero intercept may reveal a zero error. Compare the slope with theory including uncertainty. State the fitted model, the uncertainty, the residual behaviour and any limitations.
