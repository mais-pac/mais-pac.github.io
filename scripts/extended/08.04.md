## 1. Geometry is measurement with a drawing

Astronomical geometry often begins with a ruler, a protractor and a scale. The sky gives angles; a baseline or known distance converts angles into lengths. The small-angle formula is the workhorse:

$$
\theta\ \mathrm{(rad)}\approx\frac{s}{d},\qquad s=d\theta.
$$

For arcseconds, $s=d\,\theta''/206265$. This is valid when the angle is small and the geometry is approximately right-angled.

{{FIG:scale-drawing}}

## 2. Similar triangles and scale drawings

If two triangles have the same angles, their side ratios are equal. A scale drawing preserves those ratios. If 1 cm represents 10 m, then a 3.5 cm line represents 35 m. The uncertainty in the ruler reading propagates directly through the scale.

Similar triangles explain shadow measurements, pinhole images, telescope image scale and many parallax arguments. The key is to identify which lengths correspond.

## 3. Trigonometry and triangulation

For a right triangle, $\sin\theta=\mathrm{opposite}/\mathrm{hypotenuse}$, $\cos\theta=\mathrm{adjacent}/\mathrm{hypotenuse}$, $\tan\theta=\mathrm{opposite}/\mathrm{adjacent}$. For a general triangle, the sine rule is

$$
\frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C}.
$$

Triangulation uses a known baseline and two measured angles to find a distance. The same idea underlies surveying, stellar parallax and some binary-star measurements.

{{FIG:triangulation}}

## 4. Position angle and sky components

On the sky, position angle is measured from north through east. If a companion has separation $\rho$ and position angle $P$, then

$$
\Delta\alpha\cos\delta=\rho\sin P,\qquad \Delta\delta=\rho\cos P.
$$

The factor $\cos\delta$ is essential: lines of right ascension converge toward the poles. A separation in RA seconds is not the same as an angular separation on the sky unless the declination is known.

## 5. Parallax and angular diameter

Stellar parallax uses Earth’s orbital radius, 1 AU, as the baseline: the annual parallax angle $p$ is half the peak-to-peak shift against distant background stars. If $p$ is in arcseconds, the distance is $d=1/p$ parsecs. Angular diameter gives physical size when distance is known: $D=d\theta$. The Moon’s 0.5° diameter and 384,000 km distance imply a diameter of about 3350 km.

## 6. Instrumental geometry and uncertainty

A protractor has finite resolution; a ruler has a reading error; a chart has a plate scale. Convert every reading into an angular or physical uncertainty. If the plate scale is $k$ arcsec/mm and the ruler error is $\sigma_x$ mm, the angular error is $k\sigma_x$. A smaller plate scale $k$ (fewer arcsec per mm, i.e. more mm per arcsec) reduces the angular effect of the same reading error.

The physical length is $3.5\times10=35$ m. **Check:** the scale is a simple ratio; no trigonometry is needed.

<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Small angle.</span> [Return →](08.04.html#p2)

Convert 1° to radians: $0.01745$. The size is $100\times0.01745=1.75$ m. **Check:** a 1° angle is small, so the approximation is excellent.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Shadow height.</span> [Return →](08.04.html#p3)

The triangles are similar, so $h/12=1/0.8$. Thus $h=15$ m. **Check:** the tree’s shadow is 15 times the stick’s shadow, so its height is 15 times the stick’s height.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Triangulation.</span> [Return →](08.04.html#p4)

The third angle is $180-60-70=50^\circ$. The side opposite 70° is the distance from the 60° endpoint to the target. By the sine rule, $d=100\sin70^\circ/\sin50^\circ=122.7$ m. **Check:** the larger angle faces the longer side.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Position angle components.</span> [Return →](08.04.html#p5)

$\Delta\alpha\cos\delta=10\sin45^\circ=7.07''$ and $\Delta\delta=10\cos45^\circ=7.07''$. **Check:** at 45° the east and north components are equal.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Plate scale.</span> [Return →](08.04.html#p6)

The separation is $15\times2=30''$. The uncertainty is $0.5\times2=1''$. **Check:** the same ruler error produces a smaller angular error on a finer-scale chart.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Parallax.</span> [Return →](08.04.html#p7)

$d=1/0.1=10$ pc. In light-years this is $10\times3.26=32.6$ ly. **Check:** smaller parallax means larger distance.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Angular diameter.</span> [Return →](08.04.html#p8)

Convert 0.5° to radians: $0.008727$. The diameter is $384000\times0.008727=3350$ km. **Check:** the accepted lunar diameter is about 3474 km; the difference comes from the rounded angular diameter and distance.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — RA conversion.</span> [Return →](08.04.html#p9)

One second of RA is 15″ of angle along the equator. At declination 60°, the east–west separation is $15''\cos60^\circ=7.5''$. **Check:** near the pole, RA lines converge, so the same RA difference corresponds to a smaller sky angle.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Design a measurement.</span> [Return →](08.04.html#p10)

Measure the horizontal distance $d$ from the building and the elevation angle $\theta$ to the top. The height is $h=d\tan\theta$ plus the instrument height above the ground. Reduce uncertainty by measuring from farther away so the angle is not too close to 90°, repeating the angle measurement, and ensuring the tape is horizontal. State the protractor resolution and propagate it through $\tan\theta$.
