## 1. Why numerical methods exist

Many astronomical equations cannot be solved neatly with algebra. Kepler’s equation, orbital fitting, radiative transfer and light-curve modelling often require iteration. A numerical method is a controlled recipe for producing a better answer step by step. The goal is not merely to get a number; it is to know how accurate the number is and whether the method is stable.

Three questions should always be asked: Does the method converge? How fast? What is the residual or error estimate?

## 2. Root finding: bisection and Newton’s method

To solve $f(x)=0$, bisection starts with an interval where $f$ changes sign. Each step halves the interval. It is slow but robust. After $n$ steps, the interval width is $(b-a)/2^n$.

Newton’s method uses the tangent line:

$$
x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}.
$$

It can converge very quickly near a simple root, but it can fail if the derivative is small, the initial guess is poor, or the function is badly behaved. A good practice is to combine a robust bracket with a fast local method.

## 3. Kepler’s equation as a worked example

For an elliptical orbit, the mean anomaly $M$ and eccentric anomaly $E$ satisfy

$$
M=E-e\sin E.
$$

This cannot be inverted with elementary functions. Newton’s method gives

$$
E_{n+1}=E_n-\frac{E_n-e\sin E_n-M}{1-e\cos E_n}.
$$

For modest eccentricity, $E_0=M$ is often a good start. For high eccentricity and small $M$, convergence can be slower and a better initial guess or bracketing helps.

## 4. Numerical differentiation and integration

A central difference approximates a derivative:

$$
f'(x)\approx\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}.
$$

If $h$ is too large, truncation error dominates; if $h$ is too small, round-off error dominates. There is usually an optimal intermediate step.

Numerical integration adds many small contributions. The trapezoidal rule uses straight-line segments; Composite Simpson’s rule uses parabolic segments, requires an even number $n$ of equal subintervals, and is usually more accurate for smooth functions:

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\approx\frac{h}{3}\left[f_0+4f_1+2f_2+\cdots+4f_{n-1}+f_n\right].
$$

## 5. Differential equations and time stepping

Many physical laws are differential equations. The simplest time stepper is Euler’s method:

$$
y_{n+1}=y_n+h\,f(t_n,y_n).
$$

It is easy but can be inaccurate or unstable. Runge–Kutta methods sample the slope several times per step and are much more reliable. In orbital mechanics, energy conservation and long-term stability often matter more than the error in a single step.

## 6. Error, convergence and sanity checks

A numerical answer should be accompanied by a residual, step-size test or convergence test. Halve the step size and see whether the answer changes by the expected amount. Check limiting cases: circular orbit, zero noise, constant function, or known analytic solution.

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Bisection width.</span> [Return →](08.01.html#p1)

The width after $n$ steps is $2/2^n$. We need $2/2^n<10^{-3}$, so $2^n>2000$. Since $2^{10}=1024$ and $2^{11}=2048$, **11 steps** are enough. **Check:** bisection gains one binary digit of accuracy per step.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — One Newton step.</span> [Return →](08.01.html#p2)

For $f(x)=x^2-2$, $f'(x)=2x$. Thus $x_1=1.5-(2.25-2)/3=1.41667$. The exact root is $\sqrt2=1.41421$, so the error is about 0.00245. **Check:** one step reduces the error substantially because Newton’s method is quadratic near the root, but this iterate is not yet accurate to three decimal places.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Kepler’s equation.</span> [Return →](08.01.html#p3)

At $E_0=1.0$, $f=1.0-0.5\sin1-1.0=-0.4207$ and $f'=1-0.5\cos1=0.7297$. The Newton step gives $E_1=1.0-(-0.4207)/0.7297=1.5766$ rad. The residual is $E_1-0.5\sin E_1-1.0\approx0.0766$. **Check:** the residual is much smaller than the initial residual, but more iterations are needed for high precision.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Central difference.</span> [Return →](08.01.html#p4)

The estimate is $[(3.01)^2-(2.99)^2]/0.02=(9.0601-8.9401)/0.02=6.000$. The exact derivative is $2x=6$. **Check:** the central difference is exact for a quadratic because the symmetric truncation error cancels.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Trapezoidal rule.</span> [Return →](08.01.html#p5)

With $h=0.5$, the estimate is $0.5[0.5f(0)+f(0.5)+0.5f(1)]=0.5[0+0.25+0.5]=0.375$. The exact integral is $1/3=0.3333$. The trapezoidal estimate is high because $x^2$ is convex. **Check:** straight chords lie above a convex curve.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Simpson’s rule.</span> [Return →](08.01.html#p6)

Simpson’s rule gives $(1/6)[f(0)+4f(0.5)+f(1)]=(1/6)[0+1+1]=1/3$. It is exact because Simpson’s rule integrates quadratic polynomials exactly. **Check:** the method matches the analytic integral.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Euler decay.</span> [Return →](08.01.html#p7)

Euler gives $y_1=1-0.1=0.9$ and $y_2=0.9-0.09=0.81$. The exact value is $e^{-0.2}=0.8187$. Euler is low by about 0.0087. **Check:** Euler underestimates decay here because it uses the slope at the beginning of each step.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Fixed-point iteration.</span> [Return →](08.01.html#p8)

Start with $E_0=0.5$. Then $E_1=0.5+0.2\sin0.5=0.5959$. Next $E_2=0.5+0.2\sin0.5959=0.6123$. **Check:** the iteration converges because $|e\cos E|<1$ for $e=0.2$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Convergence order.</span> [Return →](08.01.html#p9)

The errors are roughly squared at each step: $10^{-1}\to10^{-2}\to10^{-4}\to10^{-8}$. This is quadratic convergence. Newton’s method behaves this way near a simple root because the tangent approximation captures the function to first order, leaving an error proportional to the square of the previous error.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Choose a method.</span> [Return →](08.01.html#p10)

A hybrid is best. Use a coarse search or bracket to locate the minimum or root robustly, then use Newton or a quasi-Newton method if derivatives are available and the model is smooth. If derivatives are expensive, a secant or Brent-type method may be preferable. A safeguard is to reject steps that leave the bracket or increase the residual, and to verify convergence by changing the tolerance or initial guess.
</div>
