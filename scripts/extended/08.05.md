## 1. A measurement is a number with a doubt

No astronomical measurement is exact. Statistics tells us how to summarize repeated readings; error analysis tells us how uncertainty moves through a calculation. A result should be written as $x\pm\sigma_x$ with units, and the meaning of $\sigma_x$ should be clear: standard deviation of individual readings, standard error of the mean, or a combined uncertainty.

{{FIG:distributions}}

## 2. Mean, median, spread and standard error

For $n$ readings $x_i$,

$$
\bar x=\frac{1}{n}\sum x_i,\qquad s=\sqrt{\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}{n-1}},\qquad \sigma_{\bar x}=\frac{s}{\sqrt n}.
$$

The mean is sensitive to outliers; the median is robust. The standard deviation describes the scatter of individual readings. The standard error describes how well the mean is determined. Increasing $n$ reduces the standard error as $1/\sqrt n$, but it does not remove a systematic offset.

## 3. Propagation of uncertainty

For independent random errors,

$$
\sigma_f^2\approx\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)^2\sigma_x^2+\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)^2\sigma_y^2.
$$

For sums and differences, absolute uncertainties add in quadrature. For products and powers, fractional uncertainties add in quadrature with the appropriate powers. If $f=x^a y^b$, then

$$
\left(\frac{\sigma_f}{f}\right)^2\approx a^2\left(\frac{\sigma_x}{x}\right)^2+b^2\left(\frac{\sigma_y}{y}\right)^2.
$$

Known systematic offsets must be corrected, not averaged away. Independent, zero-mean standard uncertainties (including the residual uncertainty of a calibration) may be combined in quadrature; correlated terms require covariance or a separate systematic budget.

{{FIG:error-propagation}}

## 4. Distributions and confidence

Many measurement errors are approximately Gaussian. For a normal distribution, about 68% of values lie within one standard deviation, 95% within two, and 99.7% within three. These are useful rules of thumb, not guarantees. Small samples, outliers and non-Gaussian noise require caution.

A confidence interval describes the reliability of an estimation procedure, not the probability that a fixed true value is “random.” In Olympiad work, it is usually enough to state the uncertainty and the assumptions behind it.

## 5. Weighted means and outliers

If measurements have different uncertainties, weight them by inverse variance:

$$
\bar x_w=\frac{\sum w_i x_i}{\sum w_i},\qquad w_i=\frac{1}{\sigma_i^2},\qquad \sigma_{\bar x_w}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum w_i}}.
$$

Outliers should be investigated, not silently deleted. A bad point may reveal a cloud, a cosmic ray, a misidentified star or a transcription error. Robust statistics such as the median or a clipped mean can help, but the clipping rule must be stated.

## 6. Build an uncertainty budget

List every important source: photon noise, sky background, read noise, calibration, atmospheric extinction, timing, ruler reading, model assumptions. Correct known offsets, then combine independent, zero-mean standard uncertainties in quadrature; keep correlated systematics separate. Identify the dominant term. If one term dominates, improving the others will not help much.

<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Mean and standard error.</span> [Return →](08.05.html#p1)

The mean is 10.0. The squared deviations are 0.01, 0.01, 0.04, 0, 0.04, summing to 0.10. The sample standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.10/4}=0.158$. The standard error is $0.158/\sqrt5=0.071$. **Check:** the mean is more precise than a single reading.

<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">

<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Systematic offset.</span> [Return →](08.05.html#p8)
