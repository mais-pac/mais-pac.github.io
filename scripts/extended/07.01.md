## 1. One spectrum, many messengers

Astronomy is mostly the art of catching light. Radio waves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are all electromagnetic radiation; they differ in wavelength, frequency and photon energy. The relations are

$$
c=\lambda\nu,\qquad E=h\nu=\frac{hc}{\lambda}.
$$

Use $c=2.998\times10^8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $h=6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$, and $hc=1240\ \mathrm{eV\,nm}$. A convenient rule is that shorter wavelength means higher photon energy. A 500 nm photon carries about 2.48 eV, while a 1 keV X-ray photon carries about 400 times more energy.

Different bands are not merely “colours.” They trace different physical conditions: cold dust emits in the far-infrared, stars peak in the optical or ultraviolet, hot gas emits X-rays, and relativistic particles can produce radio synchrotron emission.

## 2. Thermal radiation and Wien’s law

A dense warm object emits a broad thermal spectrum. For an ideal blackbody, the wavelength of maximum emission is approximately

$$
\lambda_{\max}T=2.898\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,K}.
$$

The Sun, near 5800 K, peaks around 500 nm. A 300 K planet peaks near 10 μm. A 10 K dust cloud peaks near 290 μm. The peak is only a guide: real objects have absorption lines, emission lines, dust features and non-thermal components.

The total thermal power per unit area scales as $T^4$. Doubling the temperature increases the emitted flux by a factor of 16. This is why temperature is such a powerful diagnostic.

## 3. Redshift moves the whole spectrum

Cosmological redshift stretches every wavelength by the same factor:

$$
\lambda_{\rm obs}=\lambda_{\rm rest}(1+z).
$$

A rest-frame ultraviolet line at 121.6 nm observed at 608 nm implies $z=4$. The same factor applies to a thermal peak. This is why high-redshift galaxies are often studied in the infrared: their rest-frame optical and ultraviolet light has been shifted out of the visible band.

Redshift does not change the identity of a spectral line; it changes the observed wavelength. Identifying several lines with the same $z$ is much stronger evidence than matching one feature.

## 4. The atmosphere is a selective filter

The ground is not equally transparent at all wavelengths. The optical window is broad, radio windows are excellent, and some near-infrared windows are usable from dry sites. Much ultraviolet, X-ray and gamma-ray astronomy must be done from space. Water vapour, ozone, oxygen and carbon dioxide shape the transmission.

This is not a minor engineering detail. It determines which questions can be answered from the ground and which require balloons, rockets or satellites. A “missing” band is often an atmospheric effect, not an absence of astrophysical emission.

## 5. Extinction, emission and diagnostic power

Dust scatters and absorbs short wavelengths more efficiently than long wavelengths. In magnitudes, an extinction $A_V=2$ reduces the observed flux by $10^{0.4A_V}=6.31$. Infrared observations can therefore see through dust that blocks visible light.

Spectral lines add chemical and kinematic information. The Doppler shift gives velocity; line ratios can give temperature, density or ionization state. Radio 21 cm emission traces neutral hydrogen; molecular lines trace cold gas; X-ray lines trace very hot plasma.

## 6. Build a multi-wavelength argument

A good multi-wavelength study asks what each band contributes. Optical images show stars and morphology; infrared reveals dust and cool objects; radio traces gas and magnetic fields; X-rays reveal hot gas and compact objects. The bands should be compared with matched angular resolution, calibration and aperture.

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — A visible photon.</span> [Return →](07.01.html#p1)

Use $\nu=c/\lambda=2.998\times10^8/(5.00\times10^{-7})=5.996\times10^{14}$ Hz. The energy is $E=h\nu=3.97\times10^{-19}$ J. Since $hc=1240$ eV nm, $E=1240/500=2.48$ eV. **Check:** visible photons are a few eV, so the answer is physically reasonable.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Two thermal peaks.</span> [Return →](07.01.html#p2)

Wien’s law gives $T=2.898\times10^{-3}/\lambda_{\max}$. For 500 nm, $T=5796$ K, close to the Sun’s effective temperature. For 10 μm, $T=289.8$ K, close to room temperature. **Check:** hotter objects peak at shorter wavelengths.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — A shifted line.</span> [Return →](07.01.html#p3)

The redshift is $1+z=608/121.6=5$, so $z=4$. Every rest wavelength is multiplied by 5, so 500 nm is observed at 2500 nm, in the infrared. **Check:** the same factor applies to all features from the same source.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Magnitude contrast.</span> [Return →](07.01.html#p4)

The magnitude relation is $m_1-m_2=-2.5\log_{10}(F_1/F_2)$. A difference of 5 mag corresponds to a flux ratio of $10^{0.4\times5}=100$. The smaller magnitude is brighter. **Check:** five magnitudes is exactly the historical factor of 100.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Ground or space?</span> [Return →](07.01.html#p5)

21 cm radio is ground-accessible because the atmosphere is transparent at many radio wavelengths. 550 nm optical is ground-accessible through the optical window. 200 nm ultraviolet is strongly absorbed by ozone and generally requires space. 1 keV X-rays are absorbed by the atmosphere and require space. **Check:** the decision depends on atmospheric transmission, not on whether the source is bright.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Dust penalty.</span> [Return →](07.01.html#p6)

Flux is reduced by $10^{0.4A_V}=10^{0.8}=6.31$. Only about 16% of the visual flux remains. Dust extinction is usually weaker at longer wavelengths, so infrared observations can penetrate dusty regions more effectively. **Check:** larger extinction in magnitudes means a larger flux reduction.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Counting X-rays.</span> [Return →](07.01.html#p7)

One 1 keV photon has energy $1000\times1.602\times10^{-19}=1.602\times10^{-16}$ J. The rate is $10^{-15}/1.602\times10^{-16}=6.24$ photons/s. X-ray sources are often faint enough that individual photon events are counted, and each photon’s energy can be recorded. **Check:** a few photons per second is a plausible faint X-ray count rate.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Radio versus optical resolution.</span> [Return →](07.01.html#p8)

Use $\theta=1.22\lambda/D$ in radians and multiply by 206265. For the radio dish, $\theta=1.22(0.21)/25=0.01025$ rad $=2110''$, about 0.59°. For the optical telescope, $\theta=1.22(5.0\times10^{-7})/0.10=6.10\times10^{-6}$ rad $=1.26''$. **Check:** long wavelengths need much larger apertures or interferometry for fine resolution.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — A high-redshift thermal peak.</span> [Return →](07.01.html#p9)

The rest-frame peak is $2.898\times10^{-3}/10^4=2.898\times10^{-7}$ m, or 290 nm, in the ultraviolet. At $z=2$, the observed peak is $3\times290=870$ nm, in the near-infrared. **Check:** hot stars can have their rest-frame UV light shifted into the infrared by cosmological expansion.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Design a campaign.</span> [Return →](07.01.html#p10)

Infrared imaging and spectroscopy constrain dust emission, obscured star formation and redshifted stellar or nebular features. Radio continuum and line observations constrain synchrotron emission, neutral or molecular gas, and star-formation tracers less affected by dust. Optical or near-infrared spectroscopy can still provide line ratios and kinematics where extinction permits. X-rays could test for an active nucleus or hot gas. A key issue is matching angular resolution: a large radio beam may blend regions that are separate in the infrared, so photometry must use comparable apertures or beam-matched maps.
</div>
