## 1. Error is not a mistake

In careful measurement language, distinguish three things. A **mistake** (reading the wrong star, transcribing a number incorrectly, using the wrong filter) is avoidable and should be corrected. A **known systematic offset** is a bias that should be calibrated out. **Uncertainty** is the quantified doubt that remains after corrections; it is unavoidable. Older texts often say “error” for this residual uncertainty, but the words are not interchangeable.

The two broad families are random error and systematic error. Random error causes scatter; systematic error causes an offset. A measurement can be precise but inaccurate, or accurate on average but noisy.

{{FIG:error-types}}

## 2. Random error and averaging

Random errors arise from photon statistics, atmospheric turbulence, electronic noise and small reading variations. If they are independent and have standard deviation $\sigma$, the standard error of the mean of $n$ readings is

$$
\sigma_{\bar x}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}.
$$

Averaging helps, but slowly: to halve the random uncertainty you need four times as many readings. Averaging does not help if the error is correlated, such as a slowly drifting transparency or a fixed calibration offset.

## 3. Systematic error and calibration

Systematic errors include a wrong zero point, flat-field error, atmospheric extinction, colour terms, clock error, ruler zero error and model mismatch. They do not average away. They must be calibrated, modelled or bounded.

A good calibration uses an independent standard: a known star, a known wavelength, a known length, a known time. The uncertainty in the standard becomes part of the measurement uncertainty.

## 4. Common astronomical error sources

Photon noise scales as $\sqrt N$. Sky background adds noise and depends on aperture size and sky brightness. Read noise matters for short exposures. Dark current matters for long exposures. Seeing changes the fraction of light in a photometric aperture. Extinction depends on airmass and colour. Tracking errors trail images. Timing errors shift phases. Catalogue errors affect astrometry and photometric zero points.

## 5. Building an error budget

List every term, estimate its size, correct known offsets, and combine independent, zero-mean standard uncertainties in quadrature. Identify the dominant term. If calibration dominates, more exposure time will not help much. If photon noise dominates, a larger aperture or longer exposure will help. If systematics dominate, better calibration or a different method is needed.

{{LAB:errors}}

## 6. Reporting uncertainty

Report the value, uncertainty, units and method. State whether the uncertainty is random, systematic or combined. If a result disagrees with a prediction, compare the difference with the combined uncertainty before claiming a discovery. A 2σ discrepancy is interesting; a 0.2σ discrepancy is not.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">A reliable method.</span> Ask: What would make this measurement wrong in the same direction every time? Calibrate that. Ask: What makes it scatter? Average that. Then report both.</div>

Photon noise and scintillation are mainly random. A ruler zero error and a wrong filter zero point are systematic. **Check:** random errors scatter around a mean; systematic errors shift the mean.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Averaging.</span> [Return →](10.01.html#p2)

For 9 readings, the standard error is $0.3/3=0.1$. To reach 0.05, $n=(0.3/0.05)^2=36$. **Check:** halving the uncertainty requires four times the readings.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Bias does not average.</span> [Return →](10.01.html#p3)

The 0.2 kg bias remains in the mean. Averaging reduces random scatter but not a fixed offset. The remedy is to calibrate the scale and subtract the offset, or include it as a systematic uncertainty. **Check:** 100 wrong zeros still give a wrong zero.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Combined uncertainty.</span> [Return →](10.01.html#p4)

The combined uncertainty is $\sqrt{0.03^2+0.04^2}=0.05$ mag. **Check:** independent uncertainties add in quadrature; the larger term dominates.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Photon noise.</span> [Return →](10.01.html#p5)

The uncertainty is $\sqrt{10000}=100$ photons. The fractional uncertainty is $100/10000=0.01$, or 1%. **Check:** photon noise decreases as $1/\sqrt N$.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Precision and accuracy.</span> [Return →](10.01.html#p6)

The measurements are precise but not accurate. A systematic error is likely, such as a calibration offset or wrong model. **Check:** tight clustering means small random scatter, not correctness.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Extinction error.</span> [Return →](10.01.html#p7)

The uncertainty is $0.02\times2=0.04$ mag. **Check:** extinction errors grow with airmass, so low-altitude observations are more vulnerable.

<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Timing error.</span> [Return →](10.01.html#p8)

The phase error is $1/120=0.0083$ cycles, or 3°. **Check:** a two-hour period is 120 minutes, so one minute is a small but measurable phase shift.

<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Dominant term.</span> [Return →](10.01.html#p9)

The flat-field error at 0.05 mag dominates. Improve the flat-field calibration first. **Check:** reducing photon noise from 0.01 to 0.005 would barely change the total because the flat-field term is much larger.
