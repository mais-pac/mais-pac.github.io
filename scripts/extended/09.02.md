## 1. A sky map is a model of the celestial sphere

The sky appears as a sphere surrounding the observer. Right ascension (RA) and declination (Dec) label positions on that sphere. RA is measured eastward along the celestial equator in hours, minutes and seconds; 24 h = 360°, so 1 h = 15°. Dec is measured north or south of the equator in degrees.

A chart projects the sphere onto a plane. Every projection distorts something: area, shape, distance or direction. Near the celestial poles, RA lines converge, so a fixed RA interval corresponds to a smaller sky distance.

## 2. Catalogues are databases, not pictures

A catalogue records standardized measurements: identifier, position, epoch, magnitude, band, proper motion, parallax, spectral type and uncertainties. A chart is only a visualization. When using a catalogue, check the proper-motion epoch (when the position was measured), the reference-frame equinox (the coordinate grid orientation), the photometric band, and the uncertainty. A position from 1950 is not the same as a position from 2000 if the star has proper motion, even when both are expressed in the same equinox.

Common identifiers include HD, HIP, Gaia, Messier and NGC/IC. Cross-identification can be nontrivial: a close pair, a variable star or a crowded field may have multiple entries.

## 3. Reading a chart

Identify the projection, orientation, scale and magnitude limit. North and east may not be up and left. The magnitude limit controls how many stars are shown; a deeper chart is not automatically better for finding a target because it can be crowded. Use bright asterisms as anchors, then star-hop to the target.

For a finder chart, mark the target, comparison stars and field size. State the epoch and the source of the positions.

## 4. Proper motion and epoch

Stars move. Proper motion $\mu$ in arcsec/year changes position over time:

$$
\Delta\theta=\mu\,\Delta t.
$$

For high-precision work, precession, nutation, parallax and radial motion also matter. For visual star-hopping, a recent catalogue and a chart epoch within a few decades are usually sufficient, but high-proper-motion stars can shift noticeably.

## 5. Magnitude limits and selection effects

A magnitude-limited chart includes all stars brighter than a limit, regardless of distance. This is not a volume-limited sample. Intrinsically bright stars can be seen from far away; faint stars are included only nearby. This selection effect matters when interpreting star counts or comparing clusters.

{{LAB:skymap}}

## 6. From catalogue to observation

A good workflow is: identify the target in a catalogue, check epoch and band, plot a finder chart with known orientation, choose comparison stars, and record the source. If the target is not where expected, check proper motion, coordinate units, RA/Dec order, and whether the chart is mirrored.

<div class="remark" markdown="1"><span class="remark-head">A reliable method.</span> Always ask: What epoch? What band? What projection? What magnitude limit? What is the uncertainty? A chart without metadata is a guess.</div>

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — RA to degrees.</span> [Return →](09.02.html#p1)

6 h is $6\times15=90^\circ$. Conversely, 90° is $90/15=6$ h. **Check:** 24 h corresponds to 360°.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Catalogue fields.</span> [Return →](09.02.html#p2)

Check the identifier, coordinate epoch/equinox, photometric band and magnitude, positional uncertainty, and proper motion if the time baseline is long. **Check:** a position without an epoch is incomplete.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Proper motion.</span> [Return →](09.02.html#p3)

The motion is $0.5\times50=25''$. A 1′ field is 60″ across, so 25″ is about 42% of the full field width—large enough to matter for precise pointing or narrow-field imaging. **Check:** high-proper-motion stars can move much more.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Chart scale.</span> [Return →](09.02.html#p4)

The separation is 3.5°. A mirrored chart reverses east and west, so position angles and star-hopping directions must be flipped. **Check:** many telescope finders or diagonal optics produce mirrored images.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Magnitude limit.</span> [Return →](09.02.html#p5)

The ratio is $10^{0.6(8-6)}=10^{1.2}=15.8$. **Check:** going two magnitudes deeper multiplies the number of stars by about 16 in this simple model.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Epoch.</span> [Return →](09.02.html#p6)

Precession changes the coordinate grid over decades, and proper motion changes the star’s position relative to the grid. Parallax and aberration matter for high precision. **Check:** J2000 is a reference frame, not a permanent pointing direction.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — RA separation.</span> [Return →](09.02.html#p7)

For stars at the same declination and a small RA difference, two minutes of RA is $2\times15'=30'$ along the equator. At declination 60°, the east–west separation is approximately $30'\cos60^\circ=15'$. **Check:** the cos δ factor accounts for converging RA lines.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Cross-identification.</span> [Return →](09.02.html#p8)

The entries could be a physical binary, a chance alignment, a variable star observed at different phases, or a catalogue error. Compare epochs, bands, proper motions and images; a common proper motion or resolved image can decide. **Check:** never assume the brighter entry is the target.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Finder-chart design.</span> [Return →](09.02.html#p9)

Use a field of about 15–30′ if the telescope field is small, with a magnitude limit near 11–12 so the target and comparisons are visible but the field is not overwhelmed. Mark north and east, state the epoch, and choose several non-variable comparison stars of similar colour and magnitude. Include a wider inset chart for star-hopping. **Check:** the chart must match the actual eyepiece or camera orientation.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Selection effect.</span> [Return →](09.02.html#p10)

A magnitude-limited sample includes intrinsically bright stars from large distances and faint stars only nearby, so it does not sample a fixed volume. A volume-limited sample requires distances and a cutoff within which all stars of the relevant type are included. **Check:** Malmquist bias is the classic consequence of magnitude-limited sampling.
</div>
