## 1. Calculus is the language of change and accumulation

Astronomy is full of rates and totals. Velocity is the rate of change of position; luminosity is energy per unit time; flux is power per unit area; mass can be the integral of density over volume. Calculus turns “how fast?” and “how much in total?” into precise operations.

The derivative is a local slope:

$$
f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.
$$

The definite integral is accumulated area:

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a),\qquad F'(x)=f(x).
$$

## 2. Derivatives you should recognize

Power rule: $d(x^n)/dx=nx^{n-1}$. Exponential: $d(e^{kx})/dx=ke^{kx}$. Trigonometric: $d(\sin x)/dx=\cos x$, $d(\cos x)/dx=-\sin x$. Product and chain rules let you differentiate combinations:

$$
(uv)'=u'v+uv',\qquad \frac{d}{dx}f(g(x))=f'(g(x))g'(x).
$$

In physics, the chain rule is often the difference between a correct and an incorrect rate. If $r(t)$ changes, then $d(r^2)/dt=2r\,dr/dt$, not merely $2r$.

## 3. Integrals you should recognize

Common antiderivatives include $\int x^n dx=x^{n+1}/(n+1)+C$ for $n\neq-1$, $\int e^{kx}dx=e^{kx}/k+C$, $\int \sin x\,dx=-\cos x+C$, and $\int dx/x=\ln|x|+C$. Definite integrals need limits and units. If $f$ is a rate, the integral has units of rate times the integration variable.

Symmetry can save work. The integral of an odd function over a symmetric interval is zero. The average value of a function on $[a,b]$ is $(b-a)^{-1}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$.

## 4. Small quantities and approximations

For small $x$ in radians,

$$
\sin x\approx x,\qquad \cos x\approx1-\frac{x^2}{2},\qquad (1+x)^n\approx1+nx.
$$

These are Taylor approximations. They are not magic; they are the first terms of a local polynomial. The small-angle approximation is excellent for 1° ($0.01745$ rad), but poor for 30°.


## 5. Differential equations in astronomy

Many laws are differential equations. Radioactive decay obeys $dN/dt=-\lambda N$, giving $N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}$. Newton’s second law is $F=m\,d^2x/dt^2$. Hydrostatic equilibrium is $dP/dr=-\rho g$. The solution is a function, not a single number; initial or boundary conditions select the physical one.

## 6. Optimization and physical reasoning

Maxima and minima often occur where a derivative is zero, but endpoints and physical constraints also matter. In orbital mechanics, the closest and farthest points are turning points of radius. In photometry, the minimum of a light curve may indicate an eclipse. Always check the second derivative or the physical context to decide whether a stationary point is a local maximum, local minimum or neither (for example, a point of inflection with horizontal tangent). “Saddle” is terminology for functions of more than one variable.
