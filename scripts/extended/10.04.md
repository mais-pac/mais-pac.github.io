## 1. An experiment is a designed argument

A good experiment begins with a question that can be answered by a measurement. What is the variable? What is held constant? What is the control? What calibration is needed? What uncertainty will limit the answer? The method should be written before observing, so the result is not chosen after the fact.

{{FIG:experiment-design}}

## 2. Differential photometry

Differential photometry measures a target relative to comparison stars in the same field. If the target and comparison share the same transparency, airmass and instrumental trends, the ratio cancels common variations. In magnitudes,

$$
\Delta m=m_{\rm target}-m_{\rm comparison}=-2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{F_{\rm target}}{F_{\rm comparison}}\right).
$$

A stable comparison star is essential. Choose stars of similar brightness and colour, away from crowding, and check that they are not variable. Multiple comparison stars improve reliability. The differential magnitude is relative to the chosen comparison ensemble; a calibrated zero point is needed to turn it into an absolute magnitude.

{{FIG:differential-photometry}}

## 3. Calibration and controls

Bias, dark and flat frames remove instrumental signatures. Standard stars or catalogue magnitudes set the zero point. For time-series photometry, keep exposure time, filter, focus and aperture consistent. If the target changes airmass, either correct for extinction or use comparisons at similar airmass.

A control is a measurement that should not change. If the comparison star shows the same “eclipse” as the target, the signal is probably instrumental or atmospheric, not astrophysical.

## 4. Cadence, duration and phase

Time-series work needs enough points to sample the event. For an eclipse, observe before, during and after. The cadence should resolve ingress and egress, not just the minimum. For periodic variables, cover multiple cycles if possible and record accurate times. Phase is

$$
\phi=\frac{t-T_0}{P}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1.
$$

A timing error shifts phase; a period error accumulates over many cycles.

## 5. Noise, systematics and significance

Photon noise sets a floor, but systematics often dominate: flat-field errors, colour terms, focus drift, tracking changes, comparison variability and aperture losses. Estimate the scatter from out-of-eclipse data. A claimed eclipse depth should be several times larger than the residual scatter and should **not** appear in independent stable comparison stars; a shared dip is evidence for an instrumental or atmospheric systematic.

{{LAB:photometry}}

## 6. Reporting an experimental result

Report the question, method, calibration, data, uncertainty and limitations. Show the light curve, the comparison stars, the residual scatter and the fitted depth or period. State what would change the conclusion. A negative result is still a result if the sensitivity is quantified.

<div class="solution" id="s1" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 1 — Differential magnitude.</span> [Return →](10.04.html#p1)

$\Delta m=-2.5\log_{10}(100/200)=-2.5\log_{10}(0.5)=0.753$ mag. The target is fainter than the comparison. **Check:** a factor of two in flux is about 0.75 mag.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s2" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 2 — Eclipse depth.</span> [Return →](10.04.html#p2)

The flux ratio is 0.9, so the depth is $-2.5\log_{10}(0.9)=0.114$ mag. **Check:** a 10% flux drop is slightly more than 0.1 mag.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s3" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 3 — Comparison-star choice.</span> [Return →](10.04.html#p3)

A good comparison is stable, similar in brightness and colour to the target, close enough to share the same airmass and transparency, and free from crowding or detector defects. **Check:** multiple comparisons allow a consistency test.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s4" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 4 — Common trend.</span> [Return →](10.04.html#p4)

The differential magnitude is unchanged because both fluxes are multiplied by the same factor, and the ratio cancels. **Check:** this is the main advantage of differential photometry.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s5" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 5 — Phase.</span> [Return →](10.04.html#p5)

$6.25/2.5=2.5$ cycles, so the phase is 0.5. **Check:** phase is the fractional part of the cycle count.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s6" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 6 — Calibration order.</span> [Return →](10.04.html#p6)

Bias removes the electronic zero level, dark removes thermal signal, and flat removes pixel-to-pixel sensitivity and illumination patterns. They are applied before analysis so instrumental structure is not mistaken for astrophysical signal. **Check:** calibration is part of the measurement, not an optional decoration.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s7" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 7 — Significance.</span> [Return →](10.04.html#p7)

The depth is 3 times the scatter, which is suggestive but not automatically convincing. Check the comparison stars, airmass trend, flat-field stability, focus, and whether the dip repeats at the predicted phase. **Check:** a real eclipse should not appear in stable comparison stars.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Cadence.</span> [Return →](10.04.html#p8)

The cadence is $90/15=6$ minutes per point. Observing outside eclipse establishes the baseline, reveals trends and allows the depth to be measured relative to a stable out-of-eclipse level. **Check:** ingress and egress may require even finer cadence.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — False signal.</span> [Return →](10.04.html#p9)

A simultaneous dip in target and comparison is likely instrumental or atmospheric: a cloud, tracking glitch, focus change, flat-field error or guiding issue. Test with additional comparison stars, check weather and guiding logs, and repeat on another night. **Check:** a true target eclipse should not appear in unrelated stable stars.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Design an experiment.</span> [Return →](10.04.html#p10)

Choose two or three stable comparison stars of similar magnitude and colour within the field. Take bias, dark and flat frames, and use a fixed filter and exposure time that keeps the target well below saturation while giving high SNR. Observe before, during and after the predicted eclipse with a cadence of a few minutes. Use a fixed photometric aperture or consistent PSF fitting, monitor airmass, and check that the comparison stars remain constant. Control flat-field and colour-dependent extinction systematics. Report the differential light curve, residual scatter, depth and uncertainty. **Check:** the experiment is designed to distinguish a real eclipse from common-mode noise.
</div>
