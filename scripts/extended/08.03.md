## 1. A vector has size and direction

Scalars such as mass and temperature are fully described by a number and a unit. Vectors such as displacement, velocity, force and angular momentum also need a direction. In two dimensions,

$$
\mathbf A=(A_x,A_y),\qquad |\mathbf A|=\sqrt{A_x^2+A_y^2}.
$$

If a vector has magnitude $A$ and angle $\theta$ from the $x$-axis, then $A_x=A\cos\theta$ and $A_y=A\sin\theta$. Components are not extra physics; they are a bookkeeping system that makes addition easy.

{{FIG:vector-add}}

## 2. Addition and relative motion

Vectors add head-to-tail, or component by component:

$$
\mathbf A+\mathbf B=(A_x+B_x,\;A_y+B_y).
$$

Relative velocity is a vector difference: $\mathbf v_{A/B}=\mathbf v_A-\mathbf v_B$. This is why a star’s proper motion, Earth’s motion and the observed radial velocity must be treated as directions, not just signed numbers.

## 3. The dot product: projection and flux

The dot product is

$$
\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=A_xB_x+A_yB_y=|\mathbf A||\mathbf B|\cos\theta.
$$

It measures how much one vector points along another. For a uniform field $\mathbf E$ through a flat oriented surface with area vector $\mathbf A=A\hat{\mathbf n}$, the signed flux is $\Phi=\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf A=EA\cos\theta$, with units of field times area. It is zero when the field lies in the surface (perpendicular to the normal), positive when it points along the chosen normal, and negative when it points opposite.

## 4. The cross product: rotation and orientation

In three dimensions,

$$
|\mathbf A\times\mathbf B|=|\mathbf A||\mathbf B|\sin\theta,
$$

with direction given by the right-hand rule. Torque is $\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F$; angular momentum is $\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$. In two dimensions, the cross product has only a $z$-component: $A_xB_y-A_yB_x$. Its sign tells whether the rotation from $\mathbf A$ to $\mathbf B$ is counterclockwise or clockwise.

{{FIG:dot-cross}}

## 5. Unit vectors and coordinate choices

A unit vector has magnitude 1 and specifies direction: for $\mathbf A\ne\mathbf0$, $\hat{\mathbf A}=\mathbf A/|\mathbf A|$. The zero vector has no direction. Choosing axes wisely can make a problem trivial. Align one axis with a velocity, a force or a line of sight. In spherical astronomy, directions are often written with right ascension and declination, but local calculations may be easier in horizontal or Cartesian components.

## 6. Physical checks

Vector equations must be dimensionally consistent. A dot product of two velocities has units of velocity squared; a cross product of position and momentum has units of angular momentum. If the answer is a vector, state its magnitude and direction or its components. If the answer is zero, ask whether the vectors are perpendicular, parallel, or equal and opposite.
