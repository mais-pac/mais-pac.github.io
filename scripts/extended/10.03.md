## 1. Practical geometry on a chart

A double star is a clean laboratory for angular measurement. The two quantities are separation $\rho$ and position angle $P$. Separation is the angular distance between components; position angle is measured from north through east. A ruler gives millimetres on a chart; the plate scale converts millimetres to arcseconds.

$$
\rho=k\,L,
$$

where $k$ is the plate scale in arcsec/mm and $L$ is the measured length in mm.

{{FIG:double-star-measure}}

## 2. Position angle convention

North is 0°, east 90°, south 180°, west 270°. If the companion is at position angle $P$, its sky components relative to the primary are

$$
\Delta\alpha\cos\delta=\rho\sin P,\qquad \Delta\delta=\rho\cos P.
$$

A protractor must be aligned with the chart’s north direction. If the chart is mirrored or rotated, the position angle must be corrected. Always state the convention and epoch.

{{FIG:position-angle}}

## 3. Plate scale and calibration

The plate scale can be calculated from focal length and pixel size, or measured from a known double star or star field. If a known separation $\rho_0$ measures $L_0$ mm, then $k=\rho_0/L_0$. Calibration uncertainty propagates directly into every measurement.

Plate scale $k$ is measured in arcsec/mm. A smaller $k$ means more millimetres per arcsecond, so the same ruler error produces a smaller angular error. But a very small $k$ may make the field too small or the image too dim.

## 4. Reading uncertainty

If the ruler reading uncertainty is $\sigma_L$ mm, the angular uncertainty is

$$
\sigma_\rho=k\sigma_L.
$$

Position-angle uncertainty depends on separation: for a small positional error $\sigma_\perp$ perpendicular to the line, $\sigma_P\approx\sigma_\perp/\rho$ in radians. Wide pairs are easier to measure in angle; close pairs are harder.

## 5. Measuring procedure

Centre the primary, align north, measure the separation several times, rotate the chart or protractor to check alignment, and record the epoch. If using a micrometer or reticle, calibrate its scale. If using an image, measure centroid positions rather than eyeballing the centres. Repeat measurements and report the mean and scatter.

{{LAB:doublestar}}

## 6. Reporting a double-star measurement

A complete report includes component identifiers, epoch, separation, position angle, plate scale, method, uncertainty and any resolution limit. If the pair is not resolved, state an upper limit rather than a false measurement. A good measurement is honest about what the instrument could and could not separate.

<div class="solution" id="s8" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 8 — Resolution limit.</span> [Return →](10.03.html#p8)

No. A 0.8″ pair is below the 1.5″ practical resolution, so it cannot be reliably measured as two components. Report an upper limit on separation or note that the pair is unresolved. **Check:** a false measurement is worse than an honest limit.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s9" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 9 — Epoch and motion.</span> [Return →](10.03.html#p9)

Under the constant-rate approximation, the change is $2\times10=20^\circ$. The epoch must be stated because orbital motion changes the position angle over time, and the rate can vary around an eccentric orbit. **Check:** a measurement without an epoch cannot be compared with other observations.
</div>
<div class="solution" id="s10" markdown="1">
<span class="sol-head">Solution 10 — Design a measurement.</span> [Return →](10.03.html#p10)

Align the protractor’s zero with chart north, measure the centre-to-centre length with the ruler, convert using the plate scale, and read the position angle. Repeat the measurements, rotate the chart to check alignment, and use a fine-scale chart or magnifier. Record the ruler and protractor resolution and propagate them into the final uncertainty. **Check:** repeated independent readings reveal alignment and reading errors.
</div>
