Today's Cosmic Wonder

Today's Cosmic Wonder

A comet and a rainbow meteor, caught in the same photo by accident

3 November 2026 · explained in plain English

A sky-watcher in Hautacam, France pointed a camera at a faint, slow-drifting comet and caught something else entirely: a brilliant, multicoloured meteor slicing through the same frame. Here is the twist — they are not related at all. The meteor is a speck of rock, maybe the size of a grain of sand, burning up in our atmosphere in less than a second; the comet behind it is a giant dirty snowball from the edge of the Solar System that takes centuries to complete one orbit, and it only looks nearby because of where the camera happened to be pointed. The timing is no accident, though: this same week two meteor showers, the Southern and Northern Taurids, are peaking, with the famous Leonids blazing up in about two weeks.

Astronomy & space science

  • 27 October 2026

    Webb finds a feeding black hole when the universe was 470 million years old

    A faint red speck named MoM-BL-z10 hides a black hole of roughly 2 to 36 million suns, seen just 3% of the way back to the Big Bang. The team dug it out of public Webb archive data by spotting hydrogen gas smeared to 3,480 km/s — a merry-go-round too fast for anything but a black hole. The mass estimates disagree by a factor of ~17, and the team published both rather than picking a favourite, a nice lesson in honest error bars.

    Read more: Daily Galaxy ↗  ·  Reference: NASA Webb mission page ↗

  • 25 October 2026

    Three active black holes in a single galaxy, 12.5 billion light-years away

    Galaxy J0148-4214 contains not one but three feeding supermassive black holes — two only 620 light-years apart, destined to merge, plus a third 5,500 light-years out. The find suggests early galaxies may grow their monsters by merging them together, a fast track to the giants we see today.

    Read more: ScienceDaily / MPE ↗  ·  Reference: NASA: Galaxies explained ↗

  • 21 October 2026

    Webb detects heavy metals surrounding the Milky Way's black hole

    Sagittarius A* is eating less than expected, and JWST just found a trove of supernova-forged metals in gas clouds near it, matching the composition of nearby stars. Why our galactic centre is so picky about its meals remains an open mystery; the metals may even relate to how Earth got its share of heavy elements.

    Read more: Live Science ↗  ·  Reference: NASA Webb mission page ↗

  • 16 October 2026

    The atmosphere of WASP-17b is not round — it's being stretched

    A new "transmission strings" technique uses JWST to map the true, lopsided shape of a hot Jupiter's atmosphere 1,300 light-years away: equatorial jets shovel heat from the permanent dayside to the nightside, inflating one limb. It's a first step toward weather maps of alien worlds.

    Read more: University of Bristol ↗  ·  Reference: NASA Webb mission page ↗

  • 12 August 2026

    A brand-new type of object: the "black hole star"

    Webb's bright red dot MoM-BH*-1, seen 660 million years after the Big Bang, outshines its whole galaxy yet produces 100 billion times more energy than any star could. A MIT-led team argues it is a hybrid: a ~100,000-solar-mass black hole inside a star-sized cocoon of hydrogen, possibly the answer to the mysterious "little red dots" filling Webb's deep images.

    Read more: MIT News ↗  ·  Reference: NASA: Black Holes explained ↗

Missions & rockets

  • 1–11 April 2026 · recap

    Artemis II: humans flew around the Moon — and came home safe

    NASA's Artemis II carried astronauts Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, Christina Koch and Jeremy Hansen on a free-return loop behind the lunar far side, the first crewed flight beyond low Earth orbit since Apollo 17 in 1972. On 6 April they passed 252,706 miles from Earth (breaking Apollo 13's record). Splashdown in the Pacific came on 11 April after 9 days. Next up: Artemis III's crewed landing attempt.

    Read more: NASA blog ↗ · ScienceDaily ↗  ·  Reference: NASA Artemis program ↗

  • 28 September 2026

    Starship Flight 14: first Starship to reach orbit with a payload

    The largest rocket ever built reached orbit and deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites, Flight 14's milestone, despite one upper-stage Raptor engine shutting down early during ascent. The setback became Flight 15's homework.

Follow the sky yourself

Club rule: verify before you repeat. These are the sources we check weekly — then we bring the interesting stories to meetings.

  • Primary sources first

    NASA News ↗, ESA ↗, and mission blogs — press releases before headlines. When a claim matters, find who actually did the work.

  • Science news with an editorial filter

    ScienceDaily Space & Time ↗, NASA ↗, Space.com ↗ — good for discovery stories; always click through to the paper when a number surprises you.

  • Rocket & industry coverage

    SpaceNews ↗ for launches, contracts and policy; launch trackers like Next Spaceflight ↗ for dates (all dates tentative until announced!).

  • The papers themselves

    New astronomy results appear first on arXiv astro-ph ↗. Use our Resources guide to read one properly — abstract, figures, conclusion, in that order.

  • Tonight's sky

    Stellarium Web ↗ to see what's actually above Ulaanbaatar right now — news is better when you can point a telescope at the object.

Discussion starter: pick one story from this page per meeting. Each member states (a) what was observed, (b) how we know, (c) what it does not prove. That three-line format is the seed of the paper-reading skill on the Resources page.
