Physics · Astronomy · Curiosity

Club

The Physics & Astronomy Club (PAC) is a student community at Mongol Aspiration International School in Ulaanbaatar. It is a place to begin: to ask questions, work through ideas slowly, and discover that the sky and the rules behind it can be understood.

You do not need to know all the answers to join the conversation. Curiosity, patience and a willingness to check your own reasoning are enough to start.

What we do

Learn together

Read short handouts, discuss the physical idea before the formula, and solve problems step by step. Mistakes are useful when they show us what to check next.

Explore by experiment

Use the interactive laboratories to change a parameter, make a prediction, and see what happens. The aim is to enjoy physics rather than drown in equations.

Look up

Connect classroom ideas to the Moon, planets, stars and galaxies above Mongolia, and to the wider universe that physics helps us describe.

Projects & activities

Physics club should be fun before it is formal. These are the recurring projects and game nights — join one, or pitch your own (a new activity just needs two friends and a date).

📄Paper Reading Circleweekly

One real arXiv paper, one hour, everyone reads the same pages. We skim together first (title, abstract, figures, conclusion), then reconstruct the paper's argument out loud — in our own words. Start with the start papers on the Resources shelf.

📰Club Magazinetermly

Publish! Each term we produce a student magazine: articles written by members explaining one idea each (a discovery, a math corner, a myth-busted column, sky guides for Ulaanbaatar). Roles rotate — writers, editors, illustrators, layout — so everyone ships something they're proud of.

🙋AQA Night — Ask Questions Anythingbiweekly

Open mic for curiosity: anyone writes any question on the board (“Why is the sky dark at night?”, “How do we know the Moon is moving away?”). We vote on the top questions, then research and answer them together — using the News page and Resources guides. No question too small, none too strange.

🎤Present Like a Promonthly

Topic → research → slides → talk. The full pipeline for taking a claim to an audience at high level — sources, structure, design and delivery. See the how-to below; every session ends with three-minute talks and kind, specific feedback.

🎮Kahoot Battlegame night

Members write the quiz questions from recent handouts and news — writing the distractors teaches as much as playing. Winners pick the next paper for the reading circle. Physics, astronomy, math, and “guess the mission” rounds.

🎨Poster Studioproject

Draw the physics: A3/A0 research-style posters explaining one concept (how a telescope sees, why orbit is falling, what a gravitational wave does to a ring of particles). Exhibition at club meetings; the best go on the school wall — and on the website.

🔭Sky Nightsweather allowing

Ulaanbaatar's dark skies are an asset. Plan a session around the Moon, a planet, a meteor shower or the Milky Way core; before observing, predict what you'll see from Stellarium ↗, after observing, log what you actually saw. Predict → observe → explain.

🧩Problem Slams & Relaystraining

Timed team solving from our Teaching handouts (IPhO and IOAA style), plus estimation challenges — “how many pixels does JWST send per second?” Wrong orders of magnitude are celebrated loudly and then fixed together.

How we run a paper session

  • Week before: one paper, one page of context

    The facilitator shares the arXiv link plus two sentences on why we chose it. Everyone does only the 15-minute skim from the reading guide.

  • First 20 minutes: the blind reconstruction

    Books closed. Each person writes the one-sentence takeaway template: question → method → result → why it matters. Then we compare — disagreements are where the learning is.

  • Next 30 minutes: figures and fights

    We walk through the figures together and argue about interpretations. Rule: every claim must point at evidence (“which figure shows that?”).

  • Last 10 minutes: the three questions

    What did we learn, what confused us, what should we read next? The answers become next week's agenda — and occasionally a magazine article.

From a topic to a presentation — the pipeline

The essential skill: taking a claim to a high-level performance. Five stages, in order:

  • 1 · Find the info — three trusted sources minimum

    Start from the primary source (the paper or agency page — see the news source list), add one review or encyclopedia entry for background, and one recent news article for context. Write every source down as you go — citations later take five minutes instead of an hour. If two sources disagree, that's not a problem, that's your presentation's most interesting slide.

  • 2 · Decide your claim in one sentence

    Not “a presentation about black holes” — a claim: “The first black hole picture tested general relativity in a regime we'd never observed.” If you can't state it in one sentence, you don't have it yet. Everything else in the talk exists to serve that sentence.

  • 3 · Build the skeleton: claim → evidence → warrant

    Every slide answers one of: What do I claim? What's my evidence (figure, number, measurement)? Why does that evidence support the claim? Three slides per section, roughly: hook, evidence, so-what. Signpost along the way (“there are three reasons; first…”).

  • 4 · Design slides people can actually read

    One idea per slide. Big figure, small text — steal the paper's best figure and add your own annotation. Six words per bullet maximum; you are the slides' audio track, not their subtitle. Test from the back row: if you can't read it standing at the screen, neither can they.

  • 5 · Rehearse out loud — twice

    Once to time it (usually you're 50% over — cut), once to a friend who must ask one hard question. High-level delivery is 20% content and 80% command: steady pace, eyes up, hands off the podium, pause instead of “um”, and open by promising the audience one thing they'll know by the end.

Delivery checklist (print it)

☐ One-sentence claim, said first and repeated last   ☐ Every number has a unit and a source   ☐ One figure beats five paragraphs   ☐ Signposted structure   ☐ Rehearsed out loud twice   ☐ Anticipated the hardest question   ☐ Ending returns to the opening promise.
