Physics P3: Solutions

Gravity, Orbits, and Waves · MAIS PAC

Problem 1.

(a) A satellite’s orbital plane stays fixed relative to the distant stars (inertial space), so to hover over a fixed point on Earth’s surface it must match Earth’s true 360° rotation period relative to the stars—one sidereal day (86,164 s). Moreover, Earth’s gravity pulls toward Earth’s center, so every Keplerian orbit lies in a plane passing through Earth’s center (a great circle). Hovering perpetually at latitude 47.9° N would define a small circle north of Earth’s center whose centripetal acceleration points toward Earth’s rotation axis rather than Earth’s center, leaving an unbalanced southward component of gravity.

(b) From Kepler’s third law r = (GM⊕P2 / 4π2)1/3, using GM⊕ = 3.986 × 1014 m3/s2 and P = 86,164 s:

r = ((3.986 × 1014)(86,164)24π2)1/3 ≈ 4.216 × 107 m = 42,160 km.
(1)

Subtracting R⊕ = 6,370 km gives an altitude h ≈ 35,790 km (about 5.6 Earth radii above the equator).

Problem 2.

(a) For a circular orbit of radius r, kinetic energy is K = +GMm/(2r), potential energy is U = −GMm/r, and total energy is E = −GMm/(2r) = −K. When drag does negative work Wdrag = ΔE < 0, total energy E becomes more negative, so r decreases—and therefore kinetic energy K = −E increases by ΔK = −ΔE > 0! The satellite speeds up as it spirals lower.

(b) Because the satellite spirals slightly inward rather than moving strictly perpendicular to gravity, its velocity vector has a tiny downward radial component toward Earth’s center. Gravity therefore does positive work ΔWgrav = −ΔU = +2|ΔE|, twice as large as the energy −|ΔE| dissipated by drag, leaving net kinetic energy gain ΔK = +|ΔE|.

Problem 3.

(a) Since rp + ra = 2a, the semi-major axis is a = 1.00 + 1.522 = 1.26 AU. A full orbit on this ellipse takes P = a3/2 = (1.26)3/2 ≈ 1.414 yr. Coasting from perihelion (Earth) to aphelion (Mars) is exactly half an orbit:

ttransfer = P2 ≈ 0.707 yr × 365.25 d/yr ≈ 258 days (≈ 8.5 months).
(2)

(b) At r = r1, Earth’s circular speed satisfies v⊕2 = GM⊙/r1. Dividing the vis-viva equation by v⊕2 gives

vpv⊕ = √(2 − r1a) = √(2 − 1.001.26) ≈ 1.098.
(3)

Thus vp ≈ 1.098 × 29.8 km/s ≈ 32.7 km/s, requiring a departure boost Δv1 = vp − v⊕ ≈ 2.9 km/s.

Problem 4.

(a) Around the center of mass, momentum vanishes: M★v★ = mpvp. For mp ≪ M★, the planet’s orbital radius a satisfies Kepler’s third law a = (GM★P2 / 4π2)1/3, so its circular speed is vp = 2πa/P = (2πGM★ / P)1/3. Multiplying by mp/M★ immediately gives

v★ = mpM★ (2πGM★P)1/3 = mp (2πGM★2P)1/3.
(4)

(b) With P = 11.86 × 3.156 × 107 s ≈ 3.74 × 108 s, Jupiter’s orbital speed is vJ = (2πGM⊙/P)1/3 ≈ 1.306 × 104 m/s. Multiplying by mJ/M⊙ = 9.55 × 10−4 yields v⊙ ≈ 12.5 m/s (a brisk bicycle ride!), producing a tiny Doppler shift Δλ/λ0 = v⊙/c ≈ 4.2 × 10−8. Modern stabilized spectrographs measure shifts down to ~0.3 m/s, routinely discovering rocky planets around nearby stars.
