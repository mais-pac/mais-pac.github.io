Astronomy A2: Solutions

Light, Telescopes, and Stars · MAIS PAC

Problem 1.

(a) Neglecting the small secondary-mirror obstruction, the collecting-area ratio is (D/de)2 = (200 / 7.0)2 ≈ 816. A star at the telescope’s detection threshold can therefore be 816 times dimmer than a 6.0-magnitude star:

mlim = 6.0 + 2.5 log10(816) ≈ 6.0 + 7.28 ≈ 13.3,
(1)

easily bright enough to reveal Pluto (~14.3 on photography) and hundreds of galaxies and globular clusters.

(b) At λ = 5.50 × 10−7 m and D = 0.20 m, θmin = 1.22(5.50 × 10−7)/0.20 = 3.36 × 10−6 rad ≈ 0.69″. From the ground, turbulent temperature pockets in Earth’s atmosphere (“astronomical seeing”) refract starlight randomly over ~0.5″–2″, blurring large telescopes unless deformable adaptive-optics mirrors correct the wavefront in real time.

Problem 2.

(a) By Wien’s displacement law, T = b/λmax = (2.90 × 10−3 m·K)/(8.30 × 10−7 m) ≈ 3,490 K (≈ 3,500 K), giving Betelgeuse its unmistakable orange-red glow in winter skies.

(b) Using LL⊙ = (RR⊙)2 (TT⊙)4:

RR⊙ = √(LL⊙) (T⊙T)2 ≈ √(105) (5,7803,490)2 ≈ 316 × 2.74 ≈ 870 R⊙.
(2)

Dividing by 215 R⊙/AU gives R ≈ 4.0 AU—if placed at the center of our Solar System, Betelgeuse would engulf Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, and much of the asteroid belt!

Problem 3.

(a) At orbital distance a, stellar flux is F = L★/(4πa2) = (4πR★2σT★4)/(4πa2) = σT★4(R★/a)2. Equating absorbed power πRp2(1 − A)F to thermally emitted power 4πRp2σTeq4 cancels πRp2σ on both sides:

4 Teq4 = (1 − A) T★4 (R★a)2  ⇒  Teq = T★ (1 − A)1/4 √(R★2a).
(3)

(b) Substituting Earth’s numbers gives Teq = 5,780 × (0.70)1/4 × √(6.96 × 108 / 3.00 × 1011) ≈ 5,780 × 0.9147 × 0.04817 ≈ 255 K (−18 °C). Without an atmosphere, Earth’s oceans would freeze solid; trace greenhouse gases (water vapor and CO2) are transparent to incoming visible sunlight but absorb outgoing thermal infrared radiation, warming the surface by ~33 K to a habitable 288 K (+15 °C).

Problem 4.

(a) Available fusion energy is Etot ≈ 0.007 × (0.10 M⊙)c2 = 7.0 × 10−4 (1.99 × 1030 kg)(3.00 × 108 m/s)2 ≈ 1.25 × 1044 J. Dividing by L⊙ = 3.83 × 1026 W yields

τ⊙ ≈ 1.25 × 1044 J3.83 × 1026 W ≈ 3.27 × 1017 s ≈ 10.4 Gyr
(4)

(comfortably longer than the Solar System’s current 4.6 Gyr age).

(b) Since fuel reserves scale as M while burn rate scales as L ∝ M3.5, lifetime scales inversely with mass:

τMS ∝ ML ∝ M−2.5  ⇒  τMS ≈ 10 Gyr (MM⊙)−2.5.
(5)

A massive 10 M⊙ star shines ~3,160 times brighter than the Sun and exhausts its core in just 10 Gyr × 10−2.5 ≈ 32 million years, exploding as a core-collapse supernova. Conversely, a thrifty 0.20 M⊙ red dwarf lasts 10 Gyr × (0.20)−2.5 ≈ 560 Gyr (even longer in practice because low-mass dwarfs are fully convective and burn nearly 100% of their hydrogen)—more than 40 times the present 13.8 Gyr age of the universe!
