Astronomy A1: Solutions

The Night Sky and Celestial Motion · MAIS PAC

Problem 1.

(a) Using θ = 2R/D in radians and multiplying by (180/π) × 60′ = 3437.75′/rad:

θ⊙ ≈ 2(6.96 × 105)1.50 × 108 × 3437.75′ ≈ 31.9′,  θM ≈ 2(1,737)3.84 × 105 × 3437.75′ ≈ 31.1′.
(1)

Although the Sun is ~400 times larger in radius than the Moon, it is also ~390 times farther away, making both disks span roughly half a degree (~31′–32′) on our sky!

(b) Near lunar perigee (closest approach), θM increases to ~33.5′ > θ⊙, completely covering the solar photosphere and revealing the faint solar corona (a total eclipse). Near apogee, θM shrinks to ~29.4′ < θ⊙, leaving a bright ring of sunlight around the Moon’s silhouette (an annular eclipse). Eclipses do not happen every month because the Moon’s orbital plane is tilted by 5.1° relative to the ecliptic, so at most new moons the Moon passes several degrees north or south of the Sun; eclipses occur only when new moon coincides with one of the two orbital nodes.

Problem 2.

(a) At φ = 47.9° N, 90° − φ = 42.1°. Stars within 42.1° of the north celestial pole—that is, δ > +42.1°—are circumpolar and never set. Symmetrically, stars within 42.1° of the south celestial pole—δ < −42.1°—never rise above Ulaanbaatar’s horizon.

(b) Checking each star’s declination:

  • • Dubhe (δ = +61.8° > +42.1°): circumpolar. Its polar distance from the north celestial pole (at altitude 47.9°) is 90° − 61.8° = 28.2°, so Dubhe reaches upper culmination at hmax = 47.9° + 28.2° = 76.1° and dips at lower culmination to hmin = 47.9° − 28.2° = 19.7° due north.
  • • Vega (δ = +38.8°): seasonal (just 3.3° shy of being circumpolar, rising high to hmax = 80.9° in summer nights).
  • • Sirius (δ = −16.7°): seasonal winter star, culminating due south at hmax = 90° − (47.9° − (−16.7°)) = 25.4° above the southern horizon.
  • • Alpha Centauri (δ = −60.8° < −42.1°): never visible from Ulaanbaatar (peaking 18.7° below the southern horizon).

Problem 3.

(a) Imagine Earth froze its spin relative to the distant stars while completing one revolution around the Sun: an observer on Earth would still experience one apparent solar cycle (in reverse!). Because Earth spins in the same prograde direction as its orbit, one orbital revolution subtracts one solar day relative to the number of sidereal turns, so Nsid = N + 1 = 366.2422. Hence

Tsid = 365.2422366.2422 × 24 h ≈ 23.93447 h = 23 h 56 m 4.1 s.
(2)

(b) Every solar day, any given star crosses the meridian 24 h − Tsid ≈ 3 m 55.9 s earlier on our wristwatch. Over 30 days this accumulates to 30 × (24 h / 365.24) ≈ 1.97 h ≈ 1 h 58 m earlier, so Orion will transit at roughly 21:02 local time.

Problem 4.

(a) The faster inner body advances in angle at ω1 = 2π/P1 while the slower outer body advances at ω2 = 2π/P2. Between two consecutive alignments, the inner body gains one full lap (2π radians) on the outer body: (ω1 − ω2)S = 2π. Dividing through by 2πS gives 1S = 1P1 − 1P2.

(b) Relative to the Earth–Sun line (which rotates at 1/P⊕), the Moon’s phase cycle satisfies

1S = 127.32 d − 1365.25 d  ⇒  S ≈ 29.53 days.
(3)

(c) Neptune crawls along its orbit so slowly (1/165 yr−1) that after Earth completes one 365.25-day lap, Neptune has barely moved ~2.2°, taking Earth only 2.2 days to catch up! Mars, by contrast, moves more than half as fast as Earth, leading Earth on a long chase over more than two full years (~780 days) before Earth laps it again.
