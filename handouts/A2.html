Astronomy A2: Light, Telescopes, and Stars

MAIS Physics & Astronomy Club (PAC)

Unlike laboratory physicists, astronomers cannot touch a star, put it on a scale, or insert a thermometer into its photosphere. Virtually everything we know about stars—their temperatures, radii, compositions, and lifetimes—comes from decoding the starlight collected by telescopes.

1. Luminosity, Flux, Magnitudes, and Telescopes

A star of intrinsic power (luminosity) L radiates isotropically across a sphere of area 4πd2 at distance d, delivering energy per unit area per unit time (flux) F = L/(4πd2). Historically, Hipparchus ranked visible stars into six brightness classes; Pogson formalized this logarithmic scale so that a difference of 5 apparent magnitudes corresponds to a flux ratio of exactly 100:

m1 − m2 = −2.5 log10 (F1F2).
(1)

A circular telescope objective of aperture diameter D has two primary jobs: collecting light over area π(D/2)2 ∝ D2, and resolving fine angular detail down to the wave-optics Rayleigh diffraction limit θmin = 1.22 λ/D (in radians).

[1] Problem 1 (Why aperture rules). Compare a 200 mm (8-inch) club reflector telescope with a dark-adapted human eye of pupil diameter de = 7.0 mm at visible wavelength λ = 550 nm.

  • (a) How many times more light does the telescope collect than a single eye? If the unaided eye can see stars down to apparent magnitude m = 6.0 under a dark sky, how faint a star (in magnitudes) can the telescope reach?
  • (b) Compute the theoretical diffraction limit θmin of the 200 mm telescope in arcseconds. Why do ground-based telescopes rarely achieve much sharper raw images than ~1″ without adaptive optics?

2. Thermal Radiation and Stellar Radii

To a good approximation, a star’s photosphere radiates like an ideal thermal blackbody at effective surface temperature T. Two fundamental laws tie color and power directly to temperature:

λmax T = b ≈ 2.90 × 10−3 m·K,   L = 4πR2 σT4,
(2)

where Wien’s displacement law gives the peak wavelength λmax and the Stefan–Boltzmann law (σ = 5.67 × 10−8 W·m−2·K−4) gives total radiated luminosity from surface area 4πR2.

Remark. Avoid plugging in σ whenever you can! Dividing L = 4πR2σT4 by the solar values gives LL⊙ = (RR⊙)2 (TT⊙)4, which eliminates 4π and σ in a single line.

[2] Problem 2 (Measuring a red supergiant without resolving it). Betelgeuse in Orion has luminosity L ≈ 1.0 × 105 L⊙, and its continuous spectrum peaks in the near-infrared at λmax ≈ 830 nm (take T⊙ = 5,780 K and 1 AU ≈ 215 R⊙).

  • (a) Find the effective surface temperature T of Betelgeuse.
  • (b) Estimate Betelgeuse’s radius in units of R⊙ and in AU. Which planets’ orbits would lie inside Betelgeuse if it replaced the Sun?

[2] Problem 3 (Planetary equilibrium temperatures). A rapidly rotating spherical planet of radius Rp orbits a star of radius R★ and temperature T★ at distance a. A fraction A (the Bond albedo) of incident starlight is reflected back to space, while the remaining fraction (1 − A) is absorbed over cross-sectional disk area πRp2 and re-radiated thermally over full spherical area 4πRp2.

  • (a) Show that the planet’s radiative equilibrium temperature is Teq = T★ (1 − A)1/4 √(R★2a), independent of Rp.
  • (b) Evaluate Teq (in K and °C) for Earth (A = 0.30, T⊙ = 5,780 K, R⊙ = 6.96 × 108 m, a = 1.50 × 1011 m), and explain why Earth’s actual global mean surface temperature is +15 °C (288 K).

3. Nuclear Fuel and Stellar Lifetimes

During its main-sequence life, a star fuses hydrogen into helium in its core, converting roughly ε ≈ 0.007 (0.7%) of the fused rest mass into radiant energy via ΔE = ε Δmc2.

[3] Problem 4 (Live fast, die young). Assume a star can fuse the innermost 10% of its total mass M before exhausting core hydrogen.

  • (a) Using M⊙ = 1.99 × 1030 kg and L⊙ = 3.83 × 1026 W, estimate the Sun’s main-sequence lifetime τ⊙ in billions of years (Gyr).
  • (b) Observations of main-sequence stars show the empirical mass–luminosity scaling L ∝ M3.5. Derive how main-sequence lifetime τMS scales with mass M, and estimate τMS for a bright blue 10 M⊙ star and for a dim 0.20 M⊙ red dwarf.
