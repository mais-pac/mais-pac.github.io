C1.01 · Limits — what a function is trying to say

A limit asks: as the input gets closer and closer to some value, what does the output approach? The function does not even need to be defined there — the limit cares about the journey, not the destination sign.

Super-simple explanation

Imagine walking toward a door. A limit is your prediction of where you will end up, based on your path — even if the door itself is missing. You can know you are heading to “5” without ever being allowed to stand on 5.

limx→a f(x) = L“as x approaches a, f(x) approaches L”

The essential cases. A limit exists when every path to a gives the same answer. Left-hand and right-hand limits must match: limx→a⁻ f(x) = limx→a⁺ f(x). The famous trap is 0/0 — that is not an answer, it is a question. It means “both numerator and denominator vanish here; simplify, then ask again.”

Worked example. Find limx→2 (x² − 4)/(x − 2). Direct substitution gives 0/0. Factor: (x−2)(x+2)/(x−2) = x+2 for x ≠ 2. Now the limit is 2 + 2 = 4. The function is undefined at exactly x = 2, but the limit is 4 — journey, not destination.

Toolbox. Limits obey algebraic laws: the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits, of a product the product, of a quotient the quotient (when the bottom limit is not zero). The squeeze theorem is the club favourite: if g(x) ≤ f(x) ≤ h(x) and both g and h approach L, then f is squeezed to L too.

Check: Why does limx→0 sin(x)/x = 1 make sense geometrically (hint: compare the areas of a triangle, a sector and a quarter-circle)?

In physics: limits are what let you say “the velocity at this exact instant” — you will formalise that in C1.03. Watch for limits whenever a formula politely refuses to divide by zero: electric field of a point charge at the source, density of an object of zero volume, and other “0/0” ghosts in disguise.

C1.02 · Continuity — no jumps, no holes

A function is continuous at a point when three things agree: the function is defined there, the limit exists there, and the limit equals the value. Draw it with your finger without lifting: that is continuity.

Super-simple explanation

Discontinuities come in flavours: a removable hole (the point is missing or misplaced), a jump (the graph teleports to a new height), and an asymptote (the graph escapes to infinity). Continuous functions are the well-behaved citizens that make calculus possible.

The payoff — the Intermediate Value Theorem. If a continuous function takes the values 2 and 5, it must pass through every value in between: 3.4, π, 4.999… No jumping allowed. Physically: if you read 20 °C at dawn and 30 °C at noon, the temperature was 25 °C at some moment — guaranteed, no thermometer needed for the proof.

Worked example. Show that x³ + x − 1 = 0 has a solution between 0 and 1. Let f(x) = x³ + x − 1. Then f(0) = −1 and f(1) = +1. f is a polynomial, hence continuous everywhere. By IVT, f must equal zero somewhere in (0, 1). Done — without ever finding the number.

Check: A student claims f(x) = 1/x is discontinuous “at zero.” Precisely what is missing — a hole, a jump, or an asymptote — and does the IVT apply across x = 0?

In physics: conservation laws are continuity statements in disguise: energy cannot jump from 7 J to 9 J without passing through 8 J. General relativity and fluid mechanics even build whole equations (continuity equations) from the idea that what flows in must flow out.

C1.03 · The derivative — slope at a point

The derivative is the instantaneous rate of change of a function. Geometrically it is the slope of the tangent line; physically it is the speedometer reading of whatever quantity you are tracking.

Super-simple explanation

Start with average speed: distance divided by time over a long trip. Now shrink the trip shorter and shorter — from an hour, to a minute, to a second, to an instant. The average speed settles onto a single number. That number is the derivative.

f′(x) = limh→0 [ f(x + h) − f(x) ] / hslope of the tangent = limit of the slopes of secants

Units rule (never forget it). The derivative's units are the output units divided by the input units. Position (m) differentiated gives velocity (m/s); velocity gives acceleration (m/s²); money over time is a rate of spending. If your units are wrong, your derivative is wrong.

Worked example. f(x) = x². Then f′(x) = limh→0 [(x+h)² − x²]/h = limh→0 [2xh + h²]/h = limh→0 (2x + h) = 2x. So the slope of y = x² at x = 3 is 6 — the tangent line at (3, 9) is y = 6x − 9.

Notation you will meet. f′(x), dy/dx (Leibniz), and ẋ (Newton's dot, for time-derivatives — the notation of mechanics). They all mean the same instant: “how fast is this changing, right now?”

Check: If a rocket's height is h(t) = 4.9t² metres (t in seconds), what are the units of h′(t), and what is h′(2)? Interpret both in one sentence.

In physics: velocity is dx/dt, acceleration is dv/dt, current is dQ/dt, and EMF is −dΦ/dt (Faraday's law). Every “rate” in your formula sheet is a derivative wearing a different label.

C1.04 · Rules of differentiation

Nobody differentiates from the definition after week two. These rules turn differentiation into bookkeeping:

Power: d/dx [xⁿ] = n·xⁿ⁻¹  ·  Sum: (f + g)′ = f′ + g′
Product: (fg)′ = f′g + fg′  ·  Quotient: (f/g)′ = (f′g − fg′) / g²
Chain rule: d/dx f(g(x)) = f′(g(x)) · g′(x)“differentiate the outside, keep the inside, multiply by the inside's derivative”

Super-simple explanation

The chain rule is the rule physics cannot live without, because nature composes functions: position depends on time, time-dependent angle depends on time, energy depends on position which depends on time. When one process nests inside another, you multiply the rates.

Worked example. Differentiate y = (3x² + 1)⁵. Outside: 5(·)⁴. Inside: 6x. Result: y′ = 5(3x² + 1)⁴ · 6x = 30x(3x² + 1)⁴. Check by testing a value: at x = 0, y′ = 0 — sensible, since the inner function has zero slope there.

Check: A pendulum's angle obeys θ(t) = θ₀ cos(ωt). Write dθ/dt using the chain rule — which factor carries the “how fast the phase changes” information?

In physics: the chain rule is why angular velocity ω sneaks into every rotating system: d/dt of any function of angle brings a factor of ω = dθ/dt. It is also the mathematical heart of “rate of change depends on state” — feedback.

C1.05 · Exponentials, logarithms & growth

ex is the unique function equal to its own derivative — growth proportional to current size. Its partner, ln(x), undoes it and turns multiplication into addition.

d/dx ex = ex  ·  d/dx ln(x) = 1/x  ·  d/dx ekx = k·ekx

Super-simple explanation

Interest you earn on your money behaves like ex: the more you have, the faster it grows, and that feedback loop is exactly what e encodes. Radioactive decay is the same story with a minus sign — shrinking in proportion to how much is left.

Worked example. A sample has half-life T. After time t, N(t) = N₀ · 2−t/T = N₀ · e−(ln 2/T)·t. Differentiate: N′(t) = −(ln 2/T) · N(t) — the decay rate is proportional to the amount present, exactly as observed. The constant k = ln 2/T ≈ 0.693/T is what you plug into every half-life problem.

Log rules that matter: ln(ab) = ln a + ln b, ln(aⁿ) = n ln a, and eln x = x. In astronomy, magnitudes are logarithms of fluxes — 5 magnitudes = factor 100 in brightness — so comfort with logs is comfort with the night sky's ruler.

Check: If a population doubles every 3 hours, write N(t) in the form N₀ekt and find k. What is the instantaneous growth rate when N = 1,000?

In physics: radioactive dating, RC circuits (capacitor charging), atmospheric pressure with altitude, the Boltzmann factor in thermodynamics, and the luminosity distance–redshift relation at small z are all exponentials or logarithms in disguise.

C1.06 · Applications: rates, optimisation, curves

Derivatives earn their keep when you use them:

  • Related rates. Two quantities are linked by an equation; differentiate both sides with respect to time, then substitute the known values. Water filling a conical tank: connect r and h by similar triangles first, then differentiate — never substitute numbers before differentiating.
  • Optimisation. Write the quantity to maximise as one function, use a constraint to reduce it to one variable, set f′ = 0, and check it is a maximum (f″ < 0, or test values either side). The universe loves minima too: light takes the path of least time, and nature often minimises energy.
  • Curve sketching. f′ tells you where the function rises and falls; f″ tells you which way it bends (concavity). Together they replace pages of plotting: find f′ = 0 for turning points, f″ = 0 for inflection points, then draw.

Super-simple explanation

Think of f′ as the “mood” of the function: positive means rising, negative means falling, zero means flat (a candidate for a peak or a valley). f″ is how quickly that mood changes — the function's second thoughts.

Worked example (optimisation). Of all rectangles with perimeter 40 cm, which has the largest area? Area A = xy, constraint 2x + 2y = 40 → y = 20 − x. So A(x) = x(20 − x) = 20x − x². A′(x) = 20 − 2x = 0 → x = 10, and A″ = −2 < 0, so it is a maximum. The answer: the square, 10 × 10 cm, area 100 cm².

Check: A rocket's height is h(t) = −4.9t² + 60t. At what time is it highest, and how high? Confirm with f″ rather than a picture.

In physics: maximum range at 45°, minimum energy orbits, equilibrium positions where potential energy is stationary (dV/dx = 0), and least-squares fitting of data all start with “set the derivative to zero.”

C1.07 · Preview of integration

Derivatives ask “how fast is it changing?” Integration asks the reverse: “given the rate, how much has accumulated?” The area under a rate curve is the total change — this is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the bridge you will cross fully in Calculus II.

∫ab f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a), where F′ = ftotal change = antiderivative at the end − antiderivative at the start

Super-simple explanation

If a speedometer reads constant 60 km/h for 2 hours, the trip is 60 × 2 = 120 km — area under the speed line. Integration is that same area calculation when the speed wiggles: slice the graph into thin strips, add them up, let the strips go to zero width.

Worked example. v(t) = 3t² m/s. An antiderivative is t³ (since d/dt t³ = 3t²). Distance from t = 0 to t = 2 is 2³ − 0³ = 8 m. Notice: calculus let us integrate a varying velocity without ever memorising a “formula for distance.”

Check: Which grows faster as t → ∞: t¹⁰ or et? (Differentiate their ratio — or remember that exponentials beat every power.)

In physics: integrate acceleration → velocity → position; integrate power → energy; integrate force over distance → work. Every accumulation in the formula sheet is an integral waiting to happen.
