The paper shelf — real research, direct PDF links

Ten papers the club has picked because they are famous, well-written, and genuinely readable by a student who knows Calculus I and some physics. Every link goes straight to the free PDF on arXiv — no paywalls, no login.

Note on the request: the landmark neutron-star merger is GW170817 (detected 17 August 2017 — often misremembered as “GW170717”). Both discovery papers are the first two entries below. Difficulty tags: start read first · core club canon · stretch bring a friend.

  • arXiv:1710.05832core
    GW170817: Observation of Gravitational Waves from a Binary Neutron Star Inspiral LIGO & Virgo Collaborations · Phys. Rev. Lett. 119, 161101 (2017) · Why read it: the discovery paper for the event that changed multi-messenger astronomy — two neutron stars merged 130 million light-years away, and we heard it with gravity. The abstract alone is a masterclass in precise claims: signal-to-noise 32.4, one false alarm per 80,000 years.
  • arXiv:1710.05834core
    Gravitational Waves and Gamma-rays from a Binary Neutron Star Merger: GW170817 and GRB 170817A LIGO, Virgo, Fermi-GBM & INTEGRAL · ApJL 848, L13 (2017) · Why read it: the companion paper — 1.7 seconds after the merger, a gamma-ray burst arrived. From that time delay the team constrained the speed of gravity to agree with the speed of light to within parts in 10¹⁵. One number, one deep test of relativity.
  • arXiv:1602.03837core
    Observation of Gravitational Waves from a Binary Black Hole Merger LIGO & Virgo · Phys. Rev. Lett. 116, 061102 (2016) · Why read it: GW150914 — the first gravitational wave ever detected (14 September 2015). Two black holes of 36 and 29 solar masses became one of 62; 3 solar masses vanished into pure spacetime ripples. The paper that opened gravitational-wave astronomy.
  • arXiv:1606.04855start
    GW151226: Observation of Gravitational Waves from a 22-Solar-Mass Binary Black Hole Coalescence LIGO & Virgo · Phys. Rev. Lett. 116, 241103 (2016) · Why read it: the second detection, and the cleanest “how a discovery paper is structured” example: detection → significance → masses → distance → what it constrains. Short, well-annotated, ideal first paper.
  • arXiv:1906.11238stretch
    First M87 Event Horizon Telescope Results. I. The Shadow of the Supermassive Black Hole Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration · ApJ 875, L1 (2019) · Why read it: how the first picture of a black hole was made — a planet-sized virtual telescope, four nights of data, and a ring of 42 micro-arcseconds. Study Figure 1 the way you'd study a map; the physics is in the asymmetry of the bright crescent.
  • arXiv:1605.07211start
    Temperate Earth-sized planets transiting a nearby ultracool dwarf star Gillon et al. · Nature 533, 221 (2016) · Why read it: the discovery of TRAPPIST-1 — three (later seven) Earth-sized planets 40 light-years away, two near the habitable zone. A friendly example of the transit method: the star dips, and the dip's depth and timing give the planet's size and orbit.
  • arXiv:2005.10828start
    A fast radio burst associated with a Galactic magnetar Bochenek et al. · Nature 587, 54 (2020) · Why read it: a millisecond radio scream from a magnetar inside our own galaxy — the “aha” moment connecting fast radio bursts to neutron stars. Short, vivid, and a great example of how chance coincidence is quantified (probability of 5 × 10⁻⁸ appears in its sibling paper).
  • arXiv:1807.06209stretch
    Planck 2018 results. VI. Cosmological parameters Planck Collaboration · A&A 641, A6 (2020) · Why read it: our best census of the universe — age 13.8 billion years, ordinary matter 5%, dark matter 27%, dark energy 68%. Don't read all 68 pages: title, abstract, the parameter table, and the discussion. That's a complete paper-reading session in one sitting.
  • arXiv:1903.07603core
    Large Magellanic Cloud Cepheid Standards Provide a 1% Foundation for the Hubble Constant… Riess et al. · ApJ 876, 85 (2019) · Why read it: one half of the famous Hubble tension — the local universe expands at 74.03 ± 1.42 km/s/Mpc, while the early universe says ~67. Two careful measurements, one uncomfortable disagreement. Read alongside the Planck paper above and you're doing real cosmology.
  • arXiv:1511.03190stretch
    Significant-loophole-free test of Bell's theorem with entangled photons Giustina et al. · Phys. Rev. Lett. 115, 250401 (2015) · Why read it: the experiment that closed the escape hatches for “hidden variables” — the statistical chance of local realism producing the data is below 4 × 10⁻³¹. Quantum mechanics, tested honestly. Read the first paragraph slowly; it defines local realism better than most textbooks.

How to read a STEM research paper (and keep the point)

Research papers are not novels — you are not meant to read page 1 to page N in order. This two-pass method, used by researchers worldwide, takes 40–60 minutes for a first paper and gets faster every time.

  • Before you open it: know your reason

    Write one sentence: “I'm reading this to learn ___.” Curiosity counts (“how did they photograph a black hole?”). Without a reason you'll drown in jargon; with one, everything irrelevant becomes skippable.

  • Pass 1 — the 15-minute skim (decide if it's worth deep time)

    Read the title, the abstract twice, then the section headings, every figure caption, and the conclusion. Do not read the methods yet. At the end, you must be able to say the paper's question and headline result in two sentences — if you can't, re-read the abstract and look up at most three unfamiliar terms (write them down).

  • Extract the main point with this template

    Fill it in from memory: “The authors studied [question] using [method/instrument], found [result with units or numbers], which matters because [why it changes something].” If any blank is empty, you haven't found the point yet — go back to the abstract and figures. This single sentence is the seed of every reflection you'll ever write.

  • Pass 2 — the deep read (30–60 minutes)

    Order: Introduction (what gap they claim to fill) → Figures (study each one before its text; figures are the paper's real argument) → Results (what was actually measured) → Discussion/Conclusion (what it means, what they admit is uncertain) → Methods last (skim; check only the part you'd need to reproduce). Mark three things in the margin: claim, evidence, assumption.

  • Separate evidence from interpretation — always

    “The ring is 42 ± 3 micro-arcseconds” is evidence. “Consistent with a Kerr black hole” is interpretation (a well-tested one, but still). Papers blur these deliberately-fast; your job is to un-blur them. Ask: what would I need to believe to accept this? What would falsify it?

  • Close the loop: explain it out loud, no notes

    Two minutes, to a friend or to an empty chair: what did they ask, how did they answer, what did they find, why care? If you stall, that's your gap — return to that section. (This is the club's paper-night format; see Club activities.)

  • Collect questions, not just facts

    End with two written questions: one you can answer now with the paper, one that needs another paper. The second kind is how you build a reading list that actually connects.

The 90-second version

Title + abstract ×2 → headings + figure captions → conclusion → (if worthy) intro → figures → results → discussion → methods skim → say the one-sentence takeaway from memory → write two questions. Never start at page 1.

Writing reflections across connected papers — in your own words

The harder skill: take two, three or four papers that talk to each other, and write a reflection or conclusion-style essay that explains them with your own understanding — no copy-paste, no ghost-writing by tools. This is how researchers write introductions and how you prove to yourself that you actually understood the fundamentals.

Step 1 — Build the conversation map

Before writing anything, make a small table (in your notebook, not in prose): for each paper — question · method · key finding (your words) · one number worth remembering. You now know who is saying what.

Step 2 — Find the relationships

Circle how they connect: Does paper B confirm A? Contradict it? Extend it? Fix a flaw? Apply A's method to a new object? The verbs (confirm, contradict, extend, refine, apply) are the skeleton of your essay — each verb becomes a paragraph.

Step 3 — Paraphrase the honest way (no AI, no sentence-lifting)

  • Read a passage, look away, write it fresh

    Cover the source. Write the idea as if telling a club member who hasn't seen the paper. Uncover and compare — fix only facts you got wrong, not wording back toward the original. If your sentence and theirs are twins, you didn't paraphrase; you copied. Start over with different structure (“whereas A measures X by…, B instead asks…”).

  • Own the vocabulary

    If you use a term (redshift, flux, divergence), you must be able to define it in one plain sentence without the paper open. Undefined jargon is the tell of borrowed understanding.

  • Cite precisely, then move on

    Facts and numbers get attributed (“Riess et al. measure 74.0 ± 1.4 km/s/Mpc”); your analysis does not — it's yours. Attribution is honesty, not weakness.

Step 4 — The essay structure that works every time

  • Opening: the shared question (2–3 sentences)

    What puzzle connects these papers? (“How fast does the universe expand — and why do two careful methods disagree?”) Name the tension in plain language; no citations needed yet.

  • Body A: paper by paper, in the conversation's order

    One paragraph each: what they asked, how, what they found — in your words — and what it contributes to the shared question. One number each, maximum.

  • Body B: the dialogue

    Where do they agree? Disagree? What assumption does each make that the other doesn't? This is the paragraph only you can write — it's synthesis, not summary. It's also where markers (and later, journal referees) look for understanding.

  • Reflection: why it's worth knowing, and what it opens

    Answer three questions directly: (1) Why does this matter? — what belief about the universe changes? (2) What doors does it open? — which new measurements, instruments or tests become possible? (3) What branches accelerate from here? — which field should a student watch next, and which of your open questions remains?

  • Conclusion: one paragraph that returns to the opening

    Restate the shared question with the new answer you've assembled, name the most important unresolved gap, and finish with a forward-looking sentence a reader could quote. Never introduce new evidence in the conclusion — it summarizes the journey, it doesn't add a new road.

Honesty rule (club policy)

Write the first draft with the sources closed and the tab blank — your memory and your map only. Then check against the papers and correct facts, not style. Tools may later check spelling or verify a citation; they may not write your analysis, because the analysis is the entire point: proof that you understood what the paper did, why it's worth knowing, and what it opens next. If you can't explain it without the page in front of you, you haven't finished reading — and that is a useful thing to discover before an exam, not after.

Suggested pairings for joint reading & reflection

  • The Hubble tension: Planck 2018 ↔ Riess et al. 2019 Early-universe answer vs local-universe answer, 67 vs 74. Reflection prompt: which assumptions could be wrong, and what measurement would break the tie?
  • Multi-messenger astronomy: GW170817 discovery ↔ GW170817 + GRB paper One event, two papers: gravitational waves alone, then waves + light together. Reflection prompt: what could the gamma-ray timing test that the waves alone could not?
  • Seeing the unseeable: GW150914 ↔ M87* EHT shadow Two different messengers (spacetime ripples vs 1.3 mm radio waves) imaging black holes. Reflection prompt: how does each paper convince you the object is real despite never “seeing” it directly?
  • Worlds around other stars: TRAPPIST-1 ↔ the FRB magnetar paper Nearby planetary systems vs nearby stellar explosions — “our neighbourhood” theme. Reflection prompt: why do both papers lean on distance measurements, and how do they get them?
